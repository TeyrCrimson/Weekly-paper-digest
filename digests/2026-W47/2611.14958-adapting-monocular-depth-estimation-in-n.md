# Adapting Monocular Depth Estimation in Novel Scenes through Disparity Refinement and Robot Ego-Motion

**Authors:** Dimitrios Chatziparaschis, Georgios Georgakis, Adnan I. Ansar, Shehryar Khattak, Konstantinos Karydis

**arXiv:** [2611.14958](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14958)

**Categories:** cs.RO, cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-11-11

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: Metric and temporally-consistent monocular depth

---

## TL;DR
EM-ViT turns a frozen monocular-depth foundation model's relative (affine-invariant) disparity output into real metric depth by learning two decoupled quantities online — a dense disparity correction and a single "effective baseline" scalar — using sparse LiDAR, photometric reconstruction, and ego-motion as self-supervision while the robot explores. On KITTI, a garage, a citrus farm, and a Mars rover (Perseverance Navcam) dataset, it adapts faster and more accurately than existing online/offline metric-tuning baselines, running on an embedded NVIDIA AGX Orin. The core trick is framing depth recovery as a "virtual stereo" problem (depth = focal length × baseline × disparity), which cleanly separates scene structure from scale.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Robots need dense, long-range depth to navigate safely (avoid rocks, plan paths), but the classic sensors have drawbacks: LiDAR is sparse, heavy, and degrades with dust/motion; stereo cameras have limited range and fail on low-texture or poorly-lit scenes (a real issue for Mars rovers). Monocular cameras are cheap and give dense images, but a single 2D image has no inherent scale — the same picture could depict a tiny toy car or a real one, depending on distance.

**Why existing MDE approaches fail here.** Deep models predicting absolute ("metric") depth directly only work well when trained and tested in the same domain/camera — they don't generalize to new scenes. To fix generalization, researchers (MiDaS, Depth Anything, etc.) trained models to predict "affine-invariant inverse depth" — i.e., depth that's correct in shape but off by an unknown scale (s) and shift (t): `disparity = s·depth + t`. This generalizes beautifully across scenes but is useless for robotics without first recovering s and t for the specific camera/scene. Prior fixes either (a) fine-tune a metric head per-domain (expensive, needs labeled data), (b) do test-time rescaling by solving for s,t at every single frame (ignores image content, must be redone constantly, as in prior work by Marsal et al.), or (c) use classical multi-view geometry (SfM/SLAM), which only recovers depth at matched features/keyframes, is sparse, and still can't get absolute metric scale from monocular video alone without an external cue.

**The paper's mechanism — "virtual stereo" decomposition.** The key idea borrows from stereo camera geometry: depth = focal_length × baseline / disparity. A stereo camera has a known physical baseline (distance between the two lenses) and directly recovers metric depth from disparity. EM-ViT pretends the single camera has an imaginary ("virtual") baseline and learns to predict it, plus a refined dense disparity map, splitting the problem into:
1. **Dense disparity refinement (`dRefNet`)**: a small Vision Transformer takes the foundation model's raw relative disparity map plus the camera image and outputs a corrected disparity map. This fixes local structural errors in the backbone's output (not just a global shift).
2. **Effective baseline scalar (`B̂`)**: the same network also predicts one single number per image — like a camera "pretending" to be a stereo rig with some baseline — which supplies the missing overall scale. Because this one number aggregates information from the whole image (object sizes, scene layout), it's more robust than dense per-pixel scale guessing.
Multiplying refined disparity × baseline × known focal length gives metric depth. Because scale lives entirely in the scalar B̂ and structure lives entirely in the dense map, errors in one don't contaminate the other ("scale-structure disentanglement") — this is the paper's central innovation.

**How it's trained, online, with scarce labels.** Three self-supervision signals combine: (a) sparse LiDAR points projected onto the image give ground-truth depth at a few pixels (masked L1 loss); (b) a photometric reconstruction loss warps previous frames into the current frame using predicted depth and robot pose, penalizing pixel mismatches (classic self-supervised depth trick, extended with an `odomNet` that corrects noisy odometry/pose estimates); (c) a "gradient matching loss" compares the *normalized gradients* (edges) of the refined disparity to the original backbone disparity, so fine structural detail (e.g., object boundaries) from the foundation model isn't destroyed during adaptation — important because LiDAR-only supervision is sparse and can over-smooth outputs. The whole thing is lightweight (912K parameters total) so it can be retrained in short bursts (~10s of data) while the robot moves, continuously adapting to new terrain (Fig. 4's rolling collect→train pipeline).

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: the explicit disentanglement of scale (single scalar baseline) from structure (dense disparity refinement), framed through the stereo camera equation. This differs from prior test-time rescaling (which solves for scale+shift per frame via least-squares, ignoring image content) and from direct metric-head fine-tuning (which conflates scale and structure in one dense prediction).
- **Claimed vs. supported**: The paper claims faster *and* more accurate adaptation than existing foundation-model fine-tuning/rescaling baselines. The evidence (Table I-VI) does support consistent accuracy gains and ~16-40% less training time across garage, citrus farm, KITTI, and Mars Navcam datasets — a reasonably broad evaluation for a robotics paper. The ablations (Table II, III, V) substantively support the design choices: the model degrades gracefully with very sparse LiDAR (even single-beam "rangefinder" case), lower sampling rates, and the dense-refinement approach beats a simple least-squares (scale/shift) solver by a clear margin, directly supporting the "disentanglement matters" claim.
- **Useful secondary finding**: smaller input resolution (220px vs. the backbone's native 518px) still yields usable, cross-domain-generalizable disparity, enabling real-time onboard operation on an embedded GPU (Orin) — relevant for SWaP-constrained robots.
- **Practical relevance**: demonstrated on a real extraterrestrial dataset (Mars Perseverance Navcam) where stereo matching struggles with shadows/low texture — a compelling, somewhat unusual application domain for this kind of method.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Dynamic scenes / non-Lambertian surfaces** (stated): the photometric loss assumes static scenes with Lambertian reflectance; moving objects or occlusions corrupt training and real-time performance. Authors suggest LiDAR as a backstop but don't solve this.
- **Sampling-rate tuning is manual and scenario-dependent** (stated): authors admit the right sampling rate depends on sensor specs, robot speed, and compute, and that under-sampling risks training on spatially clustered (non-diverse) viewpoints, degrading accuracy; they only recommend (not implement) a coverage-based sampling fix.
- **Pseudo-ground-truth on Mars data** (stated implicitly in Section IV-D): since no real ground truth exists for the Navcam dataset, the authors use another learned stereo model (FoundationStereo) as "pseudo ground truth" — this makes the Mars evaluation a comparison against another model's predictions, not verified real-world metric accuracy.
- **No LiDAR = untested regime** (inferred): future work states they want to "minimize sensory dependencies... such as LiDAR," implying the current method still depends on at least some LiDAR (or stereo-derived pseudo-truth) during training in all reported experiments; a fully camera/IMU-only version is not demonstrated.
- **Normalization choice dismissed without full data** (stated): the median-normalization variant (Eq. 7-8) is mentioned as giving "only marginal quantitative improvements" and omitted from experiments, but no actual numbers are shown — this claim isn't backed by a visible ablation table.
- **Baseline comparisons use re-implemented/downsized versions** (inferred): FusionDepth is explicitly described as "downsized... for onboard efficiency" and "adjusted" by the authors, and the online DAV2 ViT-S baseline only fine-tunes a small DPT head — these are modified versions of other papers' methods rather than the original full systems, which could tilt comparisons in EM-ViT's favor; the paper doesn't discuss whether these modifications were validated against the original papers' reported numbers.
- **KITTI exception acknowledged** (stated): on KITTI's 0-10m range, the Marsal et al. baseline actually outperforms EM-ViT's online DAV2 competitor, attributed to KITTI's denser Velodyne HDL-64E LiDAR — the authors admit their method's advantage shrinks or reverses when very dense LiDAR is already available, somewhat undercutting the "always better" framing.
- **Limited dataset diversity / no truly dynamic outdoor scenes** (stated in Future Work): authors explicitly flag the need for evaluation on "more diverse, outdoor, and dynamic scenes" (e.g., FusionPortableV2, GrandTour) as future work, meaning current results are confined to relatively controlled or static-ish environments (garage, orchard rows, KITTI driving, Mars rover traverse).
- **Single backbone tested** (inferred): all experiments use one specific foundation model (Depth Anything V2 ViT-S); generalization of the virtual-stereo decomposition to other MDE backbones (larger ViT-L, other architectures like UniDepth/Metric3D) is not addressed in the paper.
- **Truncated text**: part of the paper (end of Section IV-D and part of the Conclusion) was cut in the provided text, so any additional caveats or results discussed there are not addressed in this digest.
