# From Depth to Safe Depth: Uncertainty-Aware Monocular Perception for Mobile Robot Navigation

**Authors:** Saeid Firouzi Daghigh, Saeed Ayat

**arXiv:** [2611.13769](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13769)

**Categories:** cs.RO, cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-11-10

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: Metric and temporally-consistent monocular depth

---

## TL;DR
Off-the-shelf monocular depth foundation models (Depth Anything V2) systematically overestimate obstacle distance, causing a standard Nav2 navigation stack to collide in 63% of simulated episodes. The authors add a tiny (0.58M-parameter) frozen-backbone head that predicts a per-pixel log-depth correction and uncertainty, converts this into a conformally-calibrated "safe depth" lower bound, and feed that into the unmodified navigation stack, cutting collisions to 3% and raising success from 37% to 70%. They show this beats a naive constant safety margin calibrated to the same conservatism level, which instead just freezes the robot (30% success).

## First-principles breakdown

**The underlying problem.** Mobile robots need to know how far away obstacles are to avoid hitting them. LiDAR/RGB-D sensors give this directly but cost money, weight, and power. A single RGB camera plus a learned "monocular depth" model is cheaper, so there's a temptation to swap it in as a drop-in depth sensor. Monocular depth foundation models (e.g., Depth Anything V2, Metric3D v2) are trained on huge mixed datasets and generalize well on benchmark accuracy metrics. But "accurate on average over a benchmark" and "safe to drive on" are different things: what matters for a navigating robot is whether, at pixels representing nearby obstacles, the model's errors go in the dangerous direction (looks farther than it is, so the robot doesn't slow down).

**Why naive use fails.** The paper runs an unmodified Nav2 stack (the standard ROS2 navigation framework: builds a costmap from sensor points, plans a path, drives with a local controller) but swaps the depth sensor for Depth Anything V2's metric output. Result: 63% collision rate over 120 episodes. Diagnosis: the model over-estimates distances to nearby obstacles (59.8% of near pixels overestimated by >0.2m), so the costmap thinks space is clear when it isn't. Critically, the costmap treats every depth pixel as equally trustworthy — there's no signal distinguishing "the model is confident here" from "the model is guessing" (e.g., on glass, blank texture-less walls, dark regions).

**Why a blanket fix (just assume depth is smaller) doesn't work either.** One cheap fix: shrink all predicted depths by some constant factor so the robot is "more cautious everywhere." The paper tests this (the "margin" baseline: depth × e^-c, with c tuned so collision risk matches the proposed method). It only reaches 30% success — worse than doing nothing, because shrinking depth uniformly also shrinks the perceived width of corridors and gaps between furniture, so the robot thinks there's no way through and gets stuck/times out 65% of the time. Caution has to be spent selectively, not uniformly.

**The proposed mechanism, step by step:**
1. *Freeze the depth backbone.* Depth Anything V2 is kept exactly as pretrained — no fine-tuning of the big model, avoiding catastrophic forgetting of its general depth knowledge and keeping training cheap.
2. *Add a small head.* A 0.58M-parameter U-Net-like head takes as input: the 32-channel decoder features already computed inside the depth model, the log of the predicted depth, and the RGB image (36 channels total, half resolution). It has two downsampling stages with dilated convolutions (to see broad context on large uniform surfaces like blank walls), then upsampling with skip connections, ending in a 1×1 conv that outputs two numbers per pixel: a correction µ (how wrong, systematically, is this pixel's depth, in log space) and log-uncertainty s (how noisy/unreliable is this pixel's depth likely to be).
3. *Why log-space and why a correction + uncertainty.* Monocular depth errors tend to scale multiplicatively with distance, so working in log-depth makes the correction additive and easier to learn. The corrected depth is just log(d̂) = log(d_DA) + µ. Separately, σ = e^s is a learned per-pixel uncertainty, estimated with no explicit uncertainty labels — it falls out of how the loss is constructed (see below).
4. *Training objective — Gaussian negative log-likelihood (NLL).* Given ground truth depth and residual error r = log(d_gt) − log(d̂), the model is trained to minimize ½r²e^{-2s} + s. This is a standard trick in probabilistic regression: the network predicts both a mean estimate and a per-sample uncertainty, and the loss rewards accurate uncertainty — if σ is set equal to the typical size of the error, the loss is minimized (so σ naturally learns to track local error magnitude even without any ground-truth uncertainty labels). µ absorbs the systematic (fixable) part of the error; σ absorbs the remaining (irreducible-looking) noise.
5. *The β-NLL trick.* Plain Gaussian NLL has a known failure mode (Seitzer et al. 2022): gradients on µ get divided by σ², so on hard pixels the network can "cheat" by inflating σ instead of actually correcting the bias. The paper multiplies the loss by a stop-gradient-detached σ^(2β) term (β=0.5) to rebalance this, so the head still tries to fix what it can fix rather than just declaring everything uncertain. Pixels are also weighted by 1/distance so that errors near the robot (which matter most for collisions) are prioritized during training.
6. *From uncertainty to a safety bound.* Rather than feeding the corrected mean depth to the navigation stack, they feed a one-sided lower confidence bound: d_safe = d_DA · e^{µ − kσ}. This says "assume the obstacle might be at least this much closer than our best guess, scaled by how unsure we are." The constant k controls how conservative this bound is.
7. *Choosing k via conformal calibration.* Rather than assuming the Gaussian model is perfectly calibrated (which would suggest a fixed k=2 for ~95% coverage) they use split-conformal prediction: on a held-out validation world, compute the normalized residual z = (log d̂ − log d_gt)/σ for near pixels, and set k to the empirical 97.7th percentile of z. This is a distribution-free way to guarantee (empirically, on that validation world) that the true depth is ≥ the bound for 97.7% of near pixels, without trusting the Gaussian assumption itself. This gave k=3.0.
8. *The "phantom floor" problem and fix.* Because the camera looks downward at the floor, shrinking depth along a ray doesn't just move points closer to the camera along the viewing direction — geometrically it also lifts floor points upward (toward "obstacle height"). A camera at 0.55m height with even a 14% depth underestimate can make flat floor register as an obstacle in the costmap, which would make the whole floor "lethal." They fix this with a "ground test": a pixel is classified as floor if its estimated depth is close enough to where the floor plane would be at that pixel (within a threshold τ); when classified as floor, its depth is overwritten to not register as an obstacle. For the safe-depth variant, τ is itself widened to kσ — i.e., a pixel also counts as floor if the floor lies within its uncertainty band. This is a second place where uncertainty directly controls robot behavior (not just how far obstacles seem, but whether a pixel counts as an obstacle at all).
9. *Everything downstream is untouched.* The corrected, bounded depth is converted to a point cloud and fed into the stock Nav2 costmap/planner/controller — no changes to planning or control logic, isolating the contribution of the perception fix.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new combination, not a new algorithm per component.** Gaussian NLL uncertainty heads, β-NLL, BayesCap-style frozen-backbone post-hoc heads, and conformal calibration are all pre-existing techniques (properly attributed to prior work). The contribution is the specific plug-in system that composes them to solve a navigation-specific problem, plus the floor/ray geometry insight.
- **The core demonstrated finding: *where* caution is spent matters more than *how much*.** The margin baseline and the "safe" variant are calibrated to the identical pixel-level coverage guarantee (97.7% near-pixel coverage), yet differ by 40 percentage points in task success (30% vs 70%). This is good evidence (not just a claim) that uniform conservatism is a poor strategy and spatially-varying, learned uncertainty is doing real work — this is probably the paper's most defensible and interesting empirical result, backed by a well-designed paired/matched-coverage control.
- **Uncertainty helps even beyond bias correction.** µ-only correction gets to 60% success/15% collisions; adding σ (the full "safe" method) gets to 70%/3%. The authors show this gap is statistically significant via McNemar test (20 vs 8 discordant episodes, p=0.036) — i.e., the uncertainty term is pulling weight on its own, not just redundant with the bias fix.
- **The floor-lifting geometric interaction is a non-obvious, somewhat elegant observations**: that depth shrinkage along camera rays interacts badly with a downward-pitched camera's floor, and that the same uncertainty signal can double as "is this pixel floor" classifier. This generalizes the idea that uncertainty isn't just for the depth value, it's for a downstream semantic decision (is-this-an-obstacle) too.
- **Evidence actually supports:** the specific numbers (AbsRel 0.262→0.134, near overestimation 59.8%→12.0%, collision 63%→3%, success 37%→70%) are measured in one simulated benchmark family with one robot/camera/depth-model config — these are solid relative comparisons *within this test setup*, not validated as general real-world performance numbers.
- **Not supported yet:** any claim about real-robot transfer, different depth backbones, different camera placements, or generalization beyond the procedurally generated world family — the paper does not claim these, and explicitly flags them as future work.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Simulation-only evaluation** (stated): "All experiments are in simulation with one robot, one camera configuration and one depth model (ViT-S)." No real-robot or real-camera test is reported.
- **Ground-truth odometry used throughout** (stated): localization error is deliberately excluded ("Ground-truth odometry isolates perception but hides localisation error"), so real-world performance could be worse once odometry noise interacts with the perception pipeline.
- **Flat-floor assumption in the ground test** (stated): "The ground test assumes a flat floor" — ramps, thresholds, or uneven terrain are not handled and could break the floor-vs-obstacle logic.
- **Train/test distribution closeness** (stated): the correction head is trained on data from "the same generator family as the test worlds, although on different layouts, so transfer to real images needs to be verified" — i.e., this is in-distribution-ish synthetic data, and the model's strong gains may not transfer to genuinely novel real scenes or a different simulator's rendering style.
- **Single depth backbone, single architecture scale** (inferred): only Depth Anything V2 ViT-S (the smallest variant) is tested; no comparison with Metric3D v2 or larger Depth Anything variants, so it's unclear whether the 63% baseline collision rate and the fix's gains are specific to this particular (possibly weaker) backbone choice.
- **Narrow-passage conservatism not fully solved** (stated): "with large k, narrow passages shrink, and part of the remaining 27% of non-collision failures are such conservative stops" — the method still has a meaningful failure mode (freezing/failing in tight spaces), just less severe than the margin baseline.
- **Pixel-level calibration target is not what's actually optimized for** (stated/discussion): the authors note the real goal is navigation success, but calibration is done on pixel-level coverage as a proxy; they explicitly suggest "a calibration target defined on navigation outcomes" as unaddressed future work — meaning the current k selection is a reasonable but acknowledged proxy, not the ideal objective.
- **Small/narrow test slices for hardest case** (inferred): the "Glass" hard-case subgroup has only 16 episodes, and the paper itself flags "their intervals are wide" — the headline claim that gains "hold on glass" rests on a small sample with limited statistical power.
- **Ground-truth baseline is itself inflated in simulation** (stated): "the simulated depth camera sees glass as solid, which makes the ground-truth baseline optimistic compared with real depth sensors" — meaning even the reference upper-bound (93% success) is not representative of what a real sensor would achieve on glass, slightly undermining the "close to ground truth" framing.
- **Compute/speed constraint** (stated): "Monocular inference at about 1 Hz on a 4 GB GPU also limits the speed at which the robot can safely drive" — the approach has not been shown to scale to faster robot speeds or more constrained embedded hardware.
- **No ablation on head architecture choices** (inferred): dilation rates, number of U-Net stages, choice of β=0.5, choice of weighting function (1/distance) — none of these are ablated; it's unclear how sensitive results are to these specific hyperparameter choices.
- **Single-seed/no variance reporting on training** (inferred): training is described as a single run (5 epochs, one GPU) with no repeated-seed variance reported for the learned head itself, only Wilson CIs on navigation outcomes — so it's unclear how reproducible the learned correction/uncertainty quality is across training runs.
- **Comparisons are against a self-constructed baseline, not an independent SOTA safety method** (inferred): the main "competitor" (constant margin) is authored by the same team as a controlled ablation, not a reproduction of a previously published competing safety layer (e.g., MonoMPC or CARE, both cited as related work but not run as baselines here) — so the paper doesn't position its gains against the closest prior navigation-safety systems in its own benchmark.
