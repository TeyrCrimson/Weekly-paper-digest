# A 10K drone image dataset with component-level annotations for rooftop photovoltaic inspection

**Authors:** Penglei Zhang, Wan Zhang, Ruizhe Deng, Bo Chen, Wenhao Duan, Jinhe Zhang, Yanzhen Zhang, Qi Chen, Zhiling Guo, Haoran Zhang, Yuntian Chen

**arXiv:** [2611.13892](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13892)

**Categories:** cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-11-10

**Relevance:** 8/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
PVIS-10K is a new open dataset of 10,065 multi-angle drone images from 22 rooftop solar installations in China, with 3.5M+ polygon annotations delineating 63,632 individual PV modules (not just array boundaries). The authors benchmark four supervised instance-segmentation models and zero-shot SAM 3 on station-disjoint train/test splits, finding supervised models (best: HTC) reach 58–90% Mask AP depending on city, while zero-shot SAM 3 lags far behind (2–33% AP) despite maintaining reasonable pixel-level coverage. The contribution is the dataset and its annotation pipeline, not a new model.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Rooftop solar inspection increasingly relies on drone photos, but existing public datasets (Bradbury, BDAPPV, Queens, PV01, Emmen) only label where PV arrays/coverage regions are — a single blob per installation, derived from overhead/orthographic imagery. That's enough to say "there's solar here" but not to count or track individual modules, which matters for maintenance (e.g., "module #42 is faulted"). Getting component-level labels is hard for several concrete reasons: modules look different depending on camera angle and installation height (perspective changes apparent size/shape), they're packed densely with repetitive grid-like edges that are easy to confuse with module boundaries, they're often partially blocked by shadows, structures, or other panels, and reflections/lighting can hide or create false edges. A model (or human) has to decide where one physical module ends and the next begins under all these distortions.

**Why prior data falls short.** Because existing datasets label coverage regions, not instances, and are collected from directly-overhead ("orthographic") viewpoints, they can't test a system's ability to separate adjacent modules under oblique, varied viewing angles — the realistic case for drone inspection flights.

**The annotation mechanism — the real technical contribution.** Rather than manually drawing module outlines separately on every single photo (where the same physical module might appear in dozens of overlapping images), the authors annotate once in 3D and then project that single annotation into every 2D photo where it's visible:
1. Fly a drone in oblique photogrammetric patterns over each rooftop (80%/70% image overlap) and use structure-from-motion (standard technique: triangulate matching points across many overlapping photos to reconstruct 3D camera positions and a 3D surface model — like how photogrammetry software builds a 3D scan from photos).
2. On the reconstructed 3D model, a human annotator draws each module's outline just once in 3D space, each getting a unique ID.
3. For every 2D photo, use the known camera position/orientation/lens parameters to mathematically project each 3D module corner back onto the image plane (the "collinearity equation," a standard formula: a 3D point, the camera center, and its 2D image point must lie on a straight line, adjusted by the camera's rotation matrix and internal lens parameters). This yields automatically generated 2D polygon outlines in every photo without re-drawing.
4. Humans then review and fix each projected 2D annotation image-by-image (displaced outlines, missed modules, wrongly merged/split instances), since projection alone isn't perfect (e.g., occlusion isn't automatically known — occluded modules get their full projected shape kept if partially visible, removed if fully hidden).
5. Clean up and convert to COCO format (the standard object-detection/segmentation annotation schema used by most CV tooling), with automated validity checks (polygon geometry, size thresholds, duplicate IDs).

This 3D-annotate-then-reproject approach is the key efficiency trick: it turns what would be "draw N objects × M photos" work into "draw N objects once + correct projections," which is how they could label 3.5 million instances.

**Station-disjoint splits.** Rather than randomly splitting images into train/test (which would leak information since nearby images of the same station are highly correlated), they keep entire rooftop stations together — 12 Ningbo stations for training, 6 different Ningbo stations plus all of Shaoxing and Shenzhen stations held out for testing. This tests generalization to genuinely unseen buildings/cities, a much harder and more realistic bar than typical random-split benchmarks.

**Benchmarking.** They train four standard instance segmentation architectures (Mask R-CNN, HTC, SOLOv2, Mask2Former — all off-the-shelf, not novel) on 512×512 crops, then stitch predictions back onto full images with overlap-handling rules (non-max suppression, mask-containment filtering). They also run SAM 3 (Meta's latest "segment anything" model) in zero-shot mode, prompted with the text "individual solar panel," to see how well a general-purpose, non-finetuned model performs without ever training on this data. Evaluation uses standard COCO-style mask Average Precision (requires correctly separating instances) plus a looser foreground IoU/F1 (just measures overall coverage overlap, ignoring whether instances were split/merged correctly).

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: This is, by the authors' comparison table, the first open dataset with true component-level (individual module) instance segmentation labels from oblique multi-angle drone imagery, at a scale (3.5M annotations, 63K physical components) far beyond prior array-level datasets. The 3D-annotate/2D-reproject pipeline is a legitimate, verifiable methodological contribution for reducing multi-view relabeling cost — this is well supported by the described workflow and quality-control process (claimed and evidenced).
- **Supported finding**: instance-matching accuracy (Mask AP) and pixel-coverage accuracy (IoU/F1) diverge substantially — SAM 3 achieves high foreground IoU (86–90%) while scoring very low Mask AP (2–33%), demonstrating concretely that a model can "cover" the right pixels while merging/splitting individual modules incorrectly (shown qualitatively in Fig. 4). This is a genuinely useful empirical point for anyone evaluating segmentation systems in this domain — coverage metrics alone can mask poor instance-level performance.
- **Claimed vs. evidenced generalization**: the paper frames cross-city (Shaoxing, Shenzhen) results as testing generalization, but the finding that cross-city Shenzhen AP (90%) exceeded within-city Ningbo test AP (73%) actually undercuts any strong claim that cross-city is "harder" — generalization difficulty seems to be driven more by station-specific factors (density, layout) than city identity itself, something the paper notes but doesn't deeply explain.
- The claim that zero-shot SAM 3 "benchmarks" foundation-model readiness for this task is supported only weakly — one fixed text prompt, selected from only 10 training images, is a thin basis for concluding general-purpose segmentation models are far behind supervised ones (plausible, but not a rigorous prompt-engineering comparison).

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **No ground control points, RTK-only geolocation** — stated. Camera positions rely on onboard RTK rather than surveyed ground control points, which can affect 3D reconstruction accuracy; no quantified positional error is given.
- **Annotations are per-image, not persistent cross-view identities** — stated explicitly ("Annotations identify per-image component observations, not persistent physical identities across views"), meaning the dataset cannot directly support multi-view tracking/counting consistency without extra work.
- **Geographic scope limited to China (Ningbo, Shaoxing, Shenzhen), single drone/camera model** — inferred. All data from one UAV platform (DJI Mavic 3E) and one country; generalization to other panel types, climates, roof architectures, or camera systems is untested.
- **SAM 3 prompt selection on only 10 images, single fixed text prompt** — stated as methodology, but this is a thin/likely best-case search that may not reflect SAM 3's true ceiling performance (inferred weakness: the zero-shot baseline could be underestimated or just reflect one arbitrary prompt choice, not a thorough prompting study).
- **No hyperparameter/architecture tuning beyond epoch count** — stated: "Preliminary station-disjoint validation with Mask R-CNN... was applied to all four supervised models" — i.e., only Mask R-CNN's epoch schedule was tuned and then reused as-is for HTC, SOLOv2, and Mask2Former, which may disadvantage models with different optimal training regimes (inferred: this could bias the model comparison, especially since Mask2Former uses a very different optimizer/learning rate regime per Table 5).
- **No released trained checkpoints** — stated ("The trained checkpoints of the four supervised baselines are not distributed"), limiting independent reproducibility of the exact reported numbers without retraining.
- **Large station-level AP variance not fully explained** — stated as an observation (e.g., HTC ranges from 38% to 92% AP across stations) but the paper does not analyze which specific station attributes (density, occlusion, height) drive this variance beyond qualitative examples; inferred that benchmark numbers aggregated at city level may obscure large practical performance swings.
- **No comparison to other recent zero-shot/foundation segmentation models besides SAM 3** — inferred; only one zero-shot baseline is tested, so claims about "foundation models underperform" rest on a single model/prompt combination.
- **Fully occluded components are excluded from ground truth** — stated; this is a reasonable but non-trivial labeling decision (removing rather than estimating hidden modules) that affects how "complete" counts/boundaries actually are.
