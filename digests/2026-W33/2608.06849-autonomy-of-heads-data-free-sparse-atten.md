# Autonomy-of-Heads: Data-Free Sparse Attention from Frozen Query-Key Geometry

**Authors:** Yehan Yang, Junyuan Shang, Yang Li, Guanqun Zhao, Shuohuan Wang, Dianhai Yu

**arXiv:** [2608.06849](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06849)

**Categories:** cs.CL, cs.AI

**Submitted:** 2026-08-07

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
AoH identifies "retrieval" vs. "streaming" attention heads purely from a model's frozen query/key weight matrices — no calibration data, runtime attention traces, or training required — by computing the effective rank of a small per-head kernel matrix. Using this weight-only signal to route retrieval heads to full attention and streaming heads to sink+recent-window attention, AoH retains 96.5% of full-attention LongBench performance at 50% sparsity while cutting prefill latency up to 41.4%, decode latency up to 66%, and KV-cache memory up to 50% (more at higher sparsity/longer context).

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Transformer self-attention costs grow quadratically with sequence length, and the KV-cache (the stored keys/values for every past token) grows linearly, so both compute and memory balloon at long context lengths (e.g., 128K–256K tokens). A well-known mitigation is to notice that not all attention heads behave the same way: some heads ("retrieval heads") need to look at distant, content-specific tokens anywhere in the context, while others ("streaming heads") mostly just attend to a few fixed "sink" tokens (usually the first few) and a small window of recent tokens. If you can correctly identify which heads are which, you can give streaming heads a cheap bounded cache (sink + recent window) and only pay full quadratic/linear cost for the retrieval heads — this is the basis of prior work like StreamingLLM, H2O, SnapKV, Quest, and DuoAttention.

**Why prior approaches are costly to deploy.** Existing head/token-selection methods decide "what to keep" using *runtime signals*: actual attention scores observed while processing a prompt, calibration prompts run in advance, observation windows, or a small gate module that must be trained. This means the head classification is input-dependent (must be recomputed or re-observed per prompt/deployment) and/or requires extra training or calibration data — adding latency, engineering complexity, and fragility.

**The core idea: heads "know what they know" from their frozen weights alone.** During attention, the score between a context token and a query token for head *h* is `scores = X_ctx · W_K^T · W_Q · x_i`. The two projection matrices `W_K` and `W_Q` are head-specific and don't depend on the input; the authors bundle them into a single "kernel attention matrix" `M_h = W_K^T · W_Q` (size `d_model × d_model`). This matrix encodes, independent of any actual input, which directions in the model's embedding space attend to which — a kind of built-in blueprint of the head's "matching style."

They then look at the **singular value spectrum** of `M_h` (via SVD). If a handful of singular values dominate (a "concentrated spectrum"), the head only has a few strong ways of matching queries to keys — this correlates empirically with retrieval behavior (attends to specific relevant tokens anywhere in the context). If the singular values are spread evenly ("diffuse spectrum"), there's no strong preferred matching direction, correlating with streaming behavior (mostly attends locally/sink tokens). This is quantified with **effective rank**: normalize singular values into a probability-like distribution, compute their Shannon entropy, and exponentiate — giving a number between 1 and the true rank that summarizes how "concentrated" vs. "spread out" the spectrum is (low = concentrated = retrieval; high = diffuse = streaming).

They validate this isn't just a plausible story: (1) per-head attention distance (how far back tokens attend) is stable across context lengths and negatively correlated with effective rank; (2) forcibly converting low-effective-rank heads to streaming attention collapses passkey-retrieval accuracy, while converting high-effective-rank heads barely hurts it — showing effective rank actually tracks functional importance, not just a correlation with cosmetic behavior.

**Making it cheap to compute.** Naively, `M_h` is a `d_model × d_model` matrix (huge — e.g., 3584×3584 for Qwen2.5-7B), and SVD on it would be expensive. The authors use the fact that `M_h`'s rank is bounded by `d_head` (the much smaller per-head dimension, e.g. 128) because it's a product of two low-rank factors. Via Sylvester's determinant theorem, the nonzero eigenvalues of `M_h^T M_h` equal those of a much smaller `d_head × d_head` proxy matrix `C_h = (W_Q W_Q^T)(W_K W_K^T)`. So they compute effective rank from this small matrix instead — a ~1000x reduction in FLOPs (4.6×10⁷ vs 5.9×10¹⁰ operations in their example).

**Turning classification into a deployment policy.** Once every head (or, for GQA models where multiple query heads share one KV head, every KV group, aggregated by mean effective rank) is labeled retrieval or streaming, weights are physically reordered so retrieval and streaming heads sit in contiguous blocks — enabling simple slicing instead of gather/scatter. At inference: retrieval heads keep the full KV cache and do standard (chunked-prefill-compatible, FlashAttention-2-compatible) attention; streaming heads keep only a small fixed sink+recent-window cache. Outputs from both head types are concatenated per layer as usual. Because the classification happens once, offline, from frozen weights, this can be applied starting at prefill (not just after "observing" the input), unlike input-dependent methods.

An appendix extension, H2Share, adds cross-layer index sharing (only integer token positions are shared across a block of adjacent layers, not KV vectors themselves) but this is a secondary contribution beyond the core AoH method.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: A data-free, training-free, input-independent way to classify attention heads as retrieval vs. streaming, derived purely from static weight geometry (effective rank of `W_K^T W_Q`). Prior head-wise methods (DuoAttention, RazorAttention, LycheeDecode) all rely on trained gates, calibration, or observed attention — AoH removes this dependency entirely, which simplifies deployment (compute once, use for any prompt/context length).
- **The efficient reformulation** (reducing an intractable `d_model × d_model` SVD to a `d_head × d_head` eigenproblem via Sylvester's theorem) is a genuinely useful engineering contribution that makes the method practical to run at all.
- **Evidence supports the core claim reasonably well**: ablations against random and reversed head-ordering (using the *same* sparsity budget) show large gaps (e.g., 47.55 AoH vs. 30.89 random vs. 23.31 reversed on Llama3.1-8B), which is a fairly convincing sign that effective rank captures real structure rather than an arbitrary partition. Comparisons against other spectral metrics (Frobenius norm, spectral norm, stable rank) also favor effective rank specifically, not just "any spectral score."
- **Claimed vs. supported**: The claim that AoH "retains 96.5% of full-attention performance on average" and beats input-dependent baselines (SnapKV, H2O, Quest, DuoAttention) at comparable KV budgets is supported by the LongBench tables across 3 models (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-8B, Llama3.1-8B) plus one MoE model. The efficiency claims (up to 3.24x prefill / 9.14x decode speedup, up to ~4x memory reduction) are demonstrated but only at the extremes of context length (256K) and sparsity (75%) — the paper's headline 41.4%/66.0% latency reduction figures correspond to more modest settings within their tables, so readers should check which sparsity/context cell a given percentage refers to.
- The RoPE-aware variant (AoH-RoPE) shows the method's head ranking is robust to positional encoding effects (Spearman correlation >0.98 with vanilla AoH even at 128K relative distance), suggesting the core geometric signal is not an artifact of ignoring RoPE.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Fixed, manually-chosen sparsity budget** — stated. The paper picks a constant retrieval/streaming split (e.g., 50%) manually and uniformly across layers; no automatic or per-layer-adaptive budget selection is implemented, which the authors flag as future work.
- **Limited architecture coverage** — stated. Evaluation is restricted to decoder-only LLMs with standard/GQA attention; encoder-decoder models, multimodal LLMs, and heavily modified attention mechanisms are explicitly not tested.
- **Small model set** — inferred. Core results center on three ~7-8B models (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-8B, Llama3.1-8B-Instruct) plus one MoE model (Qwen3-30B-A3B) in an appendix; no larger-scale (e.g., 70B+) or substantially different architectures (e.g., non-RoPE, no-GQA) are tested, so generalization to larger/other model families is unverified.
- **Single benchmark suite for main claims** — inferred. Almost all headline accuracy numbers come from LongBench (21 tasks); passkey retrieval and MMLU appear only as secondary/ablation checks. Broader generalization (e.g., reasoning-heavy or agentic long-context tasks mentioned as motivation in the intro) is not directly evaluated.
- **Latency/efficiency benchmarked under a "deliberately simple" backend** — stated. The authors note decode latencies use vanilla eager PyTorch attention (not optimized fused kernels) specifically "to isolate the effect of head-level sparsity from low-level kernel engineering." This means reported speedups may not transfer directly to production serving stacks using highly optimized attention kernels, and real-world gains could differ (higher or lower).
- **Single-sequence, batch-size-1 efficiency measurements** — inferred. All latency/memory efficiency tables are described as batch size = 1; no batched-serving throughput numbers are given, despite the paper claiming compatibility with "batched serving" as a benefit.
- **Streaming-cache cost not fully counted in "sparsity" definition** — inferred/stated. The paper defines sparsity via head counts and explicitly approximates "KV budget ≈ 1 − sparsity," treating the streaming heads' small sink+recent cache as negligible; this is a reasonable approximation at very long contexts but slightly overstates savings at shorter contexts (e.g., 4-8K) where the fixed 128+256 token cache is a larger fraction of total tokens.
- **H2Share (cross-layer index sharing) is an appendix-only extension** — inferred. It's presented with only one ablation figure (Figure 12) and limited discussion; it appears less thoroughly validated than the core AoH method and is described as building on AoH's "strongest scalability," a somewhat promotional framing not fully backed by extensive experiments in the main text.
- **No discussion of classification stability under fine-tuning or quantization** — not addressed in the paper. Since AoH depends on exact frozen weight geometry, it's unclear how robust the retrieval/streaming labels are after further fine-tuning, LoRA adaptation, or quantization of the model — a plausible deployment concern given the method's core selling point is being computed once from weights.
- **Group aggregation ablation shows some sensitivity** — stated (as an ablation, not a caveat by the authors, but worth flagging). Max-aggregation for GQA groups drops average LongBench score by ~1.7 points vs. mean, indicating the method's group-level rule isn't fully robust to aggregation choice, though the authors mitigate this by choosing mean by default.
