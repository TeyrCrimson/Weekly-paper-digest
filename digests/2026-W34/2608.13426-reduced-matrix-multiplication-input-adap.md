# Reduced Matrix Multiplication: Input-Adaptive Matrix-Product Reduction for LLM Inference

**Authors:** Zixuan Lan, Yanhong Li, Jiawei Zhou

**arXiv:** [2608.13426](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13426)

**Categories:** cs.LG, cs.AI, cs.CL

**Submitted:** 2026-08-13

**Relevance:** 8/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
RMM is a training-free, input-adaptive inference method that speeds up Transformer LLMs by dynamically selecting a subset of "informative" indices along the contraction (shared) dimension of each matrix multiplication in attention and MLP layers, based on activation magnitudes. Tested on models from 1B to 70B parameters (plus a vision-language model), it beats static pruning, random pruning, and H2O baselines at matched compute budgets, and shows attention computations tolerate much more reduction than MLP computations. Custom Triton kernels demonstrate real wall-clock speedups (up to ~1.9x on isolated GEMM ops, ~1.4x end-to-end at 4096 tokens) on an A100 GPU.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Every Transformer forward pass is dominated by matrix multiplications: projecting hidden states into queries/keys/values, computing attention scores (QK^T), aggregating values (attention-weights × V), and the large up/gate/down projections inside the MLP block. All of these have the general form Y = AB, where A is an activation matrix (n×d) and B is a weight matrix (d×m), and computing Y costs O(n·d·m) — proportional to the size of the shared "contraction" dimension d. The paper asks: does every one of the d indices along that shared axis actually need to be computed for a given input, or is there redundancy that can be skipped on the fly?

**Why prior approaches fall short.** Existing efficiency methods attack different axes of the problem:
- *Static/structural pruning* (SparseGPT, Wanda, SliceGPT, magnitude pruning) permanently removes weights or dimensions ahead of time, based on some calibration set. This is a fixed, one-size-fits-all decision that doesn't adapt per input.
- *Context/cache-level methods* (KV-cache eviction like H2O, token compression, sparse attention) shorten the sequence or manage what's cached, but don't touch the actual matrix-multiply computation inside a layer.
- *Activation sparsity methods* (TEAL, CATS) skip small-magnitude entries in activation tensors feeding into projections — input-dependent, but scoped only to the activations supplied to linear/MLP layers, not to the internal products inside attention (QK^T, and attention-weights×V).
- *Classical randomized approximate matrix multiplication* (Drineas et al. 2006) samples column-row pairs by importance and averages over many draws to reduce variance — but a single Transformer forward pass gives no chance to average over repeated samples, so stochastic sampling has unreliable per-instance quality.

None of these directly ask, and answer, "can the contraction axis of each individual matrix product be adaptively shrunk per input, for every kind of matrix product in the network (not just projections)?"

**What RMM actually does.** For any matrix product Y = AB with shared dimension d, RMM:
1. Computes an importance score for each index j along the shared axis: s_j = ||A_:,j||_2 (the L2 norm of that column of activations under the *current* input).
2. Picks the top ⌈ρd⌉ indices by this score (ρ is a user-set "retention ratio," e.g. 0.8 keeps 80%).
3. Computes only the reduced product A_:,I · B_I,: over that selected index subset — the rest is simply not computed.

This is applied to: (a) linear/MLP projections (selecting from the hidden or intermediate dimension), (b) the QK^T attention-score product (selecting from the per-head feature dimension using query-column norms), and optionally (c) the attention-weights×V product (selecting from the token dimension using attention-weight column norms). Because the selection is recomputed from the live activations at every layer/head/decoding step, the retained subspace can differ across inputs, layers, and time steps — this is what the authors call "input-adaptive." No weights are modified or removed; the same pretrained weight matrix is just indexed into differently on the fly.

**Why TopK-by-norm and not something else.** The authors prove (Appendix E) that if you only observe A (not B) at selection time, TopK-by-column-norm minimizes the worst-case Frobenius-norm approximation error over all possible B — i.e., it's minimax optimal among any selection rule that looks only at the activation side. This gives a principled reason for using activation magnitude rather than, say, some learned or heuristic criterion.

**Why dynamic beats static (the mechanistic story).** The authors report (Appendix A.1) that when they tried identifying a single fixed low-dimensional subspace from a reference dataset, it worked on that dataset but failed to generalize to other tasks — suggesting Transformer representations don't have one global low-rank structure; "important" dimensions shift with input, layer, and decoding step. RMM's per-input recomputation of the selected subspace is designed to track this drift, whereas static pruning bakes in a single snapshot.

## Key insights

- **Reframing "what to reduce":** the genuinely new framing is operating at the level of the matrix-product contraction axis itself (applicable uniformly to projections, QK^T, and PV), rather than pruning weights, tokens, or activation entries in isolation. This is a legitimate conceptual unification, and the theoretical minimax-optimality argument for TopK-by-norm selection is a real (if modest) contribution, not just an empirical heuristic.
- **Evidence supports:** under matched retention ratios, RMM consistently beats static pruning (SparseGPT/Wanda/SliceGPT/magnitude), random dynamic pruning, and H2O on QA and summarization benchmarks (Tables 1–2), and the ablations (dynamic selection vs. static variant; activation-aware vs. random) show both design choices contribute to the gains — this seems reasonably well supported by the presented data.
- **Structural asymmetry finding:** attention-side computations (Q, QKV projections, QK^T, PV) tolerate much more aggressive reduction than MLP components (Up/Gate/Down projections), and reducing the *whole* MLP block simultaneously is far more damaging than reducing any single MLP projection alone (Table 16 / Appendix C). This is a useful, actionable empirical finding for anyone deploying such a method, though it is model/architecture-specific (studied mainly on LLaMA 3.1 8B).
- **Scale trend:** larger models generally tolerate more aggressive reduction (e.g., LLaMA 3.1 70B stays near full performance at RR=0.8 while smaller models degrade earlier), but the authors themselves caveat this is not uniform — it varies by model family and task (Qwen3 32B doesn't show as clean a trend, and some Qwen3 32B RMM numbers actually exceed baseline, which is odd and unexplained).
- **Practical latency claim:** wall-clock benefit is demonstrated (not just theoretical FLOPs reduction) via custom Triton kernels, with speedups increasing at longer sequence lengths (1.05x at 1024 tokens up to 1.40x at 4096 tokens end-to-end) — this is a claim actually backed by measurement, not just complexity analysis.
- **Extends to multimodal:** shown to work on Qwen2.5-VL-7B with similar qualitative behavior (attention maps stay focused on relevant image regions), though the authors explicitly say this is a limited/preliminary exploration.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Training-free scope only, not explored beyond it** (stated): the paper explicitly limits itself to training-free application and has not studied incorporating the idea into pretraining or fine-tuning.
- **Multimodal analysis is shallow** (stated): the authors admit they have not conducted a detailed study of how reduction behaves across the different components of vision-language models, despite presenting VLM results as a headline generalization claim.
- **No universal retention ratio; deployment guidance is heuristic** (stated): the paper recommends users run an ad hoc "label-free consistency sweep" (comparing dense vs. RMM outputs on unlabeled prompts) to pick a retention ratio per deployment, rather than providing a principled or automatic method — this is presented as a "preliminary example" with just 87/100 passages matching at RR=0.7, a small and informal validation.
- **KV-cache is not actually reduced** (stated, Appendix D.1): RMM reduces FLOPs in the score and value-aggregation products but does not shrink the stored KV cache or eliminate K-cache memory access, so claimed efficiency gains are compute-only, not memory-bandwidth gains — a significant caveat for real deployment where memory bandwidth is often the bottleneck at scale.
- **Selection overhead not fully accounted for at scale** (stated): complexity analysis explicitly excludes norm computation, TopK selection, and gather overhead; latency experiments are only run on LLaMA 3.1 8B on one GPU (A100) at batch size 1 — no wall-clock numbers given for the 70B model or larger batch sizes in the main text (mentioned as being in the appendix, but that section is in the truncated middle of the provided text, so not verifiable here — "not addressed in the provided text").
- **Aggressive reduction still degrades performance substantially** (stated): the authors admit that even with dynamic selection, RR=0.5 causes real degradation on some tasks (e.g., GSM8K accuracy on Qwen3.1 7B collapses from 39.9 to 1.7 at RR=0.5), undercutting the "smooth and predictable trade-off" framing at more aggressive settings.
- **Inconsistent/non-monotonic scaling trend** (inferred): Table 3 shows Qwen3 32B's RMM accuracy at RR=0.5–0.9 sometimes *exceeds* the full baseline (e.g., COPA 82–83.6 vs. baseline 81.4; HumanEval 40–46 vs. baseline 37.8), which is left unexplained and is suspicious — it suggests either evaluation noise, benchmark quirks, or possibly non-trivial variance not addressed by error bars/repeated runs anywhere in the text.
- **No confidence intervals or repeated-run variance reported** (inferred): despite claiming "smooth and predictable" behavior, no standard deviations or multiple-seed results are shown for the QA/generation benchmarks (only latency numbers are averaged over runs), making it hard to judge whether small deltas between methods (e.g., RMM vs. Static at RR=0.8 in Table 2) are meaningful.
- **Baseline comparison for TEAL is limited and mixed** (stated): the paper explicitly says "RMM does not outperform TEAL in every individual setting," which is an honest admission, but the comparison is run only on one model (LLaMA 3.1 8B) at one retention ratio (RR=0.7), and part of this section falls in the truncated middle of the paper so the full picture isn't available here.
- **Wall-clock speedup benchmarks are narrow** (inferred): end-to-end latency is measured only at batch size 1, on a single model (LLaMA 3.1 8B), single GPU (A100), single retention ratio (ρ=0.8), and the dense baseline uses HuggingFace `generate` with SDPA rather than a more optimized inference stack (e.g., vLLM, TensorRT-LLM) — real-world deployment gains under batching, quantization, or more competitive serving baselines are not addressed in the paper.
- **"Reduction tolerance depends on model family, task, component, and ratio" is a hedge baked into the abstract itself** (stated) — the authors' own framing acknowledges the method's benefits are inconsistent and context-dependent rather than a clean universal win, which somewhat weakens the "scalable direction" claim in the conclusion.
