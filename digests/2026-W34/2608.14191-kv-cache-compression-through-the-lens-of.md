# KV Cache Compression Through the Lens of Transform Coding

**Authors:** Hannah Laus, Claudio Mayrink Verdun, Hao Wang, Flavio du Pin Calmon, Felix Krahmer

**arXiv:** [2608.14191](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14191)

**Categories:** cs.LG, cs.CL, eess.SP

**Submitted:** 2026-08-14

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
The KV cache in transformer inference is usually compressed by minimizing reconstruction error on the keys/values themselves, ignoring how that error actually propagates through attention. This paper proves that, under a white-noise quantization model, attention-output distortion decomposes exactly into additive key and value terms that factor across tokens and channels, and uses this to derive Attention-Aware Transform Coding (AATC): whiten key/value channels, then allocate bits per channel via reverse waterfilling weighted by query magnitude and output-projection sensitivity. On Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct across five benchmark suites, AATC hits ~5.8x compression that is statistically indistinguishable from FP16, while KIVI, PALU, and KVQuant baselines each degrade noticeably on at least one benchmark.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** During LLM inference, each token generates a key vector and value vector at every layer; these are cached so future tokens' attention can look back without recomputation. As context length grows to hundreds of thousands or millions of tokens, this KV cache — not the model weights — becomes the dominant memory cost. The natural fix is to store keys/values at lower precision (quantization) or in a compressed lower-dimensional form (low-rank projection), but every bit saved risks corrupting the model's answers.

**Why prior quantization approaches are mis-targeted.** Existing methods (KIVI, KVQuant, PALU, KVTC) pick quantization schemes to minimize reconstruction error of the cached keys/values themselves — i.e., they minimize `E‖k - k̂‖²` and `E‖v - v̂‖²`. But the thing that actually matters for the model's output is the *attention output* after keys/values pass through the softmax-weighted averaging and output projection. A channel or token can have huge raw reconstruction error but contribute almost nothing to the final output (e.g., a token the query barely attends to), while another channel with small raw error could be exactly the one the query "reads." Optimizing the wrong objective wastes bits on unimportant components and under-protects important ones. Even the closest prior work (KVTC), which does apply transform coding, acknowledges this proxy gap.

**Transform coding and reverse waterfilling (background from signal processing).** This is the classical two-step recipe used in codecs like JPEG for allocating a fixed bit budget across a correlated signal: (1) apply an orthonormal "whitening" transform that decorrelates the components and concentrates energy into a few high-variance coefficients (this doesn't change total distortion since it's orthonormal); (2) allocate bits per coefficient via "reverse waterfilling" — a closed-form rule that gives more bits to high-variance coefficients and literally zero bits (i.e., drop entirely) to coefficients whose variance falls below a threshold ("water level"), tuned so the total bit budget is met. This is optimal for Gaussian-like sources under high-rate approximations.

**This paper's mechanism, step by step:**
1. *Attention-aware distortion measure (Theorem 1).* The authors model quantization noise as zero-mean, symmetric, independent across tokens/channels/key-vs-value ("white-noise quantization," the standard idealization in rate-distortion theory). Under this model, they do a second-order Taylor expansion of the softmax response to key perturbations and show that the expected squared error of the *attention output* (not the raw KV values) decomposes almost exactly into two additive pieces: a key-distortion term `D_K` and a value-distortion term `D_V`, plus a provably small remainder `R`. Critically, `D_K` and `D_V` further factor as a product of a *token-dependent weight* (how much that token matters — attention weight squared times how much its value differs from the current output) and a *channel-dependent weight* (how much the query cares about that key channel, i.e. `q_c²`, or how much the output projection weights that value channel, i.e. `‖W_O,c‖²`). This factorization is the paper's central theoretical contribution: it shows that many previously separate heuristics (token eviction methods like H2O/SnapKV, channel-aware quantization methods, output-projection-aware methods) are all just optimizing one piece of this single unified distortion formula.
2. *Whitening (Step 1 of AATC).* Using a calibration dataset, compute the empirical covariance of the model's activations, take its Cholesky factor, and use it to "whiten" the key/value projection matrices via an SVD-based factorization. This produces a transformed representation where channels are decorrelated and ordered by importance (singular value), analogous to PCA — closely related to what PALU and KVTC already do for the transform step.
3. *Attention-aware bit allocation (Step 2 of AATC).* Instead of allocating bits to minimize plain reconstruction variance (as KVTC/PALU implicitly do), AATC runs reverse waterfilling weighted by the query-derived term `q_c²` (for keys) and the output-projection norm `‖W_O‖²` (for values), estimated from the calibration set. This yields per-channel, per-layer bit counts — including zero bits for unimportant channels, which is effectively adaptive rank reduction, unlike PALU's fixed-rank truncation.
4. *Runtime.* At inference, each new token's key/value is projected into the whitened space, quantized per-channel at its allocated bit-width, cached, and reconstructed by multiplying back by the inverse transform when computing attention.

**Evaluation.** They compress the KV cache of Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct roughly 5.8x and check accuracy on LongBench (long-context QA/summarization/code), RULER (long-context retrieval), GSM8K/MATH-500 (math reasoning), and MMLU-Pro (multiple choice), comparing against KIVI (per-channel/per-token uniform quantization), KVQuant (non-uniform, sensitivity-weighted), and PALU (low-rank projection).

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new (claimed and evidenced):** A closed-form proof (Theorem 1) that attention-output distortion under KV quantization decomposes additively into key and value terms, each factoring into token-weight × channel-weight. This is a real theoretical contribution — it's a first-order/second-order Taylor analysis of softmax under noise, not just an empirical trick, and the paper provides a full proof (Appendix A) with an explicit bound on the remainder term.
- **Unifying framework (claimed):** The paper argues this decomposition subsumes/explains why token-eviction methods (H2O, SnapKV, StreamingLLM), channel/query-aware quantization (A2ATS, SQuat, OSCAR), and output-projection-aware methods each work — they're each implicitly optimizing one factor of the same objective (Table I). This is a useful conceptual contribution but is more an *interpretive/organizational* claim than something independently tested — the paper doesn't run head-to-head experiments against all these token-eviction methods.
- **Practical method (claimed and evidenced):** AATC, combining whitening + attention-aware reverse waterfilling, achieves near-lossless accuracy (within 2 standard errors of FP16) across 18 benchmark cells at ~5.8x compression, while all three baselines (KIVI, PALU, KVQuant) degrade on at least one benchmark. The evidence for this specific claim is reasonably solid: consistent results across two model families and five benchmark suites, with a statistical-indistinguishability criterion defined up front.
- **Ablation supports the "attention-aware" part of the claim, with caveats:** The "AATC var-only" ablation (same transform, but ignoring `q_c²`/`W_O` weighting — essentially a per-layer analog of KVTC) is statistically tied to full AATC at 5.8x on Llama, but shows a real gap on Qwen and at more aggressive 2-bit/7x compression, especially for long-context RULER. This is honest and useful: it shows the attention-aware weighting matters most exactly where compression is most aggressive, but also shows it barely matters in the milder, headline 5.8x setting for one of the two tested models — the "wins" are concentrated in the harder regime and in Qwen specifically.
- The comparison table (Table VI) ablation shows the bit-allocation strategy (adaptive vs. global) is the dominant factor (8.6 LongBench points), while the specific attention-aware terms (`q_c²`, `W_O`) contribute smaller but nontrivial amounts (3.8 and 0.8 points respectively) — useful for readers wanting to know which design choice actually carries the paper's gains.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Cross-token independence assumption is an acknowledged idealization** (stated): the white-noise model assumes quantization errors are independent across tokens, but the authors explicitly note neighboring KV representations are correlated in real language, and that exploiting this dependency would require an intractable token-varying allocation. They claim it "does not seem to have a strong negative impact" but this is asserted rather than rigorously quantified.
- **No system-level/CUDA implementation** (stated): the method lacks a production-ready kernel implementation, so real-world latency/throughput benefits are not demonstrated — only accuracy at a given compression ratio, not actual wall-clock or memory-bandwidth speedups.
- **Only two model families tested** (stated): Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct; authors acknowledge broader model coverage (different scales, architectures) is left to future work.
- **Remainder term R is only asymptotically small** (inferred/partially stated): Theorem 1's decomposition is exact only up to a remainder that is proven small as quantization noise → 0, i.e., in the high-rate limit. The paper's own low-bit experiments (2-bit, 1.5-bit, 1-bit) are precisely the regime where this approximation is least justified theoretically, even though they report it "remains predictive." This is a genuine tension between the theory's assumptions and the aggressive-compression regime being marketed as a strength.
- **Baseline reimplementation, not baseline authors' code** (inferred): "For KIVI and KVQuant, we reimplemented their method" — reimplementations can inadvertently disadvantage baselines relative to published numbers, and no comparison to the original papers' reported results is given to check fidelity.
- **Uneven baseline treatment of context windows** (inferred): the authors added a recent-token full-precision window (size 128, flushed in blocks of 32) and kept first-4-tokens in full precision for KIVI/KVQuant "for fairness," and used their own hyperparameters for PALU (30% dimension reduction + 4-bit). Whether these choices are optimally tuned for the baselines (vs. tuned mainly for AATC) isn't independently verified, raising a mild risk of an implicit tilt toward AATC.
- **KIVI shows an unusually catastrophic collapse on Qwen** (inferred): KIVI drops to 0.351 LongBench average and near-random on some RULER/GSM8K settings for Qwen, while performing fine on Llama — this asymmetry is large enough (e.g., GSM8K 0.818→0.165) to warrant scrutiny of whether the KIVI reimplementation or its default settings are mismatched to Qwen's architecture, rather than reflecting a fundamental weakness of the method itself; the paper offers no specific diagnosis for this beyond "Qwen is markedly harder to compress."
- **Statistical-indistinguishability criterion is somewhat generous** (inferred): defining "lossless" as within 2 standard errors of FP16 on benchmark scores is a relatively loose bar, especially for benchmarks with small per-example sample counts (MATH-500 has only 500 examples), so "near-lossless" claims should be read with this precision caveat in mind.
- **Ablation experiments run only on Llama at 2-bit** (inferred): Table VI's design-choice ablation (which factors matter most) is conducted only for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, not Qwen, despite Qwen being where the attention-aware weighting showed the largest benefit in the main results — so the ablation may not fully explain why Qwen benefits more.
- **Calibration data choice not deeply justified** (stated as inconclusive in the paper itself): the ablation on calibration corpus composition (FineWeb vs. OpenR1-Math vs. mixture) shows only modest differences, and the paper's own interpretation ("reasoning text being important for calibration") is speculative and not further tested.
- **Middle sections of the paper (Section IV-B mapping to other methods, Appendix B derivation, Appendix D transform equivalence, Appendix C classification of related work) were truncated in the extracted text**, so some supporting details behind the "unifying framework" claim could not be verified from the provided text — not addressed in the available excerpt.
