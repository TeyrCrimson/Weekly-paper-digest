# TEMPO: Makespan-Aware Expert-Parallel Load Balancing Across Memory- and Compute-Bound Regimes

**Authors:** Jie Li, Chenxin Jia, Jinliang Shen, Cunzhuang Liu, Ruiyi Ding, Jianwen Xian, Kang He, Chengru Song

**arXiv:** [2608.13057](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13057)

**Categories:** cs.DC, cs.AI, cs.CL, cs.GT

**Submitted:** 2026-08-13

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
Current MoE expert-parallel dispatchers balance a single proxy—token counts or activated-expert counts—but real GPU cost is neither: below ~156-168 tokens/expert, HBM weight-streaming dominates (cost scales with activated replicas), and above it, grouped-GEMM tile padding makes splitting an expert costly, and real decode batches sit in both regimes at once. TEMPO fits a cheap two/three-term max-affine cost model per (GPU, kernel, dtype), formalizes per-batch dispatch as an NP-hard fixed-charge makespan problem, and solves it in milliseconds with a heuristic (+ensemble safety net) that tracks the best fixed policy everywhere and beats it by up to 15.5% where regimes mix. End-to-end on SGLang it gives Qwen3-235B (inside the predicted "win region") 4-6% throughput and ~15.6% p99 latency gains, while DeepSeek-V3 (outside the win region, communication-dominated) shows only overhead-free parity — the paper's core claim is the predictive phase diagram itself, not a universal win.

## First-principles breakdown

**The setting.** In expert-parallel (EP) MoE serving, each layer's experts are sharded across GPUs. Every layer does: router → all-to-all dispatch of tokens to their assigned experts → per-GPU grouped-GEMM compute of each resident expert's FFN → all-to-all combine back. Both all-to-alls are synchronization barriers, so the wall-clock cost of a layer equals the *makespan*: the time of the single slowest GPU that step, not the average. This is why load balancing matters — one overloaded GPU stalls all others every layer, across dozens of layers.

**Two layers of balancing.** A *placement* layer (e.g., DeepSeek's EPLB) decides, on a minutes-scale horizon, which physical GPUs host replicas of which experts, based on long-run average load. A *dispatch* layer decides, per incoming batch, how that batch's tokens for each expert get split across the expert's available replicas. Because routing fluctuates far more batch-to-batch than the long-run average, dispatch is where most of the recoverable imbalance lives — this paper's target.

**Why existing dispatchers are wrong.** Existing dispatchers optimize a *proxy* for GPU time because it's easy to reason about: token-count balancers (EPLB's default, LPLB, UltraEP) minimize the max tokens per GPU, implicitly assuming time is linear in tokens. Activation-count balancers (METRO) minimize the max number of *distinct experts* instantiated per GPU, implicitly assuming time is linear in expert count. The paper's central empirical finding is that neither assumption holds, for two separate hardware reasons:
1. **Memory-bound regime (low token count per expert):** Loading an expert's weight matrices from GPU HBM is a fixed cost per instantiated expert-replica, independent of how many tokens it processes. Below ~156-168 tokens/expert (measured on their GPUs), this weight-streaming cost dominates — an expert with 5 tokens costs about the same as one with 100. So the "cost" is per *activated replica*, invisible to a token-count metric.
2. **Compute-bound regime (high token count per expert):** Grouped-GEMM kernels round every expert's token batch up to the nearest 128-token tile (an M-tile). Splitting one busy expert across k replicas triggers k separate roundings, manufacturing wasted "padded" compute that a pure token-count objective doesn't see either.

They capture both with a simple **max-affine cost model**: `t = max(a + bG, c + βN)` where G = activated (expert, replica) pairs on a GPU and N = tokens on that GPU; b is the per-replica weight-streaming floor, β is the per-token compute slope. A refined version adds a third piece to model the sub-linear "staircase" of tile-boundary effects (Eq. 2), and a fourth term for network all-to-all cost at multi-node scale (Eq. 4). All parameters are fit via a short black-box calibration (~10 minutes) by benchmarking the deployed kernel across a (G, N) grid — no need to derive them analytically.

**The killer observation:** a single real decode batch typically contains *both* regimes simultaneously — a handful of "hot" experts deep in the linear (compute-bound) region carrying most tokens, and many "cold" experts sitting in the flat (memory-bound) region. So neither proxy is a safe simplification even within one batch; whichever proxy you pick is "systematically wrong" in the other's home regime, and measured dispatch decisions from the two proxies differ 1.4-1.7x in true cost, with the losing proxy flipping depending on the regime mix.

**Formalizing the real problem.** The paper poses dispatch as a *fixed-charge makespan* optimization: minimize the max per-GPU cost under the two-piece (or three-piece) model, choosing token splits and activation patterns. They prove this is NP-hard in general (reduction from balanced PARTITION — intuitively, the "max" of two terms turns one time budget into two simultaneous knapsack-like constraints, an activation cap and a token cap), but tractable in each pure regime alone (b→0 reduces to the token LP; β→0 reduces to a polynomial semi-matching problem). This mirrors the systems insight: each proxy is fine in its own regime; the hard part is exactly where they interact.

**The TEMPO algorithm (tempo_fast).** Rather than solve the NP-hard problem exactly, they use a fast heuristic: (1) greedy seeding — place whole experts by descending token count onto the least-loaded replica; (2) augmenting-chain moves to fix activation-count imbalance (the flat-regime fix); (3) bottleneck local search with partial token migrations to fix compute-bound imbalance (the linear-regime fix); (4) an ensemble step that also evaluates the classic token-LP and a round-robin baseline under the same cost model and switches to whichever wins by more than a 1% tolerance (this guarantees TEMPO is never much worse than the classical proxies "by construction," and inherits a provable additive approximation bound in the fully-replicated case). This runs in ~2ms, ~550x faster than an exact MILP solver, off the latency-critical path.

**Systems integration.** They integrate into SGLang: the probabilistic dispatch and per-expert token-count collection are fused into a single CUDA-graph-resident kernel (zero extra in-graph kernels/collectives), while the actual makespan solve runs out-of-process on a separate CPU worker, publishing refreshed dispatch tables asynchronously (with a race-tolerant table format so a torn read never crashes, just occasionally uses a stale/uniform fallback). This design choice matters practically — much of their measured advantage over SGLang's existing LPLB dispatcher turns out to be this decoupled architecture (avoiding an in-graph solver and per-layer collective), not the smarter cost objective per se.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new:** the empirical characterization that memory-bound weight streaming and compute-bound tile-padding are two ends of one mechanism (a "tile staircase") whose floor is measurable and hardware-grounded (matches a roofline HBM-bandwidth estimate within ~13-24%), and that realistic decode batches straddle both regimes *simultaneously* (92-100% of batches in their traces), which existing single-proxy dispatchers structurally cannot handle correctly.
- **Genuinely new:** formalizing per-batch expert dispatch as a fixed-charge makespan problem, proving NP-hardness precisely in the regime where both cost terms interact, and giving a fast heuristic with a proven additive-approximation guarantee under full replication — a principled floor under a fast heuristic, not just an empirical algorithm.
- **The "phase diagram" framing is the actual headline claim**, not a universal speedup: the paper explicitly frames its contribution as predicting *when* dispatch optimization helps (compute-bound-ish flagship-shape models with few logical experts per GPU, e.g., Qwen3-235B) versus when it doesn't (communication-dominated, many-experts-per-GPU settings like DeepSeek-V3, where the win predicted by the simulator collapses to mechanism-cost parity in real deployment). This falsifiable, bracketed evaluation (one model inside, one outside the predicted win region) is presented as evidence the model is doing real predictive work, not just fitting after the fact.
- **Honest self-critique embedded in the results:** they explicitly attribute most of TEMPO's advantage over SGLang's shipped LPLB dispatcher to integration architecture (out-of-process solving, fused kernels) rather than the smarter objective, once they port token-LP into the same architecture for a fair comparison — the objective's own edge narrows to stability at the compute-bound point, not raw throughput.
- **What's actually supported by evidence vs. claimed:** the core "track-the-best-everywhere, win up to double digits in mixed regimes" claim is supported across three evaluation layers (calibrated simulation, an 8-GPU wall-clock microbenchmark, and full SGLang serving on two models) — a stronger evidentiary chain than pure simulation. However, the largest headline gains (up to 15.5%) are explicitly flagged as "model-scored" extrapolations to EP32-64 configurations not physically tested on the available hardware.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Simulation-anchored, not fully wall-clock validated at scale.** The phase diagram and largest claimed gains (up to 15.5% at EP32-64) come from calibrated *simulation*, extrapolated beyond the 8-GPU testbed actually available. (stated)
- **Model transfer/calibration has real identifiability limits.** The authors report that on the full deployed pipeline, parameters `a` and `b` are "nearly collinear" (correlation -0.997) and cannot be independently pinned down from that data alone — successive refits report different (a,b) splits despite consistent solver behavior. (stated)
- **Negative control acknowledged directly:** for a small-expert model (Qwen3-30B shape), the calibration "degenerates" (fitted b becomes negative, i.e., meaningless), and measured runs show ±11% run-to-run variance from kernel non-determinism — meaning the model cannot discriminate policy quality there, and neither, in their account, does hardware. (stated)
- **DeepSeek-V3 result is essentially a null result dressed as validation.** Outside the predicted win region, "every workload lands at -2 to -3%, indistinguishable from the noop control" — i.e., the method shows no measurable benefit and the paper frames this as confirming the phase diagram rather than as a limitation of the approach's applicability. (stated, but also an inferable red flag: half of the two-model "bracketing" evidence is a demonstration of no effect)
- **Comparison against SGLang's shipped LPLB dispatcher is confounded by architecture**, and the authors admit "a like-for-like port attributes most of that gap to integration architecture rather than the objective" — meaning the flashy multi-x throughput numbers vs. LPLB substantially overstate the algorithmic contribution. (stated)
- **Traffic/communication model parameters are the least validated part of the system:** the (c2, γ) all-to-all terms are "fitted once, on the proxy pipeline's static-vs-uniform crossover, and reused unchanged on Testbed B," and the authors themselves flag this as "the least independently validated part of the model." (stated)
- **Retracted/non-replicating result disclosed:** "an earlier single-window +5% did not replicate; we retract it" for the 1024-request adaptive-vs-stale-static comparison — a positive sign of rigor, but also confirms some earlier headline-adjacent numbers were noise. (stated)
- **Anonymized hardware.** GPU models, microarchitecture, and vendor specs are anonymized "in this version," which limits independent reproducibility of the absolute timing numbers (though relative/regime conclusions are less affected). (stated)
- **Narrow model classes tested end-to-end.** Full serving evaluation covers only two model families (Qwen3-235B, DeepSeek-V3) plus a DSv2-Lite/proxy setup; generalization to other MoE architectures, other expert counts/shapes, or non-decode-heavy workloads beyond what's tested is not addressed in the paper. (inferred)
- **Restricted-replica (non-fully-replicated) placements lack theoretical guarantees.** The paper explicitly notes the additive approximation bound doesn't provably extend beyond full replication and calls this "an open problem," meaning realistic EPLB-style partial replication is only empirically, not provably, covered. (stated)
- **Multi-node results are heavily bring-up-dependent.** The authors describe several "hazards worth two orders of magnitude of performance" from NCCL/transport misconfiguration discovered during multi-node experiments — suggesting the measured baselines (and possibly the gains attributed to TEMPO) could be sensitive to infrastructure tuning that isn't standardized across production deployments. (stated, but the sensitivity itself is an inferred reproducibility risk)
- **Some excluded/discarded data points.** In the EP16 multi-node staleness experiments, "two further windows hit the bench-client artifact... and are excluded," and exclusion is governed by a "pre-declared" rule — the rule itself is described but not independently auditable from the text. (stated, with inferred risk that window-selection could still shape reported medians)
