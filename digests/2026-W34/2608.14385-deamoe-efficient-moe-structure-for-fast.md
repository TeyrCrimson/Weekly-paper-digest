# DeaMoE: Efficient MoE Structure for Fast Small-Batch Decoding

**Authors:** Zewen Jin, Shen Fu, Zeping Duan, Shannon Wang, Weihao Wu, Chengjie Tang, Congkun Ai, Ping Gong, Zijian Dai, Youhui Bai, Cheng Li

**arXiv:** [2608.14385](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14385)

**Categories:** cs.LG, cs.AI

**Submitted:** 2026-08-14

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
DeaMoE restructures Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layers so experts within a "department" share most of their weight matrices and each expert keeps only a small private delta, cutting redundant weight loading during small-batch decoding. Paired with a two-stage (department-then-expert) routing scheme, it reduces per-step loaded weights by up to 50.9% and delivers up to 1.33x end-to-end TPOT speedup on a 7.3B pre-trained model and up to 2.00x/1.97x microbenchmark speedup on DeepSeek-V3 (A40/H100), while matching baseline accuracy on downstream benchmarks. It requires pre-training the model from scratch under this architecture rather than being a post-hoc modification.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem: MoE decoding is memory-bound at small batch sizes.** In a standard MoE layer, each token is routed to a top-k subset of experts (e.g., 8 of 256), and each expert has its own full set of gate/up/down projection matrices. During interactive serving (coding assistants, voice/video agents), latency requirements force small batch sizes (roughly 4-128 tokens per decoding step). With few tokens but many possible experts, the union of experts activated in one step is large and weight reuse across tokens is minimal. Since GPU compute (matmuls) is cheap relative to the cost of streaming expert weights from memory, decoding time becomes dominated by *loading* expert parameters rather than computing with them — the workload is memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound. Modern models make this worse by having hundreds of experts (Kimi-K2: 384, Qwen3-Next: 512), each already tens of megabytes, so the odds that two tokens in a small batch share an expert shrink further.

**Why prior fixes don't target this.** Two existing directions exist: (1) *post-hoc compression* (e.g., MoLAE, D2MoE, MoE-SVD, MoBE) — after training, factorize each expert's weights into shared low-rank bases plus expert-specific coefficients to shrink total parameters. This can degrade accuracy because it's an approximation imposed after the model already learned full-rank weights. (2) *Pre-training-time fine-grained expert design* (e.g., NVIDIA Nemotron) — use many smaller experts, relying on large-scale expert parallelism (spreading experts across many GPUs) to reduce what any single GPU must load. This doesn't help when serving is small-scale (few GPUs, small batches), since parallelism isn't available to hide the loading cost. Neither line explicitly targets "loading redundancy under small batches."

**DeaMoE's mechanism.** The key structural idea: group the n experts into m "departments." All experts in a department share one large "backbone" for gate, up, and down projections (Wg, Wu, Wd — the expensive, big matrices). Each individual expert then adds a small private correction matrix (Ag, Au, Ad, sized hffn×hffn — much smaller than the backbone) that gets applied with a nonlinearity (SiLU) between the shared backbone computation and the expert-specific computation. Concretely, expert i's gate output is SiLU(Ai_g · σ(Wj_g x)) — the token first passes through the department's shared transform, then a nonlinearity, then the expert's small private transform. This is loosely analogous to how humans in the same professional department (e.g., different engineers) share deep common knowledge/tools but differ in small specialized skills — the "backbone vs. private parameters" framing.

The efficiency payoff requires a matching *routing* strategy: rather than routing straight to individual experts (which would still require loading each expert's private params scattered across departments, but worse, still force loading whichever departments' backbones are touched per token individually), DeaMoE uses two-stage routing. First, all tokens in the batch are grouped by which department their selected experts belong to. Each department loads its large shared backbone matrices *once* and processes all tokens assigned to it in a batch. Only after this shared computation does each token get routed to its specific expert's small private matrices for the final differentiation. Because the backbone (the large, expensive-to-load part) is loaded once per department per step rather than once per expert, the total bytes moved from memory drops substantially — this is the core trick.

To keep the comparison fair, they budget-match DeaMoE configurations against standard MoE baselines (same total parameters, same per-token FLOPs, computed via "group top-k" routing that caps how many departments/experts are activated), so any speedup isn't just from having a smaller model.

**Training details that make this trainable.** Two choices matter for quality: (1) a nonlinearity (SiLU, chosen empirically over GeLU/RMSNorm/none via ablation) must sit between the shared backbone and private expert transforms, otherwise stacking two linear maps degrades quality. (2) Expert-specific matrices are initialized as identity mappings (`torch.nn.init.eye`) rather than the standard zero-mean Gaussian used for normal MoE experts — this makes every expert initially behave exactly like its department's backbone, avoiding injecting random noise into a supposedly shared representation at the start of training; specialization is then learned gradually.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: a decoding-latency-first MoE parameterization (departments with shared backbones + lightweight private per-expert deltas) combined with a two-stage department-then-expert routing scheme, explicitly designed to reduce the *amount of unique weight bytes* loaded per decoding step, not to reduce total parameter count or rely on serving-time parallelism. This differs in target from prior compression (which degrades quality) or fine-grained-expert designs (which rely on parallelism unavailable in small-batch regimes).
- **Evidence supports**: (a) weight-loading reduction up to 50.9% and consistent 1.02x–1.33x end-to-end TPOT speedup for a small (7.3B) pre-trained model on A40 GPU across a batch-size sweep; (b) larger microbenchmark speedups (up to 2.00x/1.97x) on DeepSeek-V3-scale configurations on A40/H100, suggesting the technique's benefit scales with expert size — plausible since larger experts mean larger absolute bytes saved per department consolidation; (c) accuracy roughly on par with a budget-matched standard MoE baseline pre-trained from scratch on 110B tokens, across 10 downstream tasks and 3 perplexity benchmarks (mixed wins/losses, differences small).
- **Claimed but only partially/indirectly supported**: the DeepSeek-V3, DeepSeek-V2, Qwen3-235B-A22B, and Kimi-K2 "DeaMoE counterparts" are *not* actually pre-trained — only the 7.3B Baseline-7B/DeaMoE-7B pair was pre-trained and accuracy-evaluated. The large-model results (2.00x/1.97x speedups) are pure inference microbenchmarks on architecturally-adapted (not retrained) weight configurations, so we don't know if those larger DeaMoE variants would preserve accuracy if actually trained — this is inferred from the paper's own structure, not stated as a limitation.
- The non-monotonic loading-ratio curve (savings peak at small-to-moderate batch sizes, shrink toward baseline as batch grows) is a sensible and honestly-reported characterization of when the method helps — it doesn't oversell uniform gains across all batch sizes.
- The ablation study (separate, smaller 5B-scale model, 12.4B tokens) supports specific design choices (SiLU nonlinearity, identity init, soft vs. strict department-coverage routing) but is run on a different, smaller model than the main accuracy comparison.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **stated**: Ablations were run on a smaller-scale model (5B params, 12.4B tokens) than the primary accuracy comparison (7.3B, 110B tokens) "considering the limited resource budget for training" — design choices validated on ablation model, not directly on the evaluated model.
- **inferred**: Accuracy validation (pre-training + downstream benchmarks) was performed only for the 7.3B model. The headline large-scale speedup numbers (DeepSeek-V3, Kimi-K2, Qwen3-235B-A22B, DeepSeek-V2) come from microbenchmarks on budget-matched *architectural configurations*, not models that were actually pre-trained and accuracy-tested at that scale — so accuracy preservation at production scale is unverified.
- **stated**: On H100, gains for the smaller Baseline-7B model are "noticeably smaller" and show "mild regressions at very small and very large batch sizes," attributed to the large L2 cache on H100 already absorbing small-expert weight reuse — the method's benefit is hardware- and model-scale-dependent, not universal.
- **inferred**: Pre-training corpus is limited to 110B tokens (main model) or 12.4B tokens (ablation model) — both far smaller than typical production LLM pre-training runs (often trillions of tokens), so conclusions about accuracy parity at production training scale/duration are not established.
- **inferred**: Only one dataset family (RedPajama-v1) is used for pre-training; generalization to other corpora, larger context lengths, or instruction/RLHF-tuned settings is not addressed in the paper.
- **stated**: The paper explicitly rejects a "strict" routing variant that enforces exact department coverage per token because it hurt quality (ablation: loss 2.519 vs 2.504) — meaning the routing design space has real quality/efficiency tradeoffs and the softer constraint is a design choice validated only via training loss, not downstream task metrics.
- **inferred**: The comparison baseline throughout is "standard/vanilla MoE" — the paper does not compare DeaMoE's actual decoding speed or accuracy against the existing compression/fine-grained-expert alternatives it critiques (MoLAE, D2MoE, MoBE, MoE-SVD, NVIDIA fine-grained design) empirically; those are discussed only qualitatively in related work, not benchmarked head-to-head.
- **inferred**: Kernel implementation is custom (Triton, closely mirroring vLLM's fused MoE kernel) authored by the same team; while they state efforts toward fair comparison (same Grouped GEMM primitives, CUDA graphs enabled for both), no independent or third-party kernel benchmarking is presented, and kernel-level engineering skill could partially confound the reported speedups.
- **inferred**: Code is stated as "will be open-sourced later" — not yet available for independent verification at time of writing.
- **not addressed in the paper**: any discussion of training cost/time overhead introduced by the more complex DeaMoE architecture (e.g., additional matrix multiplies for shared+private paths) relative to baseline MoE training cost; also no discussion of memory/implementation complexity for the two-stage routing in distributed (multi-GPU, expert-parallel) production serving settings beyond single-GPU A40/H100 benchmarks.
