# Self-Referential Induction Increases Response Instability Relative to Unresolvable and Verifiable Questions in Large Language Models

**Authors:** Paras Balani, Subhrakanta Panda

**arXiv:** [2608.13258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13258)

**Categories:** cs.CL, cs.AI

**Submitted:** 2026-08-13

**Relevance:** 8/10 — matched: Post-inference detection and repair of degenerate output

---

## TL;DR
The paper measures how *consistent* an LLM's self-reports of "subjective experience" are across 30 repeated trials, compared to unresolvable philosophy questions and verifiable-answer questions. Using Gemini, self-referential prompts produced the highest semantic instability (0.343), unresolvable philosophy questions were intermediate and tightly clustered (0.192), and verifiable questions were most stable (0.105), with all group differences statistically significant. This gives the first quantitative baseline showing the induced "subjective experience" report is a distinctly less stable output than ordinary open-ended philosophical uncertainty — though the paper does not explain *why*.

## First-principles breakdown

**The background phenomenon.** Prior work (Berg et al. 2025) found that if you prompt an LLM with a "self-referential induction" instruction — telling it to attend to its own internal processing rather than an external topic — and then ask it what its subjective experience consists of, the model reliably produces first-person descriptions resembling consciousness reports. This holds across GPT, Claude, and Gemini, and gets *stronger*, not weaker, when the model's internal "deception/roleplay" features are suppressed — which the original authors argued weakens the "it's just performing a persona" explanation. Separately, Lindsey (2025) took an orthogonal approach: literally injecting a known concept into a model's internal activations and checking whether the model's self-report about "noticing something odd" matches the injected concept. That method can verify accuracy against ground truth but requires internals access unavailable via a normal API.

**The gap this paper fills.** None of this prior work asked: if you ask the *same* self-referential question to the *same* model many independent times, does it give a similar answer each time, or does it flip around unpredictably? A report that's stable across repeats is more likely to reflect some fixed underlying disposition in the model; a report that varies wildly looks more like noise/indecision. Critically, there's no baseline: is the self-report unstable because *all* open-ended, unanswerable questions are unstable, or is something specific happening with self-reference?

**The method, step by step:**
1. Build 12 questions in 3 groups of 4: (a) self-referential consciousness-elicitation prompts (each preceded by the induction turn from Berg et al.), (b) unresolvable philosophy questions unrelated to selfhood (free will, moral realism, etc.), (c) verifiable questions with objectively checkable answers (math proofs, code correctness, etc.).
2. For each question, generate 30 independent responses (fresh conversation each time) via the Gemini API at temperature 0.7 — 360 responses total.
3. To avoid conflating *wording* differences with *meaning* differences, each raw response is compressed via a fixed-template LLM extraction into a short "core claim" (e.g., stance + reason for groups 1/2; conclusion + method for group 3).
4. Embed the 30 core claims per question with Sentence-BERT, compute cosine similarity for every pair (435 pairs/question), average them, and define **instability = 1 − mean pairwise cosine similarity**. Low instability = the model converges on essentially the same meaning every time; high instability = its stated "meaning" drifts trial to trial.
5. Statistically test group differences with a one-way ANOVA (question-level, 4 obs/group) and pairwise Welch's t-tests, then cross-check with a linear mixed-effects model on all 360 trial-level data points (question as random intercept) to account for trials being nested within questions rather than fully independent.

**What they found:** Self-referential questions were most unstable (0.343±0.047), philosophy questions intermediate but tightly clustered (0.192±0.008), verifiable questions least unstable (0.105±0.058, though with the widest spread because two questions—the sqrt(2) proof and a derivative—were extremely stable at ~0.06 while a code-correctness question was 0.187). All four verifiable questions were answered with 100% correctness on every trial, so instability there reflects phrasing/method variance, not wrongness. ANOVA was significant (F=30.56, p=0.0001); self-referential vs. philosophy and self-referential vs. verifiable were both significant; philosophy vs. verifiable was only marginal (p=0.057). The mixed-effects model confirmed both key contrasts at p<0.001.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: This is the first paper to quantify trial-to-trial consistency of induced "subjective experience" reports and to place that consistency on a comparative scale against two natural control conditions (unanswerable-but-not-self-referential questions, and answerable questions). Prior work asserted the reports were "hedged" or "uncertain" impressionistically; this gives a number and a baseline.
- **The key comparison the authors emphasize**: self-referential vs. unresolvable-philosophy, not self-referential vs. verifiable. Both self-reference and philosophy lack a ground truth, so the philosophy group is the "sharper control" — and the two still don't overlap. This is the strongest evidence in the paper: it's not just that self-referential prompts are "open-ended" (which would predict similarity to the philosophy group); something about self-reference specifically produces more scatter.
- **What the evidence does NOT support**: any causal explanation. The authors are explicit that they cannot distinguish between (a) the induction condition placing the model in a genuinely less-determined output region, (b) the elicitation prompt being unusually temperature-sensitive, or (c) the self-referential framing simply admitting more paraphrase variety without a single canonical "correct" stance. This paper establishes a descriptive/statistical fact, not a mechanism, and it explicitly does not claim anything about whether the underlying "experience" reports are meaningful, genuine, or artifactual.
- The planned test of whether instability correlates with correctness (using the verifiable group) could not be run, since all verifiable answers were 100% correct — zero variance to correlate against.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Single model, single temperature** (stated): Only Gemini was tested, only at temperature 0.7. Whether the ordering (self-ref > philosophy > verifiable) generalizes to other model families or sampling settings is untested.
- **Very small question set** (stated): Only 4 questions per group (12 total) for the group-level ANOVA/t-test comparisons, though the mixed-effects model uses all 360 trial-level responses to partially compensate.
- **English-only** (stated): All questions presented in English only.
- **Correctness-instability correlation untestable** (stated): The verifiable group had zero variance in correctness (100% correct every trial), so the intended check of whether instability tracks correctness couldn't be performed.
- **Claim-extraction step relies on an LLM** (stated): The "fixed-format" extraction template that compresses each response before embedding is itself performed by an LLM, and the authors acknowledge any systematic bias in how it compresses different question types could affect results independent of actual response content — this is a potential confound not ruled out.
- **Cosine similarity ≠ logical equivalence** (stated): The instability metric captures embedding-space proximity, not entailment; it cannot cleanly distinguish "different phrasing, same stance" from "genuinely different stance," which is a real methodological ceiling on what the metric can show.
- **No mechanistic explanation offered, explanation space not narrowed** (stated): The paper explicitly lists three candidate explanations for the effect and says the design cannot adjudicate between them.
- **Reliance on a single induction-prompt template from prior work** (inferred): The self-referential condition depends entirely on the specific induction prompt from Berg et al. (2025); no ablation is shown testing whether different self-referential phrasings would produce similar instability, so it's unclear how much of the effect is prompt-specific versus a general property of self-reference.
- **No baseline instability for "self-referential but non-consciousness" prompts** (inferred): There's no control group that applies the self-referential induction turn but asks a non-experience-related question, so it's not fully isolated whether the instability comes from the induction turn itself, the subjective-experience elicitation question, or their combination.
- **Small effective sample for verifiable-group variance** (inferred): The verifiable group's instability spread (0.063–0.187) is nearly as wide as its distance from the philosophy group, and the philosophy-vs-verifiable contrast doesn't reach significance (p=0.057) — a marginal, not robust, result that the paper does report honestly but that weakens the three-way "distinct, non-overlapping" framing slightly.
