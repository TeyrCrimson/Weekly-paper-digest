# HalluTracer: Hallucination Detection via Depth-Averaging Truth Signals

**Authors:** Zhihao Guo, Zonghan Wu, Huan Huo, DaYong Ye, Junwei Zhang, Weiran Yao, Zhiwei Liu, Qingsong Wen, Yilei Shao

**arXiv:** [2608.16353](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16353)

**Categories:** cs.CL, cs.AI

**Submitted:** 2026-08-17

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: Post-inference detection and repair of degenerate output

---

## TL;DR
HalluTracer detects LLM hallucinations before any answer token is generated by reading a simple linear "truthfulness" probe at every Transformer layer and averaging the resulting per-layer scores into one number, rather than picking a single "best" layer as prior white-box methods do. The authors argue (and empirically support) that per-layer probe directions are nearly random/orthogonal to each other, so uniform depth-averaging cancels layer-specific noise and is close to the best possible linear combination. Across six open-weight LLMs and five hallucination benchmarks it beats matched single-layer/single-head baselines by 1–14 AUROC points.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** LLMs sometimes state falsehoods fluently and confidently. Prior interpretability work found that a model's internal activations often already "know" the answer is wrong, even though the output text doesn't reflect it — i.e., there's a linearly-decodable "truthfulness" direction in the hidden states. This opens the possibility of a lightweight classifier that reads internal activations and predicts "hallucination risk" without needing to generate (and judge) the full answer.

**Why prior approaches fall short.** Existing internal-state detectors (e.g., Contrast-Consistent Search, Inference-Time Intervention, Fact-Probe, IRIS) each pick *one* place to look: a single layer, a single attention head, or the final hidden state. But a Transformer's forward pass is a sequence of residual-stream updates — each layer refines the representation a bit (feed-forward blocks act like lookup memories, attention blocks route information) — so "truthfulness" isn't finalized at one depth, it's built up incrementally across all layers. Picking one layer therefore throws away signal, and worse, the best layer to pick is dataset-dependent (unstable/unprincipled).

**What HalluTracer does, step by step.**
1. **Readout position:** Take the hidden state at the *answer-onset* position — the last prompt token right before the model would start generating an answer. This means detection happens before any output token is produced ("pre-decoding").
2. **Per-layer probe:** At every layer, train a small linear (logistic regression) probe on top of one selected attention head's activation at that position. This maps the high-dimensional hidden state to a single scalar "truth logit" for that layer (Eq. 2-3).
3. **Trajectory:** Stacking these per-layer scalars across all layers gives a vector called the "logit trajectory" — essentially a depth-wise curve tracking how much the model's internal state "leans toward true" as computation deepens.
4. **Aggregation — the core idea:** Instead of picking the best single layer, just average the trajectory across all layers into one scalar (Eq. 9), then feed that single number to a simple logistic-regression classifier for the final hallucinated/not-hallucinated decision.
5. **Why averaging works (geometric argument):** The paper shows the probe direction learned at each layer captures very little of the variance of the hidden state (well under 1%, comparable to what a *random* direction would capture) — i.e., truthfulness lives in a tiny, "sparse" sub-signal. Because of this sparsity, consecutive layers' probe directions end up nearly orthogonal/uncorrelated (measured cosine similarity ≈ theoretical value for random unit vectors in that dimension). When per-layer signals are this weakly correlated, averaging them cancels out much of the layer-specific noise while the shared "class-level" signal (whether the statement is actually true) survives — analogous to averaging independent noisy measurements of the same underlying quantity to shrink variance. They formalize this with a signal-to-noise-ratio argument (Proposition 3.5) and an exact Fisher-optimality bound (Theorem 3.6) showing that the theoretically optimal (but expensive/impractical) linear combination of layers would only gain a few percent SNR over plain averaging.
6. **Mechanistic decomposition (§3.5):** They further break down what changes between consecutive layers' logits into a "probe direction change" component and a "readout/head-switch" component, showing the former carries most of the discriminative content — supporting the claim that depth averaging is capturing real signal, not selection noise.

## Key insights

- **Reframing, not just a new trick:** The paper's central conceptual contribution is recasting hallucination detection as a "depth-aggregation problem" rather than a "layer-selection problem" — this is a genuinely new framing relative to prior single-layer/single-head probes.
- **Simple mechanism, justified rigorously:** The method itself (average per-layer linear-probe outputs, feed to logistic regression) is very simple; the paper's value-add is the geometric/statistical justification for *why* this simple approach should work (probe sparsity + near-orthogonality → averaging is near Fisher-optimal), backed by empirical geometric measurements (cosine similarities, variance-fraction diagnostics, permutation tests) across models.
- **Evidence supports consistent, sometimes large gains:** Across 6 models × 5 benchmarks vs. matched pre-decoding baselines (Fact-Probe, ITI-Probe, IRIS), HalluTracer wins essentially everywhere, with the largest gains (up to ~14 AUROC / ~10 AUPRC points) on the more complex "Agentic" benchmark — suggesting the benefit of depth-aggregation grows with task complexity.
- **Ablations back the mechanism, not just the outcome:** Removing the mean and keeping only "shape" statistics (slope, zone-shift, etc.) collapses performance by >19pp; the trajectory mean alone matches the full multi-feature diagnostic set — directly supporting the "mean is the dominant signal" claim rather than it being an incidental result.
- **Claimed vs. supported nuance on optimality:** The paper is careful to state Fisher-optimality is a population-level, not necessarily attainable, bound (computing the true optimal linear combination would require estimating and inverting a large covariance matrix, likely not worth it in practice) — this is honestly caveated rather than oversold.
- **Cross-regime baseline (HalluGuard):** included mainly as a reference point operating under a different (richer, post-generation) information budget; the authors explicitly say its worse scores shouldn't be read as a fair head-to-head loss.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Scope limited to "propensity" hallucinations** (stated): The method only detects hallucinations already "decided" by the model's state before generation begins. It explicitly does not address hallucinations that emerge during generation itself (auto-regressive error accumulation, attention drift, multi-step reasoning failures, "snowball" errors) — the authors devote a full appendix section to this limitation.
- **Single-response granularity** (stated): Evaluated on isolated query-response pairs, not multi-turn dialogues or extended agentic reasoning chains, so applicability to those settings is unverified.
- **White-box requirement** (inferred): The method needs access to internal activations at every layer, so it cannot be applied to closed/API-only models — not explicitly framed as a limitation but is an obvious deployment constraint.
- **Small/simple final classifier, but many trained probes** (stated, partially addressed): While the final classifier is just a 2-parameter logistic regression on the mean, the pipeline trains a full linear probe per layer per model per dataset — the paper argues (via cross-domain evaluation and probe-free geometric checks) this doesn't amount to overfitting/shortcut learning, but this is an argument, not a definitive proof.
- **Cross-regime baseline may make comparisons look better than they are** (stated as caveat but still shown in headline comparison table): HalluGuard's poor scores are explicitly said to not be a fair comparison since it operates in a different, higher-cost information regime, yet it's still displayed as the weakest baseline in the main results table, which could visually inflate perceived margins for a casual reader.
- **Truncated appendix / unverifiable claims** (inferred): The provided text is truncated in the middle (e.g., portions of Appendix G–H derivations are missing), so some theoretical claims (e.g., full derivation of the Fisher gap, Gaussian sufficiency argument) could not be independently checked from this text.
- **Dataset/benchmark composition** (not addressed in the paper, as provided): No explicit discussion of how "hallucination" ground-truth labels were curated for each of the five benchmarks, nor of potential label noise or distribution shift between training and test folds beyond the stated group-disjoint cross-validation protocol.
- **Compute/latency cost of per-layer probing** (not addressed in the paper): No discussion of the inference-time overhead of extracting and probing every layer's activations relative to baselines that only need one layer/head — potentially relevant for the "real-time intervention" use case they advocate for in the Societal Impacts section.
- **Adversarial robustness only lightly addressed** (stated): The authors acknowledge that publishing the geometric characterization could inform adversarial evasion, but wave this away as requiring white-box access and being "constrained by high-dimensional geometry" without empirical testing of such attacks.
