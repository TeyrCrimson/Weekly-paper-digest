# Mixture-of-Expert Blocks Contain Strong Hallucination Detection Signals

## TL;DR
This paper introduces InnerExpert, a method that mines internal routing signals from Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) LLMs — router entropy, expert disagreement, expert usage patterns — to detect hallucinations at the per-token level using only a single forward pass. It trains a lightweight classifier on these signals (plus standard hidden-state/attention features) using labels from an LLM-as-a-judge pipeline, requiring no human annotation. Across five QA datasets and two MoE models, it reports up to 0.91 answer-level and 0.76 token-level AUROC, beating sampling-based, internal-signal, and trainable baselines while being far cheaper than multi-sample methods.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** LLMs sometimes generate fluent but factually wrong text ("hallucinations"). Detecting this is hard because you don't have ground truth at inference time — you need a proxy signal that correlates with "the model doesn't actually know this." Most existing detectors only score a whole answer or sentence as hallucinated/not, which doesn't tell you *which words* are the problem — useful for flagging/fixing specific spans.

**Why prior approaches fall short:**
- *Sampling-based* methods (SelfCheckGPT, Semantic Uncertainty/Entropy, INSIDE) generate the same prompt multiple times (e.g., 5x) and check whether the answers agree. If the model is consistent across samples, it's presumed confident/correct. This works reasonably but costs 5x+ inference compute and time, and only yields one score per answer, not per token.
- *Internal-signal* methods (LLM-Check, MIND) look at hidden states, attention patterns, or output logits from a single forward pass — cheap, but they only look at signals available in any transformer (dense or MoE), missing information that's unique to sparse models.
- *Trainable detectors* (HaluNet, FacLens) train classifiers on transformer-internal features, but again ignore MoE structure and operate at answer-level, not token-level.

**Background: what is MoE routing, concretely?** In a Mixture-of-Experts transformer, instead of one feedforward network (FFN) per layer, there are N separate FFNs ("experts"). For each token at each layer, a small "router" network looks at the token's hidden state and outputs a probability distribution over the N experts (via softmax), then picks the top-k experts (e.g., 8 of 64) to actually process that token; their outputs are combined (weighted by router probability) and added back to the residual stream. This routing decision happens fresh at every token and every layer — meaning it naturally produces a token-level signal stream that dense models simply don't have.

**The core hypothesis (theoretical hook).** The paper leans on the aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty decomposition: total predictive uncertainty = uncertainty from inherent ambiguity in the task (aleatoric, e.g. "the weather is ___" has many valid fillers) + uncertainty from the model's own lack of knowledge (epistemic, reducible with more information). Hallucinations are linked to *high epistemic uncertainty masked by a confident-looking output*. Since MoE routing decisions arise from a mixture of "expert opinions," disagreement among experts (or the router being unsure which expert to use) is argued to be an empirical proxy for this within-model disagreement/epistemic uncertainty — analogous to ensemble disagreement, an idea borrowed from a prior vision paper (Pavlitska et al. 2025) that used MoE segmentation models this way. This connection is asserted as motivation rather than rigorously proven for LLMs (the authors admit the formal Bayesian link breaks down for non-Bayesian, "misspecified" models like LLMs).

**The mechanism, step by step:**
1. **Generate** an answer token-by-token with the host MoE model (OLMoE-1B-7B or Gemma-4-26B), greedy decoding.
2. **Collect signals per token, per layer, cumulatively over the generated prefix so far:**
   - *Standard signals* (available in any transformer): (a) *Hidden-state score* — take the covariance of hidden states across the generated tokens so far, compute its singular values, average their logs. Low value = representations are low-rank/redundant = confident. (b) *Attention score* — cumulative log of the diagonal of the attention matrix, approximating how much the model attends to itself/consistently.
   - *MoE-specific signals* (six new ones, this paper's core contribution): 
     - *Router entropy* — Shannon entropy of the router's probability distribution over experts; high = router unsure which expert fits.
     - *Expert hidden score* — same "hidden-state score" idea but computed on each activated expert's own output sequence, weighted by router probability.
     - *Expert similarity* — average pairwise cosine similarity between the outputs of the activated experts for a token; low = experts disagree (like ensemble disagreement).
     - *Expert usage distribution, Gini impurity, inverse Herfindahl index* — track, over the whole generated sequence, how routing has spread across experts (concentrated on a few vs. scattered across many).
3. **Assemble a compact per-token feature vector** combining all these scores (order of ~1,360–4,470 numbers, tiny compared to full hidden states/attention matrices).
4. **Classify:** feed this vector into a lightweight, separately-trained classifier (they try Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, MLP, small Transformer) that outputs a per-token hallucination probability.
5. **Aggregate to answer level** by taking the max score over tokens (an answer is "hallucinated" if any token looks hallucinated).

**Training without human labels.** To train the classifier without manual annotation, they pull questions from RealtimeQA (a live, continuously-updated QA feed), generate two answers per question (one with retrieved evidence "RAG-style," one without, which is more hallucination-prone), and use another LLM (GLM-5.1) as a judge to compare each answer against the reference evidence, producing a binary hallucinated/not label plus the exact hallucinated substrings. These spans are mapped down to per-token binary labels by string overlap. This "judge" labeling is then validated against human annotations (86.5% agreement vs. 59.5% for a naive lexical-overlap heuristic baseline).

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new:** This is claimed (and appears well-supported by novelty search in the text) to be the first work to extract MoE-routing-specific internal signals (router entropy, expert disagreement, usage statistics) specifically for hallucination detection, and the first to combine such signals into a *per-token* (not just answer-level) detector.
- **The combination matters more than any single signal.** The signal-ablation analysis (Table 3) shows no individual MoE signal is consistently strong across both tested architectures — e.g., expert similarity is near-random on one model but decent on the other; router entropy is even *anti-correlated* on Gemma. Only the trained combination (via XGBoost/MLP) is consistently near the top. This is a legitimate, evidence-backed finding, not just a claim.
- **Efficiency claim is well supported:** single forward pass (~3x vanilla generation cost due to signal extraction) vastly undercuts sampling-based methods (5x+ generations, ~6-7x cost), while matching or beating their AUROC on the reported benchmarks (Figure 2 Pareto analysis).
- **Answer-level vs. token-level performance gap:** 0.91 AUROC answer-level is strong, but 0.76 token-level is markedly weaker — appropriately, the paper is transparent that this is the harder, less mature task, though it still claims to beat baselines there.
- **What's a claim, not fully demonstrated:** the theoretical "epistemic uncertainty" grounding (Appendix A) is offered as motivation/interpretation, not proof — the authors themselves note the formal Bayesian decomposition doesn't strictly apply to LLMs, so the routing-signals-as-epistemic-uncertainty story is a plausible narrative rather than a validated mechanism.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Small-scale human validation (stated).** The authors explicitly note their human validation of the LLM-as-judge labels used only 200 samples with a single annotator, and call for larger-scale, more diverse validation in future work.
- **English-only evaluation (stated).** All datasets and evaluations are English; generalization to other languages is left to future work.
- **Only two MoE architectures tested (inferred/implicit).** Despite claims of generality across "MoE architectures," only OLMoE-1B-7B and Gemma-4-26B were evaluated — both fairly specific scale/expert-count configurations (64 and 128+1 experts respectively). Whether findings hold for other MoE designs (e.g., different top-k, shared-expert ratios, much larger models like Mixtral or DeepSeek-MoE) is untested.
- **Threshold/classifier instability (stated in results, understated in abstract).** XGBoost achieves the best AUROC but its F1 (threshold-dependent) score "degrades sharply" on Gemma and even underperforms individual raw signals in some cases — the abstract's headline AUROC numbers mask real fragility in practical (thresholded) deployment.
- **Circular/self-referential evaluation labels (inferred).** All ground-truth labels — for training *and* evaluation — come from an LLM judge (GLM-5.1), not gold human annotations at scale. The reported AUROC numbers are therefore AUROC against another LLM's judgments, only spot-checked against humans on 200 examples. This is a meaningfully different (weaker) claim than "detects hallucinations" in an absolute sense.
- **Class imbalance skews some metrics (stated in appendix).** OLMoE's label distribution is extremely imbalanced (83–99.7% "hallucinated" for base/no-evidence generation), which the authors themselves flag as inflating F1 scores and making the F1 ranking "less informative than AUROC" for that model.
- **Short generations only (inferred).** Generation is capped at 65 new tokens per answer — a short-form QA setting. It's not addressed in the paper whether the cumulative signal aggregation (which grows over the sequence) remains discriminative for longer, multi-sentence generations typical of many real LLM use cases.
- **No ablation isolating standard vs. MoE-specific signal contribution in the full combined model (partially addressed).** Table 3 shows individual signal AUROCs, but doesn't clearly isolate how much of InnerExpert's full-combination gain comes specifically from adding MoE signals on top of standard signals versus just having more features/a stronger classifier in general — not fully addressed in the paper.
- **"First to..." novelty claims rely on the authors' own literature survey (inferred).** As with most papers, the claim of being the first to exploit MoE routing signals for hallucination detection is only as strong as the completeness of their related-work search; not independently verifiable from this text alone.
- **Judge-model dependency (inferred).** Both training labels and evaluation metrics depend on GLM-5.1 as judge; if that judge has systematic biases or blind spots (e.g., favoring certain phrasing, missing subtle factual errors), these would propagate into both the training signal and the reported performance numbers without being caught by AUROC comparisons among detectors.
