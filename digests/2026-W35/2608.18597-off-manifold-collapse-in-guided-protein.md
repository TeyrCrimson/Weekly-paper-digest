# Off-Manifold Collapse in Guided Protein Language Models

**Authors:** Shuibai Zhang, Xinchi Liu, Fred Zhangzhi Peng, Zhihan Yang, Shutong Wu, Yingzi Ma, Jiawei Zhang

**arXiv:** [2608.18597](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18597)

**Categories:** cs.LG

**Submitted:** 2026-08-19

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: Post-inference detection and repair of degenerate output

---

## TL;DR
Steering protein language models (PLMs) hard enough to shift a target property (e.g., solubility) pushes their internal activations off the "natural protein" manifold into a region statistically similar to random amino-acid input, producing degenerate, unfoldable sequences — even though the property oracle being optimized can still rate them as successes. The authors introduce **Mahalanobis filtering**, a training-free, near-zero-cost post-hoc check on a finished sequence's activations that discards these collapsed candidates, recovering structural plausibility (ESMFold confidence) without touching the generator, and the check transfers across different guidance methods.

## First-principles breakdown

**The setup.** Protein language models (PLMs) like ESM-2 are neural networks trained on huge databases of real proteins to predict masked amino acids, similar to how BERT predicts masked words. In doing so they learn an internal representation ("activations" — the numeric vectors at each layer of the network) that implicitly captures what a "natural, foldable" protein looks like. People want to *design* new proteins with a desired property (e.g., high solubility, high thermal stability). Fine-tuning the whole model for each property is expensive, so a cheaper alternative called **inference-time control** has become popular: leave the model's weights alone, but nudge its behavior at generation time.

**Two families of inference-time control used here:**
1. **Activation steering**: Compute a "direction" in activation space as the difference between the average activations of known high-property example sequences and low-property ones. During generation (which proceeds by repeatedly masking positions and letting the model re-predict them), add a scaled version of this direction vector to the model's hidden state at every layer, at every step. This is borrowed from LLM-steering techniques (e.g., "Steering Llama 2"). A strength parameter α controls how hard you push.
2. **Predictor-gradient guidance**: Instead of a fixed direction, compute the gradient of a trained property-predictor ("oracle") with respect to the activation, and nudge the activation along that gradient at each decoding step. This is mechanistically different (input-dependent direction vs. fixed vector) — used as an independent test of whether the paper's findings generalize.

**Why prior approaches fail (the core diagnosis).** There's a known tension: push gently, and the property barely moves; push hard, and the resulting sequences fold badly (low ESMFold pLDDT, a confidence score for structure prediction). Other papers noticed this trade-off and proposed fixes (logit-difference amplification, lookback Monte Carlo sampling) but treated it as a reason to abandon steering, without pinning down *what specifically breaks*. This paper's contribution is characterizing the failure mechanistically: as steering strength increases, the model's internal activations don't just get "different" in a useful way — they collapse toward the *statistical center* of the activation distribution, converging toward the same region you'd get by feeding the model **completely random amino-acid sequences**. In other words, aggressive steering doesn't produce a confident, unusual-but-valid protein; it produces something the model's own representations can't distinguish from noise. Concretely (Figure 1), pushing an ESM-2 solubility-steering run hard enough yields a 100%-glycine homopolymer that the solubility oracle scores as 0.99 (near-certain success) while ESMFold's structural confidence collapses to 0.35 — i.e., the property oracle is *fooled* by (or in the case of solubility, actively *rewards*) the degenerate output, while structure/composition metrics expose it as garbage.

**How they measure "off-manifold collapse" quantitatively — the Mahalanobis score.** They pick a middle transformer layer (layer 17 of 33 in ESM-2 650M) and look at the per-position activation vectors (1280-dimensional). From ~58M natural UniRef50 protein sequences, they estimate the per-channel mean (μ) and variance (σ²) of these activations once, offline. For a new candidate sequence, they standardize each activation channel ((h−μ)/σ) and sum the squares across all 1280 channels and average over sequence positions — this is a **diagonal Mahalanobis distance**, a classic statistical "how typical is this point under a Gaussian model" measure, simplified by ignoring cross-channel correlations (a deliberate approximation, validated later against a more expensive flow-based density model with Spearman r≈0.82). Under a Gaussian assumption, this sum should look like a chi-squared distribution with mean D=1280 for genuinely "natural" activations. What they observe: as steering strength α increases, this score falls steadily from ~1200 (natural range) down to ~600, which is indistinguishable from what you get by feeding the model literally random amino acids (score ≈604). This is the titular "off-manifold collapse" — not drifting to some *other* exotic-but-valid region, but collapsing inward toward the population mean/random baseline.

**The fix: Mahalanobis filtering.** Rather than trying to fix the generation process (which they show in an ablation, Appendix G, requires property-specific retuning and doesn't generalize), they propose a purely **post-hoc filter**: after generating a candidate sequence, run one forward pass through ESM-2, compute the Mahalanobis score above, and keep the candidate only if its score is above a threshold: D − k√(2D), with k=1 fixed once and reused everywhere (threshold ≈1229 for D=1280). This is a one-sided test — it only catches "collapsed toward the mean" failures, not sequences that are outliers in the opposite direction. Because it only reads the final activations of a completed sequence, it's agnostic to how that sequence was produced — it works the same whether generated by steering, gradient guidance, or unguided sampling. Cost: ~34ms and 10KB of stored statistics per sequence — negligible compared to generation itself, and 8-90x cheaper than alternative density estimators they tested (flow-based models, pseudo-perplexity).

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: The mechanistic characterization of *why* aggressive PLM steering fails — a measurable, representation-space "collapse toward randomness" — rather than just re-observing the property/structure trade-off (which prior work already reported from the output side). This is the paper's central diagnostic contribution and is well-supported by the evidence (Figures 2–3, the layer-17 sweep, and the random-amino-acid baseline comparison).
- **A specific, actionable red flag**: the property oracle itself can fail to detect (and for solubility, actively reward) the very failure it's being used to avoid — meaning practitioners cannot trust the optimization target alone as a success signal. This is demonstrated concretely (Figure 1, Table 1) rather than just claimed.
- **The filtering method (Mahalanobis filtering)** is the paper's practical contribution: cheap, training-free, generator-agnostic, and shown to transfer across two distinct guidance mechanisms (fixed-vector steering and gradient guidance) and two different properties (solubility, thermostability). The reported effect sizes are consistent and statistically supported via paired bootstrap CIs (e.g., +0.247 pLDDT for solubility, +0.187 for thermostability at matched settings).
- **What the evidence does NOT support**: that filtered steering universally beats filtered *unguided* generation (the paper explicitly says this is property-dependent, Appendix C) — the claim is narrower: filtering recovers quality *relative to its own guided parent pool*, not that steering+filtering is the best overall strategy.
- The method is explicitly a **selector, not a repair mechanism** — it cannot manufacture good candidates if the generator's output pool has no viable "on-manifold" tail (acceptance rates in some rows are near 0%, e.g., global/sol α=1: 0% accepted).

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Stated**: The filter is one-sided and only targets "inward" collapse (toward the natural mean); it explicitly does not catch "outward" collapse or composition-driven failures like homopolymers that might not show low Mahalanobis scores (Appendix H, Section 4).
- **Stated**: No wet-lab/experimental validation anywhere — all property and structure scores are computational (in silico) proxies; the paper explicitly states "no experimental validation is claimed" and computational metrics "do not by themselves establish activity."
- **Stated**: The property oracles are the authors' own retrains of published recipes (original checkpoints weren't public), and both underperform the originally reported accuracy (0.651 vs. 0.708 for solubility; 0.687 vs. 0.76 Spearman for thermostability); the thermostability oracle was trained without redundancy reduction, which the authors admit makes its held-out number "if anything optimistic."
- **Stated**: Generator-side repair (as opposed to post-hoc filtering) was tested across a 32-condition factorial and found to be property-specific and not uniformly effective (works for thermostability via bounded clamping, but no comparable fix worked for solubility) — this justifies the choice of post-hoc filtering over generator modification, but also signals the underlying steering mechanism itself remains unfixed.
- **Stated**: Layer 17 (middle layer) was chosen somewhat by precedent/convenience, not proven optimal; the authors say "we do not claim layer 17 is uniquely optimal."
- **Stated**: The diagonal-Gaussian / chi-squared approximation ignores cross-channel covariance and is explicitly not "an exact model of the latent distribution," and the threshold constant k=1 is described as "an empirical margin on a natural scale, not a sequence-level null quantile" — i.e., not rigorously calibrated, just tuned once and reused.
- **Inferred**: All experiments use a single PLM lineage (ESM-2 650M) — generalization to other architectures (ESM3, ProGen, ProtGPT2, diffusion-based generators) mentioned in the related work is untested, despite the paper's framing implying broad applicability to "protein language models" generally.
- **Inferred**: Reference sets like `lyso` are derived from a specific published steering paper's data and are fairly small (some accepted subsets in Appendix C have n=1 or n=2, explicitly flagged by the authors as "not summarized" due to small sample size) — some transfer claims rest on thin sample sizes in specific cells of the results tables.
- **Inferred**: The within-setting correlation between the cheap Mahalanobis score and the more expensive flow-based density model varies widely (Spearman r from 0.334 to 0.900 depending on the slice, per Table 7), which somewhat undercuts the headline pooled r=0.815 figure as evidence that the cheap statistic is a reliable stand-in for a "true" density estimate at the fine-grained selection level (the authors partially acknowledge this: "we do not claim that the two scores select the same candidates").
- **Inferred**: The "solubility oracle actively rewards collapse" finding (Figure 1) is a dramatic single-example illustration; the population-level version of this claim is deferred to an appendix (Appendix B) not included in the provided text excerpt, so the strength of the population-level effect beyond one cherry-pickable pair is not fully verifiable from the given text.
