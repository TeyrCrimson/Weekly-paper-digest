# Key Findings for Small UAS for Post-Disaster Damage Assessment from the CURSE'26 Exercise

**Authors:** Thomas Manzini, Stephen Johnson, Priyankari Perali, David Merrick, Robin Murphy

**arXiv:** [2610.29777](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29777)

**Categories:** cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-10-21

**Relevance:** 7/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
The CLARKE AI damage-assessment system was stress-tested in a 23-team, two-state disaster-response exercise (CURSE'26) on 9 never-before-seen orthomosaics, roughly 3x more test imagery than its prior real-world deployments. Building damage assessment performed well (Micro F1 0.834, good transfer across states, ~3.5 min inference) while road damage assessment performed badly, over-flagging ~35% of road length as damaged against an expected ~5% baseline, mainly due to GPS misalignment between road maps and imagery. The paper's broader contribution is eight operational/ethical findings about running ML-based damage assessment in real sUAS workflows, not a new model or algorithm.

## First-principles breakdown

**The underlying problem.** After a disaster (hurricane, flood, tornado), emergency managers need fast, accurate maps of which buildings and roads are damaged, so they can route resources. Small drones (sUAS) can photograph affected areas quickly, but turning thousands of raw photos into a damage map traditionally requires manual labeling, which is slow and labor-constrained during a crisis. CLARKE is a pre-existing ML pipeline (from the authors' prior papers) meant to automate this: given a drone-stitched map, it predicts a damage level for every building and every road segment.

**How CLARKE actually works (background, not new in this paper):**
1. Drone teams fly over an area and take overlapping nadir (straight-down) photos.
2. Software called PIX4Dreact stitches these into a single large "orthomosaic" image (a geographically accurate photo-map), done on a laptop in the field.
3. This orthomosaic is combined with two pre-existing vector datasets: building outlines (from Microsoft's US Building Footprints) and road centerlines (from OpenStreetMap). These tell the model *where* to look for buildings/roads within the image.
4. **Building Damage Assessment (BDA):** a Vision Transformer encoder + UperNet decoder (standard semantic-segmentation architecture) classifies each building polygon into a damage category from the Joint Damage Scale (JDS) — essentially "how wrecked is this structure."
5. **Road Damage Assessment (RDA):** a different architecture, Attention U-Net, trained on the same team's CRASAR-U-DROIDs dataset, classifies road segments as no/partial/total damage.
6. A human "data manager" reviews the output before it's shared with decision-makers (step 7 of their pipeline), because the model doesn't correct for spatial misalignment between the map data and the photo, and might fail in unfamiliar regions.

**Why prior evaluation was weak, and what this paper does differently.** CLARKE had only been tested on 4 real orthomosaics from two actual hurricanes (Debby, Helene) — a tiny, non-representative sample, and it already showed weak transfer to Pennsylvania's mountainous terrain. CURSE'26, a full-scale government-run training exercise, gave the authors 9 brand-new orthomosaics from Florida and Missouri, collected by 23 teams simulating a real incident-command workflow (teams fly → stitch map → upload → central Remote Sensing Cell runs CLARKE → human review → distribution). This is not a new model or training method; it's a larger, independent field evaluation of an existing system embedded in a real multi-agency operational process, which is valuable because almost no prior work tests disaster-response ML end-to-end with real responders rather than offline on curated benchmark images.

**Why BDA succeeded and RDA failed, mechanistically:** BDA's unit of analysis is a building polygon — a small, localized region, so modest misalignment between the footprint polygon and the actual building in the photo doesn't change much (the whole building is still mostly inside the box). RDA's unit is a thin road centerline; the model was trained assuming the line sits exactly on the road's center. In real field data, the road line map (OpenStreetMap) often doesn't line up precisely with the photographed road, so even small misalignment pushes the "road" sampling onto sidewalks, grass, or medians — producing bogus "damage" predictions. Additional failure modes: shadows and dark asphalt confusing the model, and vegetation/construction obscuring the actual road surface.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new evidence:** First evaluation of an operational sUAS damage-assessment ML system across a large, multi-agency, two-state field exercise rather than a handful of disaster deployments — expanding CLARKE's tested orthomosaic count from 4 to 13 and showing it generalizes geographically for buildings (Florida → Missouri, a terrain type explicitly excluded from training data).
- **Supported finding:** BDA is reasonably mature for operational use (good F1, no manual spatial correction needed, fast inference ~3.5 min) — this is backed by quantitative confusion-matrix/F1 results, modulo caveats below.
- **Supported finding:** RDA is not ready — this is strongly supported since predicted damage rates (35%) vastly exceed ground-truth baseline (~5%), a stark, hard-to-dispute gap.
- **More speculative/qualitative contributions (practitioner "lessons learned," not rigorously measured):** sUAS+ML is becoming operationally normalized; flight timing (midday/overcast) can reduce illumination-driven errors; exercises are good for testing operational pipelines but poor for testing ML generalization because the imagery lacks real damage and skews toward commercial/urban areas; wireless upload (not inference) is the real bottleneck, motivating edge computing; and there are legal/ethical liability risks ("negligent misrepresentation") if ML output is distributed without expert human oversight. These are argued qualitatively from exercise observations, not measured with controlled experiments.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **No actual disaster damage in the test imagery (stated).** Only 31/320 buildings (9.7%) and ~5% of road length had real damage; the exercise targeted commercial/industrial/rural areas, not the residential/damaged areas typical of real deployments. The authors explicitly say this makes CURSE'26 "not a realistic test of the ML model" for typical damage scenarios.
- **Evaluation metric limitations due to class imbalance (stated).** Because "no damage" dominates, the authors could only use Micro F1, not Macro F1 (used in their prior CRASAR-U-DROIDs paper), making the 0.834 score not directly comparable to earlier reported results, and the full JDS class spectrum couldn't be evaluated.
- **Ground truth labeled by the authors themselves (inferred).** The paper states ground truth was collected by the authors who built the training dataset — no independent annotators or inter-rater reliability reported, raising potential label bias/circularity risk.
- **No comparison baselines (inferred).** No alternative models or ablations (e.g., different architectures, with/without spatial-alignment correction) are tested; conclusions about *why* RDA fails (misalignment vs. illumination vs. occlusion) are qualitative/visual rather than ablated quantitatively.
- **Small sample size (inferred).** Only 9 orthomosaics / 7 teams, with road lengths as short as 0.24 km for some missions — results, especially the 80% "damage" outlier on mission 3, could be dominated by a few unusual cases rather than a robust trend.
- **Inference-time hardware caveat (stated).** The reported 3.5-minute inference time used a high-end desktop GPU (RTX 4090) and is only "expected" (not verified) to transfer to the laptops actually used in the field.
- **Upload/bandwidth bottleneck claims are not measured (stated).** The paper says upload took "on the order of 1 hour" consistent with a past event (Hurricane Ian) but explicitly states "specific upload times were not recorded" during CURSE'26 — this key claim behind the "edge computing is needed" finding is anecdotal, not measured in this exercise.
- **Exercise conditions were artificially favorable (stated).** Wireless infrastructure was intact (unlike real disasters), so even the bottleneck observed likely understates real-world severity, and performance findings may not generalize to degraded-network conditions.
- **Building footprint data errors conflated with model errors (stated).** At least one failure mode (a large apartment complex represented as a single polygon) is attributed to the Microsoft Building Footprints dataset rather than the model, meaning the correctness evaluation is confounded by third-party map data quality, which was not controlled for.
- **Geographic transfer claim is tentative (stated).** The authors explicitly caveat that "claims of complete transfer cannot be made" since the full JDS class set wasn't evaluated in Missouri — the positive transfer finding is based on the absence of "obvious or persistent failure," not a rigorous statistical transfer analysis.
