# PRISM: Learning Realistic Depth via Physics-Grounded Noise Disentanglement with Semantic-Geometric Collaboration

**Authors:** Xiujian Liang, Jiacheng Liu, Mingyang Sun, Qichen He, Anda Cheng, Cewu Lu, Jianhua Sun

**arXiv:** [2610.29432](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29432)

**Categories:** cs.RO

**Submitted:** 2026-10-21

**Relevance:** 7/10 — matched: Metric and temporally-consistent monocular depth

## TL;DR
PRISM tackles the sim-to-real depth gap by splitting depth sensor noise into two physically distinct failure modes — total signal loss ("sensing invalidation," e.g. from mirrors) and biased-but-present readings ("measurement inaccuracy," e.g. from glass/translucent surfaces) — instead of modeling noise as one monolithic random process. A frozen 3D vision foundation model supplies material/semantic cues that drive a mask-predicting network and a diffusion-based residual generator, trained with a custom loss designed for the extreme rarity of invalidation pixels. On their benchmarks and real robot manipulation tasks, this disentangled approach beats prior analytical and generative noise-simulation baselines, reportedly lifting real-world task success from ~39% (clean sim depth) to 92.1%.

## First-principles breakdown

**The underlying problem.** Robots are often trained in simulation because it's cheap and safe, then deployed on real hardware. Simulated depth cameras produce geometrically perfect depth maps, but real depth sensors (e.g., RealSense stereo cameras) are messy: they produce holes (zero/NaN values) where surfaces are shiny or black, and subtly wrong values where surfaces are transparent or translucent. A policy trained only on clean simulated depth gets confused by this real-world corruption — this is the "sim-to-real gap" for depth perception specifically (separate from the more commonly discussed RGB appearance gap).

**Why prior fixes fall short.** Two existing strategies exist:
1. *Explicit analytical modeling* — hand-code the physics of light transport (ray tracing, procedural noise injection) to simulate what a real sensor would see. This requires detailed sensor specs that are often proprietary, and is computationally heavy or too rigid to generalize across surfaces/sensors.
2. *Implicit data-driven modeling* — train a generative model (GAN, diffusion) directly on pairs of (clean, real) depth to learn the noise distribution end-to-end.

The paper's key diagnosis: both treat "noise" as one undifferentiated statistical phenomenon. But physically, a mirror causing total sensor blindness and a glass pane causing a biased-but-present reading are different mechanisms with different statistics (binary dropout vs. continuous bias). Lumping them together causes generative models to either hallucinate noise in clean, easy regions or fail to predict where total voids will occur — because the model has to learn one distribution that's actually a mixture of two very different ones.

**PRISM's mechanism, step by step.** The pipeline is "Reason → Disentangle → Generate," implemented as three modules:

1. *Semantic-Physics Reasoner (SPR)*: Feeds the RGB image through a frozen, pretrained 3D vision foundation model (e.g. MoGe2 — a model originally built for monocular depth/geometry estimation, not noise modeling). The intuition: these models have implicitly learned to recognize materials and surface types (metal, glass, cloth) as a side effect of learning geometry, so their internal features act as a cheap "this looks reflective/transparent" signal without any extra labels. SPR pools these features into a single embedding and converts it into per-decoder-layer "attention vectors" that will modulate later stages.

2. *Bimodal Noise Disentangler (BND)*: A CNN encoder-decoder takes the concatenated RGB + simulated-depth image and predicts, for every pixel, the probability it will be an "invalidation" (total dropout) region. The decoder is "semantic-prompted": at each upsampling stage, the feature map is multiplied (not just concatenated) by the SPR's attention vector for that scale — essentially letting the physics/material prior gate which regions get flagged as likely to fail. The output is a binary-ish mask M̂ over the image.

3. *Noise Residual Generator (NRG)*: For the pixels NOT flagged as invalidated, a diffusion model (built on Stable Diffusion plus a ControlNet adapter, conditioned on the normalized simulated depth and the SPR semantic embedding) generates a realistic residual error map R̂ to add to the clean depth. Critically, the training loss for this diffusion model is masked by (1 − M̂), i.e., it is never asked to generate residuals inside holes — this division of labor prevents the generator from having to represent both "signal present but wrong" and "signal absent" in one output space.

The final synthetic depth is: zero out depth where the mask says "invalid," otherwise add the generated residual to the clean simulated depth (Eq. 1).

**Training challenge and fix — H-PPS.** Invalidation pixels are rare (often <10% of the image), so a naive classifier would just learn to always predict "valid" and still get low loss (class imbalance). Their fix, Hierarchical Positive-Prioritized Supervision, combines: (a) a positive-weighted binary cross-entropy that upweights the rare positive (invalid) pixels by 3x, applied at multiple decoder resolutions; (b) a Dice loss encouraging compact, shape-consistent mask predictions; (c) "Positive-Preserving Dynamic Hard Mining" (PP-DHM) — unlike standard hard-negative mining (which can discard rare positive signal), this always keeps all positive pixels in the loss and only prunes the easiest negative (background) pixels, with the fraction of negatives kept shrinking over training (0.25 → 0.1) so the model first learns broad context, then sharpens on boundary pixels.

**Downstream use.** PRISM is used offline: take a large simulated manipulation dataset (collected via MimicGen), run every clean depth frame through PRISM to inject realistic artifacts, then train a depth-aware imitation-learning policy (modified ResNet dual-stream encoder for RGB+depth) on this "corrupted" dataset. The hope is the policy learns to be robust to real sensor artifacts purely from offline-augmented training data, with zero extra computation or adaptation at deployment/inference time.

## Key insights

- **Core novel idea (claimed and reasonably well-supported by ablations):** reframing depth sensor noise as two physically distinct modalities (invalidation vs. inaccuracy) rather than one monolithic distribution, and showing via ablation (Table 15 / Fig. 6) that removing the disentanglement (w/o BND, "Monolithic") hurts both overall MAE and robot success rate — supporting the claim that disentangling the two noise types is causally useful, not just a cosmetic framing.
- **Using a frozen 3D vision foundation model as a "physics prior" for noise synthesis is a genuinely novel repurposing** — these models (MoGe2, DepthAnythingV2) were built for geometry estimation, and the paper's backbone-comparison ablation (Fig. 7 / Table 16) shows 3D-geometry-pretrained models noticeably outperform generic ViTs (DINOv2) and CNNs for this noise-prediction task, suggesting these models do encode transferable material/surface cues beyond their original purpose. This is a useful empirical finding independent of the rest of the architecture.
- **H-PPS's recall-first dynamic mining strategy is a sensible, somewhat generalizable idea** for any segmentation task with sparse positive classes (not unique to depth) — the ablation (Table 17/18) shows each component contributes incrementally, and that there's a real precision/recall trade-off curve for the positive-class weight, with a stated optimum at wpos=3.0.
- **The headline 92.1% real-robot success rate and the jump from 38.9% (clean sim depth) is the paper's strongest practical claim**, but it rests on their own task suite, policies, and sim setup — it demonstrates that *some* noise-aware augmentation helps a lot (prior baselines like Stable-Sim2Real already get 89.1%), and PRISM's improvement over the next-best generative baseline is a comparatively modest ~3 absolute points (92.1% vs 89.1% in Table 4) — much smaller than the paper's framing emphasizes, since most of the gain comes from "any noise modeling beats clean sim" rather than from PRISM's specific disentanglement.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Fixed sensor family, limited generalization** (stated): the authors explicitly note PRISM is trained per sensor family and degrades under different sensing principles (ToF, LiDAR), requiring future adaptation modules.
- **No temporal consistency** (stated): noise is synthesized per-frame independently, causing potential flicker across frames in dynamic scenes — acknowledged as an open problem, not solved here.
- **Only intrinsic, surface-correlated noise modeled** (stated): interaction-induced noise (camera shake, multi-camera crosstalk) and extreme optical environments (fog, rain, sandstorms) are explicitly out of scope.
- **Failure modes acknowledged** (stated): semantic ambiguity in the frozen VFM (e.g., mirror misread as a window) propagates to wrong invalidation masks; ultra-thin structures below VFM patch resolution are over-smoothed.
- **Improvement over best prior baseline is much smaller than the headline number suggests** (inferred): the 92.1% average success rate is only ~3 points above Stable-Sim2Real's 89.1% (Table 4), and only ~1.7 points above Stable-Sim2Real's 90.8% on the RISE-only comparison (Table 3) — the paper's framing ("53.2% absolute improvement over raw simulation") compares against a weak clean-sim baseline rather than the strongest prior noise-modeling baseline, which somewhat inflates the perceived contribution.
- **All quantitative benchmarks are on one in-house dataset (ByteCameraDepth) plus one small zero-shot test (NYU-Depth-v2)** (inferred): fidelity numbers and the "SOTA" claim rest on a dataset collected by overlapping authors' prior work, and the only cross-domain generalization check is a single dataset/sensor (Kinect) — broader sensor diversity (ToF, different stereo cameras, outdoor conditions) is untested, which is consistent with the stated "fixed sensor family" limitation.
- **Downstream manipulation tasks are a custom 6-task suite on two robot platforms designed/selected by the authors** (inferred): success-rate comparisons, while real-world (not simulation-only), are not benchmarked against any external/public manipulation benchmark, leaving room for task selection or tuning advantages favoring PRISM.
- **Baselines for depth-noise synthesis may not be the most current/strongest available** (inferred): GazeboDR (2018) and DepthSynth (2017) are relatively old heuristic/analytical methods included alongside more recent generative baselines (Stable-Sim2Real, DCL-Depth); their inclusion widens the apparent gap over "monolithic" methods, but the real comparison that matters (PRISM vs. Stable-Sim2Real, the strongest baseline) shows a much narrower margin, as noted above.
- **Compute/latency cost of the SPR+NRG pipeline is non-trivial** (inferred from Table 16: MoGe2-Large backbone is 350M params, 52ms inference vs. 12ms for ResNet) — since this is used only offline for data augmentation, this is not necessarily a deployment problem, but the paper does not report the end-to-end cost/time of generating a large-scale enhanced dataset, which matters for reproducibility and practical adoption.
- **No statistical significance testing reported** beyond mean ± std over 3 seeds/20 trials per task (inferred) — real-robot success-rate differences of a few percentage points (e.g., 92.5% vs 90.8% in Table 3) are based on small trial counts (20 trials × 3 seeds = 60 total per task), which is a fairly standard but still limited sample size for robotics evaluation, and no confidence intervals or significance tests are given to support claims of PRISM being reliably better rather than within noise.
