# On Improving Faithfulness of Podcasts from Documents

**Authors:** Soumya Dutta, Tejas Indulal Dhamecha, Pannaga Shivaswamy

**arXiv:** [2607.21961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21961)

**Categories:** cs.CL

**Submitted:** 2026-07-24

**Relevance:** 6/10 — matched: Post-inference detection and repair of degenerate output

---

## TL;DR
This paper is the first systematic study of "faithfulness" (groundedness to source documents) in LLM-generated multi-speaker podcast transcripts, showing that even GPT-4o frequently produces ungrounded conversational turns despite fluent output. The authors build a 1,500-document, five-domain benchmark, a turn-level LLM-as-a-judge evaluator validated against human ratings, and a mitigation method called catch-n-repair that detects and rewrites unfaithful turns during generation. Catch-n-repair yields consistent faithfulness gains (roughly 5–10% relative) across five generator LLMs, in-domain and out-of-domain, with minimal loss in content coverage.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** LLMs like NotebookLM can turn a document into a podcast-style dialogue between a "host" and an "expert." This is different from summarization or QA because podcasts are long, multi-turn, multi-speaker, and conversational — a model can wander off-script (e.g., mentioning ChatGPT while narrating the 2017 "Attention Is All You Need" paper, which didn't exist yet). "Faithfulness" here means *grounded in the specific source document*, not "factually true in the real world" — a statement can be true and still unfaithful if the document never said it. Prior faithfulness research (summarization, QA) evaluates whole short outputs; nobody had studied grounding at the level of individual turns in long, evolving multi-speaker transcripts, where one hallucinated turn can be surrounded by otherwise-faithful ones.

**Step 1: Building a benchmark.** They collect 1,520 documents across five domains (academic, legal, policy, financial, medical), generate transcripts with five different LLMs (GPT-4o, LLaMA-3.3-70B, Qwen2.5 at 14B/32B/72B) using a fixed host/expert turn-taking format, and need a way to score how grounded each turn is.

**Step 2: Turn-level LLM-as-a-judge.** Naive metrics like lexical overlap (Knowledge-F1) fail because faithful content is often paraphrased, not copied, and penalize legitimate rewordings. So they prompt an LLM (GPT-4o) to rate each turn on a 1–5 Likert scale for how well it's supported by the document, then average turn scores into a document-level faithfulness score. They validate this judge against 328 human-annotated turns (10 annotators, Krippendorff's α = 0.69 agreement among humans) and find GPT-4o-as-judge correlates with humans (Pearson ~0.63) far better than lexical/embedding baselines (K-Precision, K-F1, BERTScore variants, FaithCritic, all ≤0.43). They also cross-check with Qwen2.5-72B as an alternate judge to rule out self-preference bias — both judges agree on relative model rankings.

**Step 3: Diagnosing the problem.** Using this judge, they find that GPT-4o is the most faithful generator (3.89/5) but still far from perfect; bigger open models don't reliably beat mid-sized ones; and faithfulness varies by domain (worst in academic, best in policy) — plausibly because models' pretraining knowledge "fills in" gaps differently depending on domain familiarity.

**Step 4: catch-n-repair mitigation.** This is the core method, done in three steps:
1. *Generate training data*: prompt LLaMA-3.1-70B to *deliberately* hallucinate (draw on world knowledge, tangential facts) when generating podcast turns, then label each turn faithful/unfaithful using the same Likert-judge protocol (score ≥4 = faithful). This yields ~17.5k faithful and ~9.4k unfaithful turns — an open, reproducible way to get supervision without proprietary APIs.
2. *Train a detector ("catch" model)*: fine-tune LLaMA-3.1-8B with LoRA (rank 8) as a binary faithful/unfaithful classifier conditioned on turn + document. They chose an LLM over an encoder (e.g., ModernBERT) because documents exceed ModernBERT's 8,192-token limit, while the LLM handles up to 32,768 tokens — and ablation confirms the LLM backbone beats the encoder (F1 79.9%/67.3% vs 76.1%/60.9% on faithful/unfaithful classes).
3. *Repair at generation time*: during sequential transcript generation, each new turn is passed through the catch model; if flagged unfaithful, the same generating LLM is re-prompted to rewrite that turn using only facts from the document (given the bad turn as a negative example), before continuing to the next turn — preventing hallucination from propagating downstream. Crucially, this requires no access to model internals (no logits/activations), so it works on closed-source APIs as well as open-weight models.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: the paper is (to its authors' claim, plausible given the related-work review) the first to frame and measure faithfulness *at the turn level* for long-form, multi-speaker, document-grounded generation — a gap between "summarization faithfulness" and "podcast quality" literatures, which had focused on fluency/engagement, not grounding.
- **Evidence supports**: the LLM-judge is meaningfully better correlated with human judgment than existing automatic metrics in this specific long-form multi-speaker setting, and this is checked against real human annotation, not just claimed.
- **Evidence supports**: catch-n-repair produces consistent, non-trivial faithfulness improvements (documented per-model, per-domain, ID vs OOD) with a plausible mechanism (turn-level detect-then-regenerate) and reasonably controlled comparisons (same generator model before/after, two independent judges, robustness check with an alternate judge model).
- **Claimed but only partially evidenced**: that gains generalize "robustly" to domain shift — the OOD set is only 20 documents, so relative percentage gains there (e.g., "12.8%") rest on a small sample and fairly wide error bars (±0.3–0.7 on 5-point scale).
- **Coverage tradeoff appears real but modest**: repair doesn't noticeably reduce topical coverage per their own reported metric, though coverage is scored by the same class of judge model, so this isn't fully independent evidence.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **English-only, single-domain-set documents** (stated): dataset is English; multilingual/cultural generalization untested.
- **catch classifier's imperfect accuracy** (stated): F1 is only 67.3% on the unfaithful class, meaning many hallucinations are likely missed or subtle paraphrases mislabeled as unfaithful — repair quality is bounded by this detector's imperfections.
- **Prompt sensitivity** (stated): faithfulness judgments and repair depend on specific prompts; different prompts could change results, and this wasn't stress-tested.
- **Misuse / residual hallucination** (stated): ungrounded generations can still occur after mitigation.
- **Text transcripts only, no audio** (stated): prosody/delivery interaction with grounding in true audio pipelines is unexamined.
- **Judge is also an LLM, and the same family (GPT-4o) is both a top-performing generator and a primary evaluator** (inferred): although they do cross-check with Qwen2.5-72B as judge and find consistent rankings, GPT-4o being simultaneously the best-scoring generator and the main judge raises a subtle self-preference/circularity concern that the cross-model check only partially addresses (the alternate judge is not itself validated against humans in the paper).
- **Small human validation and OOD sample sizes** (inferred): human study covers only 30 documents / 328 turns; OOD test set is only 20 documents — conclusions about generalization and evaluator reliability rest on fairly limited samples for a benchmark paper.
- **Training data for the catch model is synthetically induced hallucination from one specific model (LLaMA-3.1-70B) prompted to hallucinate on purpose** (inferred): this may not represent the natural distribution of hallucinations that arise from normal (non-adversarially-prompted) generation by other LLMs, potentially limiting how well the detector transfers to unprompted, naturally occurring ungroundedness in GPT-4o or Qwen outputs.
- **No comparison to other plausible baselines for mitigation** (inferred): the paper doesn't compare catch-n-repair against simpler baselines such as retrieval-augmented regeneration, self-consistency checks (e.g., SelfCheckGPT-style), or a single whole-transcript regeneration pass — so it's unclear how much of the gain is attributable to the turn-level design versus just "regenerate flagged content once."
- **Coverage evaluation shares the same judge-model methodology as faithfulness** (inferred): since both are LLM-as-judge scores from the same evaluator, the "minimal impact on coverage" claim is not an independent check and could share correlated biases with the faithfulness score.
