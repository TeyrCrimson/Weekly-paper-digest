# Reasoning Denoiser: Denoising Reasoning Traces for Hallucination Detection in Large Reasoning Models

**Authors:** Junlin Fang, Do Nguyen-Thanh, Xiaogang Xu, Zhen Fang, Sean Du

**arXiv:** [2607.22098](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22098)

**Categories:** cs.AI, cs.LG

**Submitted:** 2026-07-24

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: Post-inference detection and repair of degenerate output

---

## TL;DR
LRM (large reasoning model) reasoning traces contain "irrelevant" and "repetitive" steps that dilute hallucination-detection signals, and neither confidence scores nor raw embedding similarity can reliably tell these apart from informative steps. The paper proposes REDE, which uses the attention the final-answer token pays to each step (a free, annotation-free signal) to train a small projection network that pulls informative steps together and pushes noisy steps apart in embedding space, so a simple k-NN distance can then filter them out before running any existing hallucination detector. Across four benchmarks and two LRM families, feeding detectors the REDE-filtered trace instead of the raw trace consistently and substantially improves AUROC (e.g., 68.6→87.3 on TruthfulQA with one detector).

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Modern "large reasoning models" (OpenAI o-series, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3, etc.) don't just answer a question — they first emit a long chain-of-thought "reasoning trace" (a sequence of steps c₁…c_K) before the final answer. People want to detect whether the final answer is a hallucination (i.e., wrong/unsupported) by looking at signals inside this trace — e.g., train a probe on hidden states, or measure token-level uncertainty. The intuition is that a model's internal reasoning should carry clues about its own confidence/correctness.

**Why prior approaches fail.** The trace is long and messy. The authors categorize the noise into two types:
- *Irrelevant steps*: tangents that don't bear on the answer at all.
- *Repetitive steps*: the model re-derives something it already computed; the earlier occurrence is now redundant because a later step covers the same ground.

Both types dilute the "true" signal a hallucination detector needs. Two obvious fixes don't work:
1. **Confidence-based filtering** (using the model's own token probability/certainty as a proxy for step usefulness) — the distributions of confidence scores for noisy vs. informative steps overlap heavily (shown empirically in Fig 1b), so thresholding confidence can't separate them. Confidence measures "how sure the model is about what it's saying," not "how useful this step is for judging the final answer's truthfulness" — a different quantity.
2. **Naive embedding similarity (k-NN in raw embedding space)** — irrelevant steps often do look like outliers geometrically, so k-NN can catch some of them. But repetitive steps are *semantically similar* to the informative steps they duplicate, so they sit right next to informative steps in raw embedding space and can't be separated by simple distance (Fig 1c).

**The core insight the paper builds on:** the *attention* the model's final-answer token pays back to each earlier reasoning step (a value already computed inside the transformer, needing no extra labels) turns out to correlate with how useful that step actually was. Concretely, they sum the softmax attention weights (Eq. 1: standard scaled dot-product attention, q·k/√d, softmaxed over all prior tokens) from the final answer token to all tokens belonging to a given step, giving a per-step score s_i. Empirically: irrelevant steps get low attention (weakly connected to the answer), repetitive steps also get low attention (because the model's attention concentrates on the *later*, non-redundant restatement, not the earlier duplicate), while genuinely informative steps get high attention (mean 6.5e-3 vs 2.1e-3 irrelevant vs 3.3e-3 repetitive on their annotated AIME set).

**Why not just threshold on attention directly then?** Two reasons given: (1) it requires computing attention at test time for every sample — extra inference cost; (2) per-instance attention is noisy/unstable across varying trace lengths and structures (citing prior "attention is not explanation" literature), so raw attention alone is not a reliable enough test-time filter (confirmed empirically — direct attention filtering underperforms their learned method, 80.51 vs 87.32 AUROC).

**REDE's mechanism, step by step:**
1. *Step embedding*: for each step, aggregate its tokens' hidden states into one vector using perplexity-weighted averaging (Eq. 2) — tokens the model found less predictable (higher perplexity = 1/probability) get more weight, since surprising tokens tend to carry more semantic content than filler tokens.
2. *Proxy labels via attention*: for each training trace, rank steps by attention score s_i; take the top-ρ% as a proxy "informative" set 𝒯 and bottom-ρ% as proxy "noisy" set ℬ. This is automatic — no human labels needed.
3. *Learn a projection*: train a small network f_φ (the underlying LRM stays frozen) that maps raw step embeddings into a new space z_i = f_φ(e_i), optimized with a three-part contrastive loss (Eq. 3):
   - **Compact**: pull all pairs within 𝒯 (informative) close together (maximize cosine similarity).
   - **Disperse**: keep pairs within ℬ (noisy) from clustering together — so noisy steps stay individually distinguishable as scattered outliers rather than forming their own dense blob (which would defeat k-NN filtering).
   - **Separate**: push 𝒯 and ℬ apart from each other.
4. *Inference-time filtering*: for a new (test) trace, no attention computation is needed at all — just embed each step, project through the trained f_φ, then compute each step's k-nearest-neighbor cosine distance to other steps in the *same* trace. Remove the top-ζ% steps with the largest k-NN distance (i.e., the most "isolated" ones) as noise.
5. *Downstream*: the cleaned, shorter trace is fed into any existing hallucination detector (probing-based, uncertainty-based, verbalized-confidence-based, etc.) unchanged — REDE is a drop-in preprocessing/denoising module, detector-agnostic.

There is also a formal theoretical appendix arguing (under stated assumptions) that: (a) noisy steps provably reduce class separability (Fisher discriminant) of the trace representation; (b) if the learned space makes informative steps locally dense and noisy steps locally sparse, k-NN scores separate them with bounded error; (c) that filtering error bound translates into a bound on how much worse the filtered-trace detection risk can be relative to an oracle-filtered trace.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: using final-answer attention as an *automatic, annotation-free supervision signal* specifically to identify noisy reasoning steps for hallucination detection (rather than for generation efficiency/compression, which is what prior "step selection" work targeted). This reframing — noisy-step filtering as a prerequisite for hallucination detection, distinct from prior step-pruning work aimed at speeding up generation — is the paper's stated core contribution.
- **Detector-agnostic plug-in**: because REDE only touches the input trace (filtering, not the detector itself), it composes with four different detection paradigms (CCS, supervised probing, perplexity, verbalized certainty) and the paper shows gains across all four — this generality claim is directly supported by Table 2.
- **The "attention as supervision, not as direct filter" distinction** is a real and demonstrated finding: Table 4 shows learned projection + k-NN beats naive thresholding on raw attention scores (87.32 vs 80.51 AUROC on CCS), supporting the claim that turning a noisy per-instance signal into a stable learned embedding structure is what actually matters, not just having access to attention.
- **Best supported empirical claim**: filtering the trace (regardless of exact method) helps — Table 2's original-vs-filtered comparison, holding the downstream detector fixed, is a fairly clean ablation isolating REDE's contribution from detector choice, and shows consistent, large gains (e.g., +18.7 pts on TruthfulQA/CCS).
- **Weaker/less certain claim**: that REDE is the *best possible* way to do this filtering — the comparison against six alternative step-selection strategies (Table 3) shows REDE winning, but those alternatives were designed for a different objective (generation quality/efficiency), so the comparison somewhat stacks the deck in REDE's favor (this is acknowledged implicitly by the paper's own framing, not fully independent).
- **State-of-the-art claim (87.32% AUROC on TruthfulQA)** is supported within their own experimental setup and baseline set, but "state-of-the-art" is only as strong as the baseline suite and datasets tested — cannot be verified against baselines/papers not included in the comparison.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Correctness labels come from an LLM judge, not ground truth** (Qwen3-32B judging answer correctness) — `stated` (paper says "robustness to alternative labeling methods is verified in Appendix C.5" implying they know this is a potential weak point, but the main results all rely on one LLM judge).
- **Annotation of irrelevant/repetitive/informative steps for the motivating analysis (Fig 1, Fig 2, Fig 3) is itself done via an annotation process described only in an appendix not included in this text** — `inferred`: the core motivating evidence for "noisy steps hurt detection" rests on this annotation scheme, whose reliability/agreement (e.g., inter-annotator agreement) is not shown in the main text provided.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: results depend on ρ (proxy set percentile), ζ (drop ratio, tuned to peak at 70%), output dimension d′, and which layer embeddings are taken from — the drop ratio finding that "70% of steps are noisy" is a striking, possibly dataset/model-specific number; whether this generalizes to other LRMs/tasks is not fully addressed — `inferred` (ablations exist in Appendix B but their robustness across tasks isn't shown in the given main-text excerpts).
- **Comparison baselines for step-selection (Table 3) were designed for a different objective** (generation efficiency, not hallucination detection) — `stated` by the paper itself as the reason they underperform, but this also means the comparison may favor REDE somewhat unfairly since REDE was purpose-built for this task while baselines weren't.
- **Theoretical analysis relies on strong simplifying assumptions**: e.g., Assumption E.1's "ε-approximate label-uninformative perturbation," a linear-probe-only detector assumption (Assumption E.10), and Assumption E.4 assuming independent Bernoulli neighbor indicators — `stated` explicitly as assumptions, but they are strong idealizations (e.g., real detectors are not always linear probes; CCS and verbalized-certainty detectors used in experiments are not linear probes on pooled embeddings), so the formal guarantees may not directly apply to all the empirical detectors tested — `inferred` gap between theory scope and empirical scope.
- **No live/production deployment or adversarial robustness evaluation** — all evaluation is on standard offline benchmarks (TruthfulQA, MATH-family, CodeElo, MultiHopQA) with greedy decoding by default — `inferred`: no test under adversarial prompts, distribution shift beyond the cross-dataset transfer experiment, or sampling-based (non-greedy) generation robustness in the main text (though sampling robustness is claimed to be addressed in an appendix not shown here).
- **Compute/latency overhead of training the projection network per dataset/model** is mentioned as "analyzed in Appendix A.7" but not shown in the given text — `inferred` caveat: readers can't verify from this excerpt whether the extra training step is cheap or costly relative to baselines.
- **Scale of "large" LRMs tested tops out at 32B** (Qwen3-32B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B) — generalization to frontier-scale reasoning models (e.g., 100B+ or proprietary o-series models) is not addressed in the paper.
- **The paper doesn't report effect on end-task accuracy** in the main text excerpt (only mentions it's covered in an appendix) — i.e., whether removing 70% of steps could hurt the model's own task performance if the filtered trace were also used for the model's answer generation (not just for post-hoc detection) is not addressed in the main text provided.
