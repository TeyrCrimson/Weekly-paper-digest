# Efficacy of Monocular Depth Estimation in Lunar Environments

**Authors:** Michael W. Lanighan, Kenji Brameld, Seth Gee

**arXiv:** [2610.38080](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38080)

**Categories:** cs.RO, cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-10-27

**Relevance:** 7/10 — matched: Metric and temporally-consistent monocular depth

---

## TL;DR
This paper tests whether modern monocular/multi-view depth estimation networks — trained entirely on terrestrial images — can still produce usable 3D reconstructions of the lunar surface, using a lab-based stereo dataset (POLAR) with LiDAR ground truth plus real Apollo 17 photos for qualitative checks. ViT-backbone methods (MoGe-2, OmniVGGT, VGGT) generalize surprisingly well to the textureless, harshly-lit lunar terrain and beat a lunar-tuned traditional stereogrammetry baseline (NASA's Ames Stereo Pipeline), while a diffusion-based method (Marigold) fails badly. The authors argue this supports using single-camera depth estimation for bandwidth-constrained lunar teleoperation, in place of or alongside stereo-pair downlinks.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Remote robot operators (on the ISS today, and on the Moon under Artemis) currently work from flat 2D camera feeds and must mentally reconstruct 3D spatial relationships — a slow, high-cognitive-load, error-prone skill. Giving operators actual 3D reconstructions would help, but building 3D models traditionally requires *stereo pairs* (two cameras with known offset, matched via feature correspondence — "stereogrammetry"). On the Moon, bandwidth and downlink windows will be scarcer than even ISS/LEO operations, so sending two camera streams per scene is expensive. Monocular depth estimation (MDE) — inferring a 3D depth map from a *single* 2D image using a trained neural network — would halve bandwidth needs, but all the leading MDE models are trained on massive terrestrial image datasets (cities, rooms, nature). Nobody knew whether that learned "depth prior" transfers to the Moon's alien visual statistics: fine, mostly textureless regolith, extreme contrast lighting, lens flare, long shadows, and no familiar object priors (cars, buildings, horizon cues).

**Why this is hard to evaluate.** You need ground-truth depth to score accuracy, but you can't get LiDAR-verified ground truth on the actual Moon. The paper's fix is an "analog-to-field validation pipeline": first, quantitatively score against a lab-simulated lunar terrain with real LiDAR truth (NASA's POLAR dataset — staged terrains shot from 3 camera positions under varied lighting, including a deliberately flare-inducing angle); second, qualitatively sanity-check the best performers on actual Apollo 17 photographs (which have no depth ground truth but are the real article).

**The methods compared.**
- *Stereogrammetry baseline (ASP)*: the classical two-camera geometric approach, tuned specifically for lunar terrain (special matching algorithm, sub-pixel refinement, larger correlation window to catch rock/crater edges). This is the incumbent approach VIPER-style missions use.
- *Single-view MDE networks*: Marigold (a diffusion model that "denoises" toward a depth map — relies heavily on having seen similar scenes during training, i.e., a generative prior); Depth Anything V2 (distills a large teacher model into a smaller robust network using a ViT/DINOv2 backbone — DINOv2 is a self-supervised vision backbone trained to extract general-purpose visual features without labels); MoGe-2 (DINOv2 backbone with separate decoder heads for relative shape and for metric scale).
- *Multi-view MDE networks* (here tested single-image/"blind," withholding their calibration/multi-image inputs): VGGT, MapAnything, OmniVGGT — all variants of a DINOv2-backbone transformer that attends across both within-image patches and across multiple views, with task-specific output heads for depth, pose/camera parameters, and 3D points.

**The metric: SILog (Scale-Invariant Logarithmic error).** Many MDE models output only *relative* depth (correct shape, but wrong or absent absolute scale — e.g., "twice as far" might be right even if the actual meters are off). A naive error metric would unfairly punish a perfectly-shaped-but-wrongly-scaled reconstruction. SILog solves this by taking the log of predicted vs. true depth per pixel, computing the best global scale factor that aligns the two, and then measuring the *variance* of what's left over — i.e., how much the shape itself is wrong, independent of scale. The paper then converts this log-space variance back into an intuitive "1σ/2σ multiplicative error factor" (e.g., SILog=2.5 means ~68% of pixels are within ±3% of true relative depth).

**What they found.** ViT-backbone models (MoGe-2 best, then OmniVGGT, then VGGT) reconstructed lunar terrain structure accurately (SILog ~2–4, i.e., 2σ error factors near 1.05–1.1×) despite never seeing lunar imagery in training. Marigold (diffusion) and the stereo baseline (ASP) both performed far worse, with ASP essentially breaking down (SILog ~100+) on textureless/flare-affected regions because it depends on matching visual features across two images — features that barren regolith and glare simply don't provide. Qualitatively, the top 3 ViT models also produced coherent, interpretable 3D reconstructions of real Apollo 17 photos (astronaut, lunar rover), though with some foreground/background bleeding errors (e.g., misjudging the rover seat's distance from the rear wheel).

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new finding**: this is (per the paper) among the first systematic, ground-truth-backed tests of whether terrestrial-trained MDE generalizes to lunar imagery, and the evidence (SILog scores against LiDAR truth across multiple terrains/lighting conditions) directly supports the claim that ViT/DINOv2-backbone MDE models transfer surprisingly well — including to lens flare and deep shadow, which the authors call a "surprising" robustness result.
- **Supported by evidence**: traditional stereogrammetry (ASP), even when lunar-tuned, is more brittle than MDE on textureless/glare-affected terrain in this dataset — a meaningful result since ASP is the real incumbent technique behind missions like VIPER.
- **Partially supported / more speculative**: the practical pitch that MDE halves bandwidth needs for lunar teleoperation is a reasonable implication of "single image in, 3D out," but the paper doesn't actually measure bandwidth, latency, or operator task performance — that's an inferred downstream benefit, not something tested.
- **Not really an insight, a caveat dressed as a finding**: the diffusion model's poor performance is explained post-hoc (reliance on "generative priors tied to terrestrial training data") — plausible, but it's a single data point (one diffusion model) being used to support a general claim about diffusion-based MDE.
- Scale recovery remains unsolved: none of the MDE models reliably output usable absolute/metric depth, so real deployment would still need some scale-anchoring trick (e.g., known robot/rover part dimensions in-frame) — the authors flag this as future work, not a solved problem.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **No real lunar ground truth** — quantitative evaluation uses a lab-simulated analog terrain (POLAR), not actual lunar surface data; Apollo imagery has no ground truth at all, so that portion is purely qualitative/subjective visual inspection. (stated)
- **Small, curated image sample** — the authors explicitly selected "representative" POLAR scenes and only 2 Apollo 17 images, chosen because they expected these to be challenging; this is a hand-picked, very small test set, not a broad benchmark. (stated/inferred — selection explicitly described, but sample size and cherry-picking risk aren't discussed as a limitation by the authors themselves)
- **Scale/metric accuracy is unresolved** — most models don't output reliable absolute depth, and the paper's own chosen metric (SILog) is scale-invariant specifically to sidestep this problem, meaning the headline "high-fidelity" results say nothing about whether distances are usable without further scale anchoring. (stated)
- **Single-view-only testing of multi-view models** — VGGT, MapAnything, and OmniVGGT are designed to exploit multiple images/calibration, but the paper deliberately withheld those inputs to test "blind" single-image performance; results may understate what these models can actually do, and the multi-view fusion capability most relevant to bandwidth tradeoffs is left as unquantified future work. (stated)
- **MapAnything and Depth Anything V2 underperform sharply and inconsistently** (e.g., MapAnything SILog jumps from ~2 to ~21 across terrains) with no ablation or explanation offered beyond raw numbers — no analysis of why these particular architectures are unstable across conditions. (inferred)
- **No end-to-end operator/task validation** — despite motivating the work with operator cognitive load and mission teleoperation, the study only measures depth-map accuracy, not whether this actually improves real operator situational awareness, task completion, or measured bandwidth savings. (inferred)
- **No statistical/significance analysis or multiple-run variance reported** — SILog values are given as single point estimates (with std dev across terrains in Table III, but not per-run model variance), so it's unclear how robust e.g. the MoGe-2 vs. OmniVGGT ranking is to noise. (inferred)
- **Potential conflict of interest / framing risk** — the work is funded by a NASA contract to a space-robotics company with an evident interest in favorable results for the camera/bandwidth approach they advocate; this doesn't invalidate the data but is worth noting given the applied, mission-pitch framing of the conclusion. (inferred)
- **Authors themselves note** lunar deployment would need new terrain datasets to fully quantify multi-view fusion errors, and that scale recovery would require additional downstream engineering (e.g., anchoring to known rover part sizes) — both explicitly flagged as open problems rather than solved. (stated)
