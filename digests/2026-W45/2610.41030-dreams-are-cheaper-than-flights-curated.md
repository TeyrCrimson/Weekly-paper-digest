# Dreams Are Cheaper Than Flights: Curated World-Model Data Improves Aerial Detection

**Authors:** Rugved Katole, Vedant Patil, Jenna Kline, Christopher Stewart

**arXiv:** [2610.41030](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41030)

**Categories:** cs.CV, cs.AI

**Submitted:** 2026-10-29

**Relevance:** 7/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
The authors seed an open video world model (Cosmos-Predict1-7B) with real drone footage to generate synthetic training frames for wildlife and urban aerial object detectors, then test the resulting detectors on held-out real field video. Adding curated synthetic frames to real data raises mAP@50–95 by 4.3–33.5% depending on dataset, and training on synthetic frames alone even beats real-only training on the wildlife dataset. Despite rejecting an estimated 87.5% of generated clips, the compute cost (~$1,100) is still cheaper than the field labor (~$3,200+) needed to collect a comparably-sized real dataset.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Training good object detectors for aerial/drone-based perception (e.g., spotting zebras, giraffes, vehicles from low-altitude drone video) requires lots of labeled real-world video. Collecting that video is expensive: it needs permits, flight-safety compliance, travel to remote sites, and careful handling so as not to disturb wildlife. A concrete example cited: one wildlife dataset (MMLA) took over 150 person-hours across two continents to gather just 37 videos. Small datasets like this cause models to generalize poorly to new conditions.

**Why prior approaches fall short.** The classic fix for data scarcity in robotics is simulation: render a synthetic environment and "domain-randomize" its appearance (textures, lighting, etc.) until a detector trained on it transfers to reality. This requires a simulator capable of rendering the target objects realistically — straightforward for, say, rigid factory parts, but very hard for live animals moving naturally through unstructured terrain. Separately, generative image models (diffusion models) have been used to synthesize extra training images, and "world models" (models that predict how a video continues from a few frames) have been explored for simulating interactive environments. But nobody had rigorously measured whether such generated data, seeded directly from real field footage, could substitute for real data in a deployed field detector, and whether it's actually cost-effective given that generative models produce flawed output much of the time.

**This paper's mechanism, step by step:**
1. **Seeding:** Take 9 consecutive real frames from training footage (never from the held-out test video) plus a short text description of the scene.
2. **Generation:** Feed these into Cosmos-Predict1-7B Video2World, an off-the-shelf 7B-parameter world model, which extrapolates roughly 120 new frames continuing that scene. Because the camera, altitude, terrain, and lighting are fixed by the real seed frames, the model only has to invent *how the scene plays out* (animal movement, etc.), not *what the world looks like* — this sidesteps the classic "reality gap" that rendered simulators struggle with, since appearance is inherited directly from the real seed rather than hand-tuned.
3. **Curation (rejection sampling):** A human expert watches every generated clip and throws out any with visible artifacts — duplicated heads, merging/vanishing bodies, physically impossible motion. Only accepted clips get bounding-box annotations and enter the training set. The paper frames this formally: if a clip takes T GPU-seconds to generate and is accepted with probability p, the *expected* compute cost per usable clip is T/p, and the fraction of total compute wasted on rejected clips is (1−p). This matters because acceleration techniques (faster diffusion samplers) reduce T but do nothing about p — the waste problem is separate from the speed problem.
4. **Training & evaluation:** Detectors (YOLO11m for the wildlife dataset MMLA, YOLOv8m for the urban dataset VisDrone) are trained with various mixes of real and synthetic frames, holding the detector architecture and hyperparameters fixed so the training data is the only variable. Evaluation is always on held-out real video from the same drones (not used for seeding), so there's no leakage between generation and testing.
5. **Cost comparison:** They tally GPU-hours priced at AWS/Azure on-demand H100 rates and compare that total (including wasted compute on rejected clips) against estimated field-labor cost (hourly wage × person-hours) for collecting the equivalent real data.

## Key insights

- **Core novel contribution:** A concrete "field-to-simulation-to-field" protocol — seed a general-purpose video world model with real sensor footage, curate outputs via expert rejection sampling, and quantify both the accuracy benefit and the dollar cost including waste. This end-to-end cost/accuracy accounting (not just "synthetic data helps") appears to be the paper's distinguishing move.
- **Evidence supports:** On MMLA (wildlife), adding curated synthetic frames to real data improves mAP@50–95 by 4.3%, and training on synthetic-only data improves it by 10.3% over real-only, with gains across all three classes (zebra, onager, giraffe). On VisDrone (urban), curated additions yield up to 33.5% relative mAP@50–95 gain, concentrated in rarer vehicle classes (truck, van). The authors offer a plausible (not proven) three-part explanation for why synthetic-only sometimes beats real-only: larger/more diverse pool, less motion blur/sensor noise in generated frames, and single-annotator labeling consistency versus multi-annotator real labels — but they explicitly state "single runs cannot separate" these factors, so this remains a hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism.
- **Cost claim supported by their own accounting:** Even wasting ~87.5% of compute on rejected clips, the synthetic route costs ~$1,100 (GPU) + ~$164 (expert review) versus >$3,200 in field labor alone for comparable real data — roughly 2.5x cheaper, and ~20x cheaper if generation waste were eliminated. This is a calculation based on their specific cost assumptions (Oct. 2026 cloud GPU rates, median US wildlife biologist wage), not an independently verified market cost comparison.
- **Important qualifier the authors themselves highlight:** curation is not optional and must be checked per-class — on VisDrone, aggregate accuracy improves even with uncurated (rejected) data, but the rarest class (bus) collapses from AP 0.211 to 0.010 when synthetic data of either kind is added. This shows aggregate metrics can mask severe per-class degradation.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Single real-world dataset family, two domains only** (wildlife MMLA, urban VisDrone) — generalization to other sensor types, altitudes, or object classes is untested. *(inferred)*
- **High rejection rate (74.7–87.5%) reviewed by a single human expert** using subjective visual criteria (deformities, merging bodies) — no inter-rater reliability or systematic/automated rejection criteria reported. *(inferred, though the rejection numbers themselves are stated)*
- **Authors explicitly flag that curation is required and must be verified per-class**, since "a poorly rendered class can vanish unnoticed in aggregate accuracy" — demonstrated by the bus-class collapse on VisDrone. *(stated)*
- **Mechanism behind synthetic-only outperforming real-only is not isolated or ablated** — the paper lists three plausible contributing factors (pool size, image quality/blur, single-annotator consistency) but states directly that "single runs cannot separate" them; no controlled ablation varies one factor at a time. *(stated as a limitation of their own analysis)*
- **Cost comparison relies on several assumptions**: a single October 2026 cloud GPU on-demand price point, a median US wildlife-biologist hourly wage as a stand-in for the true field campaign cost, and exclusion of annotation costs (stated as excluded "because both routes need it") and of travel/lodging/equipment/permits from the field-cost side being only partially estimated. This makes the "2.5x–20x cheaper" headline sensitive to pricing assumptions not stress-tested in the paper. *(inferred, though the specific exclusions are stated)*
- **Detector and generation settings are not ablated**: one world-model (Cosmos-Predict1-7B) at default sampler settings, one seed length (9 frames), one detector architecture per dataset — no comparison against alternative world models, seed lengths, or acceleration/distillation methods mentioned as future work rather than tested. *(inferred/stated — authors list faster solvers/distillation as "future work," implying they did not test them)*
- **No comparison to classic domain-randomized simulation or other generative-augmentation baselines** (e.g., diffusion image augmentation) is run in this paper's experiments — those methods are only discussed in the related-work framing, not benchmarked head-to-head. *(inferred)*
- **Test videos are "held-out" but from the same drones/same sites/campaigns as training data** — this is good for controlling leakage but means the evaluation doesn't test transfer to genuinely new deployment sites, drones, or conditions beyond what's in the original dataset collection. *(inferred)*
- **Boxes/annotations for the pilot acceptance rate (p=0.125) on MMLA come from a 40-clip pilot**, a small sample size for estimating a cost-critical probability used to extrapolate the full $1,131 GPU cost figure. *(stated as "estimated from the pilot," but the small sample size as a reliability concern is inferred)*
