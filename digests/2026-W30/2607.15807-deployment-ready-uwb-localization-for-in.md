# Deployment-Ready UWB Localization for Industrial Ground Robots with Automatic Anchor Calibration and Terrain-Aware Fusion

**Authors:** Alexander Raab, Giulio Delama, Roland Jung, Stephan Weiss

**arXiv:** [2607.15807](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15807)

**Categories:** cs.RO

**Submitted:** 2026-07-17

**Relevance:** 6/10 — matched: Delay-compensated perception for mobile robots

---

## TL;DR
This paper presents an end-to-end UWB localization pipeline for industrial mobile robots that automatically calibrates anchor positions/biases from a single robot trajectory and fuses UWB ranges with wheel odometry via a terrain-aware Extended Kalman Filter, avoiding manual tuning. Tested on a commercial logistics AMR in a warehouse (indoor and indoor-outdoor transitions) and cross-validated on an independent forklift dataset, the bias-aware measurement model substantially improves both accuracy and filter consistency over a prior range-only formulation. The method also tolerates reduced anchor/tag counts and partial line-of-sight loss.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs) in warehouses/factories need localization that's cheaper than LiDAR/vision. UWB (Ultra-Wideband) radio ranging — measuring distance between fixed "anchors" and tags on the robot via time-of-flight — is a good candidate: cheap, works indoors and outdoors, GPS-denied-friendly. But two things block real deployments: (1) you must first know where each anchor physically is (calibration), which today is manual/slow/error-prone or needs extra equipment; (2) once you have ranges, you must fuse them with the robot's other sensors (wheel odometry) into a single pose estimate, and getting this fusion to be both *accurate* and *statistically consistent* (i.e., the filter's confidence estimates match its actual errors) typically requires heavy hand-tuning.

**Why prior approaches fall short.** Two-stage systems calibrate anchors offline (careful surveying or a separate optimization run) then localize online — clean but effort-heavy. Tightly-coupled systems (e.g., UWB+VIO) estimate calibration and pose jointly, which is more automatic but complex. For the fusion side, standard filters treat UWB ranges as ideal, noise-free distances. In reality, two-way-ranging radios have systematic errors: a constant offset (bias) per anchor-tag pair, plus a bias that scales with distance. Ignoring these biases causes the previous estimator (an existing "Manifold Error-State EKF", M-ESEKF, from the authors' earlier work) to produce inconsistent estimates — meaning the filter is overconfident and its reported uncertainty doesn't match reality (quantified by the NEES metric, ideally ≈1).

**This paper's mechanism, step by step:**
1. **Calibration stage.** Drive the robot along one short trajectory. Using the robot's existing pose estimates (from onboard LiDAR/odometry-based localization, treated as a "prior"), known tag positions on the robot, and the UWB ranges collected en route, a nonlinear least-squares solver (building on prior work UVIO/[4]) simultaneously estimates: each anchor's 3D position, and pairwise constant+range-dependent bias terms, each with an uncertainty (covariance). This is a single automated step — no manual surveying.
2. **Terrain-aware pose representation.** Instead of treating the robot as free in full 3D space (which needs artificial constraints to stay on the ground, requiring tuning), the robot's motion is modeled directly on a 2D "manifold" (surface) embedded in 3D — essentially, the pose is parameterized by (x, y, heading) plus a known/smooth ground-height function z=S(x,y). A "chart map" σ converts between the 3D surface point and the reduced 2D+heading state, so the filter naturally respects "robot stays on the floor" without hacky constraints, and still handles ramps.
3. **Bias-aware range measurement model.** The raw UWB range equation (distance between anchor and tag as reconstructed from the estimated pose) is extended: measured range = (range-dependent bias γ) × (true geometric distance) + (constant bias β). These β, γ per anchor-tag pair, plus anchor positions and tag offsets, become part of an "augmented sensor state," carried with their calibration-stage uncertainties into the filter.
4. **Schmidt-Kalman handling of weak observability.** A single scalar range measurement doesn't strongly constrain all these extra calibration parameters (they're "weakly observable" from generic driving trajectories). Rather than let the filter try to update (and likely mis-update) these parameters online — which can inject error — most are held fixed at their calibrated values ("Schmidt states"), but their uncertainty is still accounted for by inflating the measurement noise covariance used in the pose update (via a Jacobian-based propagation of the parameter covariance into measurement space). This is the key trick for getting both accuracy and honest uncertainty without online recalibration risk.
5. **Fusion.** All of this plugs into an existing modular EKF framework (MaRS) that fuses UWB ranges with wheel odometry (and could add GNSS etc.), updating only the relevant state/covariance sub-blocks per sensor for efficiency.

**Evaluation approach.** Real hardware tests, not simulation: a commercial AGILOX AMR with 3 UWB tags and 12 anchors in a real warehouse (indoor trajectory used only for calibration; a separate indoor+outdoor trajectory for testing generalization to unvisited areas), ground-truthed against the robot's own high-precision LiDAR map. A second, independent, pre-calibrated forklift dataset from a different site/team tests whether the *filter formulation alone* (not the calibration pipeline) transfers to another platform. Metrics: Absolute Trajectory Error (position/heading) for accuracy, and NEES for consistency.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new contribution:** integrating an existing single-shot UWB anchor-calibration method with an existing terrain-manifold EKF via a new bias-aware, Schmidt-Kalman-managed measurement model — this specific combination and the analytical adaptation (treating calibration uncertainty as inflated measurement noise rather than online-updated states) appears to be the paper's core novelty, not either piece alone.
- **Evidence supports:** the bias-aware model clearly beats the original range-only model on the same hardware — indoor mean NEES drops from 625.3 to 3.1, and max position/heading error drops from 0.744 m/11.23° to 0.131 m/4.81°. This is a solid, well-quantified within-system improvement.
- **Evidence for robustness claims is reasonably strong but narrower than framed:** the system tolerates fewer anchors/tags and partial LOS loss in the tested outdoor trial (specific anchor/tag subsets tried), and calibration error (~18 cm mean) compares favorably to a few cited prior UWB anchor-calibration numbers (22–25 cm, 11–21 cm, >40 cm for AMR).
- **Cross-platform "transferability" claim is more limited than the abstract suggests:** the forklift test only validates the *filter/measurement-model* portion (using externally pre-calibrated anchors and offline-derived biases), not the *end-to-end pipeline* including the automatic calibration stage. The paper itself acknowledges this ("does not re-evaluate the full deployment pipeline"). Improvement there was also only "modest."
- **Outdoor consistency is not actually solved:** absolute NEES values remain high in the outdoor trial even with the adapted model (e.g., 39.48 mean vs. ideal ~1), attributed to unmodeled NLOS/multipath, surface-model mismatch, and neglected sensor cross-correlations — so the "improved consistency" claim is relative to the baseline, not consistency in an absolute sense.
- **Interesting secondary finding:** attempting to online-update anchor positions/biases as active filter states (rather than fixed Schmidt states) *improves* consistency in some cases but *reduces* accuracy, and mostly fails to improve calibration over time — attributed to weak observability on typical AMR trajectories. This is a useful negative result about the limits of online recalibration.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **No comparison to external/competing baselines** — stated: the conclusion explicitly says "further evaluation should include direct comparisons to external baselines such as tightly-coupled methods." All comparisons are against the authors' own earlier estimator formulation, not competing published systems (only indirect literature-number comparisons for calibration/ATE are given).
- **Small evaluation scale** — inferred: primary evaluation uses only two representative trajectories (indoor calibration + one outdoor test) out of a 13-trajectory dataset; no aggregate statistics across all 13 trajectories are reported for the core results, raising the possibility of cherry-picked runs.
- **Pose priors and surface model assumed available** — stated: the method explicitly "assumes pose priors during initialization, a provided surface model, and platform-specific noise parameters," which the authors note limits generality "for generic applications," though they argue these are typically available industrially.
- **Flat-ground/manifold assumption breaks down outdoors** — stated: "minor floor unevenness introduces unmodeled disturbances under the flat-ground assumption," contributing to high outdoor NEES.
- **High residual NEES outdoors despite improvement** — stated: authors attribute this to "unmodeled non-LOS (NLOS) and multipath effects, surface-model mismatch, and neglected correlations between sensor-specific state blocks" — i.e., the model is knowingly incomplete.
- **Online recalibration doesn't really work** — stated: modeling anchor positions/biases as actively updated states shows "most remain largely unchanged or degrade," attributed to weak observability; this is presented as a limitation of the online approach rather than a fixed capability.
- **No systematic sensitivity studies** — stated: authors call for "systematic studies regarding operating requirements like sensitivity to pose prior quality, surface-model mismatch and trajectory excitation" as future work, meaning the current paper does not characterize how sensitive the whole pipeline is to prior quality.
- **Ground truth itself derived from the robot's own onboard system** — inferred: the "ground truth" poses for the logistics AMR come from the robot's own LiDAR-based localization/static map, not an independent reference system, which could correlate errors between the "ground truth" and the odometry used in the fusion filter.
- **Forklift cross-validation is partial, not full-pipeline** — stated: "it does not re-evaluate the full deployment pipeline," and improvements there were only "modest," undercutting how strongly the abstract's "transferability" claim should be read.
- **Computational profiling is informal** — stated: "we ran the filter offline... without a complete profiling study," so real-time performance claims (µs-level timings) are indicative, not a rigorous embedded benchmark.
- **Single commercial platform for the main pipeline** — inferred: the full calibration+fusion pipeline is only demonstrated on one AMR type (AGILOX) at one site; broader deployment-readiness across diverse robot geometries/environments is not directly evidenced.
- **Range-dependent bias and terrain info unavailable for forklift dataset** — stated: this asymmetry between the two experiments (richer bias modeling only tested on the primary AMR) means the "transferability" result is under a weaker version of the proposed model, not the full method.
