# Energy-Aware Open-Vocabulary Detection Scheduling for Low-Power Robotic Autonomy

**Authors:** Abdul Basit, Muhammad Shafique

**arXiv:** [2611.07663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07663)

**Categories:** cs.RO, cs.AI

**Submitted:** 2026-11-05

**Relevance:** 7/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
The paper adds a robot-state-aware scheduler (odometry, map-change entropy, detector confidence) that switches a YOLO-World-S open-vocabulary detector among LOW/MID/HIGH power modes on a Jetson Orin Nano, instead of running it at a fixed rate. On 40 real AgileX LIMO navigation trials it gets slightly higher mission success (8/10 vs 7/10) and 5.2% lower mean Jetson power than an 8 Hz baseline, but mission-window energy is not lower because missions run longer; a stationary 60 s benchmark isolates the perception workload and shows a cleaner power/energy win (8.045 W→7.301 W, 484.7 J→438.1 J).

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Open-vocabulary object detection (OVD) lets a robot be told "find the bottle" at runtime rather than being limited to a fixed label set — made possible by vision-language models like CLIP and detectors like YOLO-World that ground arbitrary text queries in images. The catch: running such a detector on every camera frame is computationally expensive, and a mobile robot has a small battery shared across GPU, camera, LiDAR, radios, and motors. Running the detector at a fixed high rate (e.g., 8 Hz) wastes energy on "low-value" frames — when the robot is sitting still, re-observing already-mapped space, or looking at a visually static scene. But running it at a fixed low rate risks missing the target or responding slowly when something interesting appears. Neither a constant-high nor constant-low rate is right throughout a whole mission because the "value" of a frame changes over time.

**Why prior work doesn't solve this.** Three research threads touch pieces of this: (1) OVD/vision-language model papers (CLIP, GLIP, Grounding DINO, YOLO-World) optimize recognition accuracy and per-frame latency, not when to call the detector. (2) Language-grounded navigation/mapping papers (VLMaps, LM-Nav, ConceptGraphs) use OVD as a building block for semantic tasks but don't address its energy scheduling. (3) Adaptive video analytics (NoScope, Chameleon, ApproxDet) adapt resolution/rate/model size for passive video streams, but a robot has richer, mission-native signals (it's moving, mapping, and can fail a task) that passive video pipelines don't have access to or care about.

**The mechanism.** The paper formalizes the problem: at each time step the system picks a detector rate f(t) and precision mode (FP16/INT8). The objective is to cut "Jetson input power" (`tegrastats` VDD_IN reading — power drawn by just the Jetson module) and detector call count, while keeping mission success rate and time-to-detect (TTD) acceptable. They explicitly separate *power* from *energy*: energy is power integrated over the whole mission duration, so if slower perception makes the robot take longer to finish, total energy can go up even though instantaneous power went down.

The scheduler is a 3-state finite-state machine with hysteresis (to avoid rapid flickering between states):
- **LOW** (INT8, ~1.5 Hz): entered only after sustained evidence the robot is nearly stationary (linear speed below threshold) AND the local occupancy-grid map has stopped changing (entropy change below threshold) for a minimum dwell time. Runs a cheaper quantized model since confidence requirements are relaxed.
- **MID** (FP16, ~4 Hz): default navigation/mapping state — new viewpoints are arriving but no confident target yet.
- **HIGH** (FP16, ~8 Hz): triggered when detector confidence crosses a threshold or the robot is near a previously estimated target location; prioritizes fast, high-rate response over power savings. Only exits after confidence stays low for several consecutive evaluations (hysteresis), so a flickering detection doesn't bounce the robot out of high-alert mode prematurely.

Map "value" is operationalized as normalized Shannon entropy of a local occupancy grid window — unexplored cells are maximally uncertain (p=0.5), and entropy change over time (ΔH) measures whether the robot is still learning something new about its surroundings. Target location estimates come from back-projecting the detector's 2D bounding box through the RGB-D camera into the map frame, snapped to LiDAR returns, and smoothed exponentially; the estimate expires after 30 s without a refresh so the robot doesn't chase a stale "ghost" target forever.

All thresholds (idle speed, entropy threshold, confidence thresholds, dwell times) are fixed constants tuned once, not adapted per trial — a simple, deterministic, interpretable rule system rather than a learned policy.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: combining three real robot-native signals (motion, map-change entropy, detector confidence/target proximity) into a single rule-based scheduler that governs *detector invocation rate and precision* specifically for open-vocabulary detection, with a hysteresis design to prevent chattering between power states. The "mission resource" framing of detector calls — treating each OVD invocation as a cost to be spent only when it is "worth it" — is the central reframing, distinct from prior per-frame compression or passive-video-analytics adaptation work.
- **Evidence actually supports**: a modest, real-hardware power reduction (5.2% mean power on navigation trials, 9.2–9.6% power/energy reduction in the controlled stationary microbenchmark) with no loss (in fact slightly better aggregate count) in mission success, on a genuinely physical robot rather than simulation.
- **Important negative/nuanced finding presented honestly**: lower instantaneous power does NOT translate to lower total mission energy, because the scheduler's missions ran ~36% longer on average, which dominates the energy integral. This is a real methodological point about why you can't evaluate embedded perception scheduling on power alone — you need power, energy, success, TTD, and runtime together. This finding is actually against the "cleaner" story and the authors report it rather than suppress it, which is a mark of honesty, but it also means the headline "energy-aware" framing is only cleanly validated in the stationary microbenchmark, not in the main navigation experiment.
- **Claimed vs. supported**: the claim that the scheduler offers "competitive task reliability" and "power-management" benefit is reasonably supported for this setup; the stronger implicit framing ("energy-aware scheduling" reduces energy) is explicitly NOT supported in the main navigation results and the authors concede this directly.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Tiny sample size**: only 5 trials per object-policy combination, 10 per policy, 40 total — authors themselves say they report "descriptive rather than inferential statistics" because the sample is too small for statistical claims. (stated)
- **Success-rate difference is tiny in absolute terms** (8/10 vs 7/10 = one trial), and the object-level breakdown shows Base-8Hz actually wins on bottle trials (5/5) and loses badly on cup trials (2/5) — the aggregate "win" partly reflects object-specific baseline variance, not a robust general effect. (stated — "sample is too small to claim universal dominance")
- **Energy objective not actually achieved in the primary (navigation) evaluation**: mean mission-window Jetson energy for the proposed scheduler is higher than Base-8Hz in the main trials, undercutting the "energy-aware" framing for the real-world task; only the artificial stationary benchmark cleanly shows energy savings. (stated)
- **Non-randomized trial order**: trials were run in policy-specific blocks rather than randomized, so battery state, lighting, or thermal drift could confound comparisons across policies/time. (stated)
- **Only Jetson-module power measured, not whole-robot energy** (motors, LiDAR, Wi-Fi, etc. excluded) — so conclusions about "robot" energy efficiency are actually about one subsystem only. (stated, listed as future work)
- **Only two target object classes (bottle, cup) and one arena/environment** — generalization to other object types, environments, lighting, or clutter levels is untested. (stated as future work: "broaden objects and environments... randomize trials")
- **Fixed/hand-set thresholds, no ablation of scheduler parameters**: Table II's thresholds (vidle, τH, τhigh, τhyst, dnear, Klost, Tlow) are stated as "fixed rather than tuned per trajectory," but no sensitivity analysis or ablation shows how performance depends on these choices or how brittle the scheduler is to different thresholds/environments. (inferred — no ablation study presented)
- **Baselines are simple fixed-rate detectors, not other adaptive/scheduling systems** (e.g., no comparison against ApproxDet, Chameleon, or a learned policy) — so the comparison shows "adaptive beats fixed-rate" but not "this adaptive scheme beats other adaptive schemes." (inferred)
- **No external/ground-truth success labeling**: success field comes from the robot's own "mission summary record," i.e., self-reported by the same system being evaluated, with no independent verification mentioned; authors list "add external labels" as future work, implying current labeling may be less rigorous. (stated, future work implies a current limitation)
- **No motion-predictive or learned component** — scheduler is purely reactive/threshold-based; authors flag "motion-predictive scheduling" as future work, suggesting the current design may react late to oncoming target-rich situations. (stated)
- **TTD metric is conditional on success only**, and Base-2Hz's lowest TTD is explicitly flagged by the authors as reflecting "successful easy cases, not generally faster search" — a caution against over-reading one favorable-looking number. (stated)
