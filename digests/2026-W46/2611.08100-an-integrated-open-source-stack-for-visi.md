# An Integrated Open-Source Stack for Vision-Based Autonomous Navigation of Wheeled Rovers

**Authors:** Iaroslav Kolomiets, Iaroslav Sheipak, Mikhail Reppo, Vsevolod Egorov, Viktor Chertopolokhov, Isaac Chairez

**arXiv:** [2611.08100](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08100)

**Categories:** cs.RO

**Submitted:** 2026-11-05

**Relevance:** 8/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
This paper presents an open-source ROS 2 software stack that lets a wheeled rover autonomously follow painted directional arrows to a terminal cone and return to start, combining YOLOv8n vision detection, RGB-D SLAM, behavior-tree mission logic, and a custom proportional path-follower on STM32-controlled hardware. The detector and classical-CV baseline are benchmarked on a public dataset, and the integrated system is demonstrated in three field missions (1 indoor, 2 outdoor) with no collisions. The contribution is primarily an engineering integration effort with public code/data/weights, not a new algorithm.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** A small ground rover needs to autonomously navigate outdoors using only a camera (plus an RGB-D sensor for mapping) — detect hand-placed visual cues (arrow signs and a cone), figure out where those cues are in the world, drive to them without hitting anything, and come back to its starting point. Doing this reliably requires stitching together several independently-hard subsystems: perception (what/where is the marker), localization/mapping (where am I, what's around me), planning (what path should I take), control (how do I turn the actual motors to follow that path), and mission logic (what's the sequence of goals: find arrow → go → search next → reach cone → return). On a resource-constrained embedded computer (Jetson), each piece must also be fast enough and the pieces must agree on interfaces.

**Why off-the-shelf pieces don't just plug together.** ROS 2 gives you middleware conventions and some standard reusable modules (Nav2 for planning, RTAB-Map for RGB-D SLAM, OMPL, AprilTag/ArUco for fiducial markers), but: (1) these standard fiducial-marker systems (AprilTag/ArUco) assume coded, printed markers — this project instead needs to interpret plain uncoded arrows/cones, which requires a custom perception pipeline (object detector + geometry) rather than a marker-decoding library; (2) general planners like Nav2's MPPI controller, tuned for holonomic or faster platforms, produced jittery "back-and-forth" motion at the rover's required low speed (0.2 m/s) — a known failure mode where sampling-based controllers behave poorly under tight velocity bounds; (3) RGB-D depth sensors return noisy/invalid long-range readings that, if naively rasterized into an occupancy grid, falsely mark distant open space as "occupied," blocking the robot from pursuing goals beyond ~3m; (4) the rover's drivetrain (independently steered wheels with Ackermann and a special "point turn" mode) isn't a standard holonomic/differential-drive base, so motor commands need a custom kinematics translation layer (steering-geometry node) rather than reusing a generic base driver.

**The actual mechanism, step by step:**
1. **Perception**: A GigE global-shutter camera feeds 640×480 frames at 30Hz into YOLOv8n (a small, 3.2M-parameter real-time object detector), trained on a 6,147-image custom dataset to detect two classes: arrow-plates and cones. Direction (left/right) is decided not by the detector but by simple post-hoc geometric image analysis (mass asymmetry, width gradient, convex-hull sharpness — majority vote of 3 cues) on the detected box. Distance comes from a pre-calibrated lookup table mapping bounding-box size to real-world range (measured at 1m increments, 1-10m); bearing is computed via a simple pinhole-camera arctan formula from the box's horizontal pixel position.
2. **Localization & mapping**: Wheel encoders + IMU are fused via a standard Extended Kalman Filter (robot_localization) for odometry; RTAB-Map takes this odometry plus the RealSense RGB-D stream to build a pose-graph SLAM map with loop closure, producing a corrected map-frame pose and an occupancy grid (with the depth range manually clipped to avoid the false-"occupied"-at-long-range bug mentioned above).
3. **Mission logic**: A Behavior Tree (BehaviorTree.CPP) encodes the mission as a loop: search for a marker (via a stationary "point turn" rotation), once ten consecutive detections agree within a tolerance, convert the detection into a map-frame navigation goal (placed just outside a 2m "stop zone" around the marker), send it to Nav2, wait for arrival or timeout/re-issue, then repeat; on reaching the cone, it navigates back to the stored start pose.
4. **Planning & control**: Nav2's Smac Hybrid-A* planner (using a Reeds-Shepp motion model appropriate for a car-like, non-holonomic vehicle) computes a feasible path. Instead of Nav2's default MPPI controller (which performed poorly at low speed), the authors wrote their own simple proportional (P) controller plugin: it looks 1m ahead along the path, computes lateral offset and heading error to that point, and converts them into angular/linear velocity commands via a simple proportional law (ω = -kp·e_y, v = v_max·cos(e_θ)).
5. **Actuation**: Commands get translated by a steering-geometry node into four individual wheel steering angles (via lookup table) and speeds, sent over a checksummed serial link to STM32 boards running 20Hz PID loops on the actual motors/steppers.

The authors also built a classical OpenCV baseline (hand-crafted color/shape rules for arrows and cones) to quantify how much the learned detector helps, and compared it against YOLOv8n under one shared IoU-matching protocol.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new/useful**: A complete, publicly released, end-to-end open-source ROS 2 stack (code, trained weights, labeled 6K-image dataset, evaluation logs) that others can reuse or build on — this kind of fully-integrated, documented, hardware-to-perception release is less common than individual component papers.
- **Evidence-supported claim**: The learned YOLOv8n detector substantially outperforms a classical hand-crafted CV baseline under a shared evaluation protocol (AP@0.5 of 0.68 vs 0.23 for arrows, 0.68 vs 0.05 for cones) — this comparison is a controlled, like-for-like test and is credible.
- **Evidence-supported claim**: Low latency (25ms/~40fps) on embedded Jetson hardware with INT8 TensorRT is demonstrated and plausible given standard YOLOv8n deployment numbers.
- **Weakly-supported claim**: That their custom proportional path-follower is "better" than Nav2's MPPI controller for this platform — this is based on qualitative field/simulation observation ("frequent re-steering," "back-and-forth motion"), not a quantitative benchmark (the paper itself states no archived quantitative comparison exists).
- **Weakly-supported claim**: "Autonomous navigation and return without collisions" across 3 missions — true as stated, but this demonstrates integration feasibility on a handful of trials, not statistical reliability, accuracy, or robustness (the authors say this explicitly).
- Direction classification, range/bearing accuracy, and localization error are all claimed informally or not quantified at all — the paper is notably careful/honest about *not* claiming numbers it doesn't have, which is itself a (meta) point of intellectual honesty rather than a result.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **stated** — Only three total missions (1 indoor, 2 outdoor) were run; this demonstrates integration, not statistical reliability ("Three missions demonstrate integration, not statistical reliability").
- **stated** — No quantitative controller comparison (P vs MPPI) was archived; preference for the P controller is based on qualitative observation only, and holds only for this specific low-speed configuration.
- **stated** — Physical return-to-start error was never measured against an external ground-truth reference; "return to start" success is only verified via Nav2's internal goal-checker tolerance (0.4m/0.5rad) and qualitative console coordinate comparison.
- **stated** — Arrow-direction classification accuracy and range/bearing accuracy over the full 1–10m operating range are not established; only a small near-range (0.8–0.95m, 198 frames) jitter test was done, which doesn't generalize.
- **stated** — Exact deployed configuration values (e.g., path-follower gain kp=2.0 in config vs. Kp=1 in README; a "3m corrective cutoff" for depth filtering) could not be verified against what was actually run during the 2025 field trials — software/config provenance for the actual deployment was not archived.
- **stated** — Portability to a second rover platform has not been demonstrated despite claiming a reusable/hardware-abstracted interface design.
- **stated** — The search-rotation ("point turn") maneuver is executed open-loop outside Nav2's costmap collision checking, so its swept footprint isn't verified collision-free — flagged explicitly as a safety gap and future work.
- **stated** — Simulation-based integration testing did not archive continuous trajectory logs, so no numerical sim-based comparison (cross-track RMSE, terminal error) could be reported despite simulation being used for tuning.
- **inferred** — The "Three missions... no reported collisions" claim is self-reported by the same team running the mission without independent instrumentation; combined with "no recovery behaviors reported" and many "n/r" (not recorded) fields in the results table (route length, duration, verified marker count for M1/M2), this suggests substantial gaps in what was actually logged/measurable, weakening confidence in the success claims.
- **inferred** — The classical OpenCV baseline, while a legitimate comparison, is a somewhat weak/strawman baseline (hand-crafted color/shape heuristics) rather than any modern lightweight CNN or alternative detector, so the magnitude of YOLOv8n's advantage may be partly an artifact of baseline weakness rather than evidence that YOLOv8n is near some meaningful ceiling.
- **inferred** — Test-split mAP@0.5 (0.720) is noticeably lower than validation-split mAP@0.5 (0.826), and the paper attributes this partly to a small test-arrow count (541) and domain shift from web-sourced training images to field imagery — suggesting the headline 0.826 (and even abstract-quoted 0.720) numbers may not transfer robustly to new deployment environments.
- **inferred** — The "weighted specification-compliance checklist" scores (96–100%) reported per mission are a self-defined, self-scored rubric (by project authors/commission) rather than an external/standardized benchmark, so their apparent near-perfect scores should not be read as independent validation of navigation quality.
