# From Tokens to Watt-hours: Analytical Energy Estimation for LLM Inference on Modern GPUs

**Authors:** Tina Vartziotis, Rodopi Kosteli, Elli Vartziotis, George Dasoulas, Michael Keckeisen, Konstantinos Skianis, Sotirios Kotsopoulos, Francesca Dominici

**arXiv:** [2607.26571](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26571)

**Categories:** cs.LG, cs.SE

**Submitted:** 2026-07-29

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
This paper introduces an analytical, calibrated formula for estimating the GPU-side energy of LLM inference (compute + HBM memory traffic) without needing hardware power measurement, splitting costs into prefill vs. decode and further into compute, parameter-access, KV-cache-write, and attention-read terms. The model is calibrated against one external measurement study and matches measured per-request energy within roughly 5–27% across a handful of model sizes. It's meant as a comparative/design-time tool (e.g., for green-coding and model selection), not a replacement for real telemetry.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Measuring how much energy an LLM inference request actually consumes normally requires hardware telemetry (e.g., `nvidia-smi`, NVML, power meters) tied to a specific GPU, serving stack, and runtime. That's precise but not portable — you can't use it to compare models you haven't deployed, or to reason about energy costs at design time before you've built anything. The paper's goal is a "back of envelope, but principled" estimator: given just a model's size (and optionally its architecture) and a token workload, predict GPU energy in watt-hours without running anything.

**Why prior approaches fall short.** Two extremes exist: (1) empirical measurement studies (power meters on real GPUs) — accurate but hardware/framework-specific and not generalizable; (2) pure FLOP-counting / scaling-law analyses (à la Kaplan/Hoffmann-style compute estimates) — generalizable but only capture compute, ignoring memory movement, which becomes a large or dominant cost in specific regimes (e.g., very long generations, where attention reads over the KV-cache grow quadratically). Neither alone gives a token-level, phase-aware, memory-aware energy number.

**The mechanism, step by step.**
1. **Split the request into prefill (processing the prompt) and decode (generating tokens one at a time).** These have different compute/memory patterns: prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel; decode is sequential, each new token re-reading the growing KV-cache.
2. **Model total GPU energy as compute energy + memory energy** (Eq. 3–6): `E = α_TC · FLOPs + e_HBM · bits_moved`, where α_TC and e_HBM are hardware-specific "energy per unit work" constants (pJ per FLOP, pJ per bit), taken from a prior microarchitectural benchmarking paper (not measured by this paper itself).
3. **Compute term**: standard parameter-scaled transformer FLOP approximation, `FLOPs ≈ 6 · N · tokens` (N = parameter count), a well-known rule of thumb from scaling-law literature. Optionally add explicit attention FLOP correction terms (quadratic in sequence length) when architecture details (layers, hidden dim) are known.
4. **Memory term**: total HBM bits moved = parameter-access bits + KV-cache-write bits + attention-read bits (Eq. 10). Parameter access is scaled by a "reuse factor" γ(N) (how much of the weights actually need re-fetching from HBM, since caching/kernel fusion avoid re-reading everything every step). KV-cache writes scale linearly with output tokens. Attention reads scale quadratically with output length (each new token attends over all previous tokens), further scaled by a factor s_attn(N) capturing non-ideal cache-read overhead. An overall inefficiency factor η(N) accounts for bandwidth saturation and other non-ideal HBM behavior.
5. **Calibration**: γ(N), s_attn(N), and η(N) are not derived from first principles — they're power-law functions with a handful of free parameters, fit via least-squares against one external measurement paper's reported energy numbers (Caravaca et al.) across a small number of model sizes. This is explicitly "structure from theory, magnitude from calibration."
6. **Simplified fallback estimator**: when architecture details (layers, hidden dim) aren't available, they drop the memory terms entirely and just use compute-only, parameter-scaled energy per token (`α_eff · K · N`), with a lookup-table multiplier for prefill cost based on prompt-length bucket.

**What comes out**: energy per input token, per output token, per full request (in Wh), and a breakdown of how much of that is compute vs. parameter access vs. KV writes vs. attention reads — letting you see, e.g., that compute dominates for short-to-medium generations but attention-read memory traffic dominates for very long ones (quadratic growth).

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new/useful**: a fully transparent, reproducible closed-form estimator that decomposes inference energy by phase (prefill/decode) and by resource (compute vs. three distinct memory-traffic sources), which is more granular than prior FLOP-only or fully-measured approaches. This decomposition is useful for reasoning about *why* energy grows (e.g., identifying that long generations are increasingly memory-bound due to quadratic KV-cache reads, not just compute-bound).
- **Claimed vs. supported**: The paper claims the estimator "captures the correct order of magnitude and scaling behavior." The evidence for this is a comparison against exactly one external measurement source (Caravaca et al.) across only 4 model-size data points (8B, 24B, 70B, 72B), with errors ranging 4.7%–27.2%. This supports "order of magnitude is roughly right" but is a fairly thin evidentiary base for a broader claim of general reliability across models/hardware/serving stacks.
- **Actionable takeaways** (well-supported by the model's own structure, though not independently validated): shorter outputs, smaller/specialized models, prompt compression, KV-cache quantization, and better batching are the main energy levers — these fall directly out of which terms dominate in the decomposition (compute ∝ N·tokens, attention-read ∝ tokens² for long generations).
- The hardware energy coefficients (α_TC, e_HBM) are **not this paper's contribution** — they're borrowed from a separate microarchitectural benchmarking paper (Antepara et al.), so the novelty is in the workload-to-energy translation layer, not in measuring GPU physics.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Scope excludes most of the real energy cost** (stated): only GPU/accelerator-side compute+memory energy is modeled; CPU, host memory, networking, storage, power-supply losses, cooling, and datacenter PUE (~1.56x per cited Uptime Institute figures) are excluded — so results are not end-to-end service or carbon-emission estimates.
- **Calibration against a single external source, small sample** (stated/inferred): all three calibrated factors (γ, s_attn, η) are fit against one measurement paper (Caravaca et al.) with only a handful of model sizes; the paper itself acknowledges "small number of calibration parameters and limited data points" and that recalibration is needed per GPU generation/serving engine/batching regime — this is a narrow validation base for a "general" methodology (inferred: this also means the 4-point comparison in Table 4 isn't independent validation — the same source informed calibration, raising circularity concerns about how much of the low error is genuine predictive accuracy vs. curve-fitting to the same distribution).
- **No batching/continuous-batching modeling** (stated): evaluation assumes single-request execution; real deployments almost always batch, which changes memory-traffic and utilization patterns substantially — a major real-world confound left unaddressed.
- **Architecture effects only partially captured** (stated): grouped/multi-query attention, mixture-of-experts routing, and modality-specific (vision-language) processing are not modeled by the parameter-scaled FLOP approximation; the simplified estimator ignores architecture entirely and can give identical energy for models with the same parameter count but very different real designs.
- **No kernel/runtime-level simulation** (stated): no modeling of CUDA scheduling, kernel fusion, cache residency, or inference-engine-specific optimizations (TensorRT-LLM, vLLM, etc.); these are all folded into a few generic calibrated multipliers, which is a strong simplification.
- **No carbon/emissions estimate despite motivating context** (stated): the introduction frames the work around environmental footprint/Green AI, but the paper explicitly does not estimate carbon emissions — only GPU energy — leaving a gap between motivation and delivered output.
- **Non-monotonic/unexplained deviations** (stated): the memory-energy component shows "non-monotonic behavior for certain models" attributed to architecture differences rather than parameter count, which the authors note but don't resolve — suggesting the model's memory term may be sensitive to inputs (layers, hidden dim) in ways not fully validated.
- **Reliance on Generative AI for drafting** (stated): authors disclose using ChatGPT for language editing/restructuring/drafting assistance — not a scientific caveat per se, but relevant for provenance of the text.
- **Hardware coefficients not independently verified by this paper** (inferred): α_TC and e_HBM are taken wholesale from a different benchmarking paper; no sensitivity analysis is shown for how estimates would change if these constants were off by some margin, despite the entire estimator being linear in these two numbers.
