# A Probabilistic Interpretation of KV Cache Eviction

**Authors:** Renato Geh, Alex Chen, Daniel Israel, Aditya Grover, Guy Van den Broeck

**arXiv:** [2608.28293](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28293)

**Categories:** cs.CL, cs.AI

**Submitted:** 2026-08-28

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
This paper formalizes KV cache eviction as an expectation-estimation problem, proving the exact version is NP-complete and showing that existing heuristic eviction methods (H2O, SnapKV, TOVA, StreamingLLM) are implicitly zero-variance, unboundedly-biased estimators. It proposes reframing eviction as probabilistic sampling from a proposal distribution, which enables a self-normalized importance-sampling correction at decode time that has provably bounded bias and variance (O(1/m)). Experiments on Llama3.2-3B and Qwen3-4B show this approach is competitive with existing eviction methods and notably more robust across tasks (fewer catastrophic per-task failures).

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem: KV caching and eviction.** In transformer attention, each new token's output is a weighted average (via softmax over query-key dot products) of all previous tokens' "value" vectors. To avoid recomputing this from scratch at every generation step, models cache the keys (K) and values (V) of past tokens — the "KV cache." For long sequences, this cache becomes huge, so "eviction" methods throw away some cached entries to save memory/compute, keeping only a subset I of positions and hoping the resulting attention output barely changes.

**Why prior methods are heuristic.** Existing methods (StreamingLLM, H2O, SnapKV, TOVA, K-norm) each invent a scoring function over cached entries (e.g., total attention received, L2 norm of the value vector) and simply keep the top-k highest-scoring entries. This works empirically but has no formal justification, and — crucially — none of them do anything about the fact that future (post-eviction) attention computations are now missing probability mass that the softmax normalization no longer accounts for.

**Key reframing: attention = expectation.** The authors observe that softmax attention output is exactly the expected value of V under a categorical distribution over token positions defined by the softmax weights: `O_i = E_{p_i}[V]`. This means computing attention is literally computing an expectation, and KV eviction is a problem of *estimating an expectation using only a subset of the samples/support*. This reframing lets them borrow decades of statistical machinery (Monte Carlo / importance sampling theory) instead of ad hoc heuristics.

**Hardness result.** They define KVEVICTION formally (find a subset I of size ⌊n·r⌋ such that evicted attention approximates full attention within error ε) and prove it's NP-complete via reduction from PARTITION (a classic NP-hard number-partitioning problem). This shows even the "easy" version — where you have full information at eviction time and don't need to predict the future — is intractable in general. The realistic problem (preserving *future* decode-time attention using only currently visible information) is even harder, since you don't yet know the future queries.

**Top-k as a biased estimator.** Existing top-k methods deterministically keep the highest-scored entries — this has zero variance (always same answer) but can have arbitrarily large bias, because dropping entries changes the softmax's normalizing constant (denominator), distorting all the retained probabilities. Imagine keeping only 3 of 5 values with certain weights — the renormalized weights no longer reflect the true distribution, and the resulting expectation can be far off (illustrated in their Figure 1 example: ground truth ≈2.1 vs. uncorrected top-k ≈1.84).

**Probabilistic eviction + decode-time correction.** Instead of deterministically taking top-k, treat the (unnormalized) scores as a *proposal distribution* π, and probabilistically sample which entries to keep (keep entries sampled at least once out of m draws). Track how many times each entry was sampled (its importance weight). At decode time, when some entries are missing, use **self-normalized importance sampling (SNIS)**: reweight the surviving entries by (count / proposal probability) to correct for the distortion caused by eviction, and combine this corrected "before-eviction" expectation with the exact "after-eviction" expectation (since all entries generated after the eviction point are still available). This estimator is proven asymptotically unbiased (converges to the true expectation as sample count m→∞) with bias and variance both bounded by O(1/m) — a much stronger guarantee than the "unboundedly biased" top-k approach.

**Bias-variance dial.** They add a temperature parameter to the importance weights: at τ=1 you get full correction (more variance, less bias); as τ→∞ correction is turned off (reduces to no-correction, less variance, more bias). This exposes an explicit tunable tradeoff, and empirically they find bias reduction matters more than variance reduction for final quality.

**Existing methods as a special case.** They show algebraically that H2O, StreamingLLM, TOVA, SnapKV, K-norm can all be rewritten as picking the top-k of some implicit (unnormalized) proposal distribution — meaning their framework strictly generalizes prior heuristics and lets you "plug in" decode-time correction to any of them.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: the formal computational-complexity result (NP-completeness of exact KV eviction) — prior literature treated this informally.
- **Genuinely new**: the observation that attention = expectation, letting KV eviction be cast as an expectation-estimation/importance-sampling problem, which is the paper's central conceptual contribution.
- **Genuinely new / previously ignored** (per authors, corroborated by their related-work discussion): explicitly correcting for the distributional distortion eviction causes at *decode time* — prior work (e.g., H2O) apparently noted the issue only in passing, without solving it.
- **Useful unifying result**: showing existing top-k heuristics are a special (zero-variance, unbounded-bias) case of their more general estimator family — this is a real theoretical contribution, not just rhetorical framing.
- **What the evidence actually supports**: the corrected probabilistic estimator achieves lower attention-value error (MAE) than uncorrected top-k in a small controlled study (Section 6, one prompt/one model), and achieves competitive-or-better aggregate benchmark scores plus meaningfully better *robustness* (fewer catastrophic per-task failures, measured via a "win score" ranking) on LongBench/RULER with two models (Llama3.2-3B, Qwen3-4B). This robustness claim is the most concretely evidenced result — the paper repeatedly shows individual methods "winning" one split and "dead last" on another, while their best proposal (πmin-h) stays consistently near the top.
- The claim of "competitive performance at the same compression budget" is more modest than "better performance" — the paper is explicit that raw scores are roughly on par with SOTA, with robustness being the main advantage.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Bias-variance trade-off remains imperfect** (stated): SNIS itself is still biased and has nonzero variance; the method is not a free lunch, just a bounded-error alternative to unbounded bias.
- **Impractical sample requirements under peaked distributions** (stated, "Remark 1"): when attention is highly peaked (many near-zero probabilities), achieving low compression ratios may require impractically large m; the authors claim this doesn't arise at "reasonable" ratios (r ≥ 0.1) but this is asserted rather than exhaustively tested.
- **Implementation/memory overhead** (stated): correction requires additional memory of O(k·h·b) bytes and an efficient sparse-tensor implementation to handle per-head variable compression ratios; the authors point to a different paper (Feng et al.) as the presumed solution rather than implementing/benchmarking it themselves.
- **Small-scale MAE experiment** (inferred): the bias/variance illustration in Section 6 (Figure 2) uses a single hand-picked prompt and single first token on one model (Llama3.2-3B) — not a systematic or diverse evaluation; conclusions about bias vs. variance trade-offs are drawn from this limited setup before being (partially) generalized in later benchmark experiments.
- **Limited model scale** (inferred): only two relatively small models are tested (Llama3.2-3B, Qwen3-4B) — no evidence provided for how findings scale to larger, more widely deployed LLMs.
- **Reduced/subsetted benchmarks** (stated): RULER is evaluated on only a 130-example subset, and LongBench only on splits with context <3,000 tokens for three tasks (HotpotQA, QASPER, TriviaQA) — explicitly due to compute constraints. This significantly narrows the long-context regime where KV eviction matters most, potentially not testing the hardest/longest-context cases.
- **No wall-clock throughput/latency results** (inferred): despite the paper's premise being about throughput gains from eviction, no actual runtime/memory/throughput benchmarks are reported — only attention-value error and downstream task scores. The practical speed benefit of probabilistic eviction + correction (given its extra sampling and correction bookkeeping) is not empirically demonstrated.
- **Proposal distribution choice still heuristic** (inferred): while the framework is principled, the four proposals tested (πmin, πmin-h, πH2O, πH2O-h) are still hand-designed, and the "best" one (πmin-h) is chosen post hoc from these four — the paper doesn't claim or show a principled way to select the optimal proposal in general.
- **Complexity/tractability of sample-count selection is itself unsolved** (stated, Appendix F): the exact number of samples needed to hit a target compression ratio (EXPECTEDSAMPLES via coupon-collector) is conjectured intractable in closed form; they fall back to a binary-search approximation, an engineering workaround rather than a clean solution.
- **Self-reported computational cost caveat** (stated): the authors note the "full research project required more compute than what is reported," including failed/erroneous preliminary experiments — a transparency note but also a reminder that reported results are a curated subset of experimentation.
- **Grouped-query attention handling adds complexity/approximation** (inferred): the mixture-proposal construction for GQA groups (Section 5) is presented without empirical validation of how much this approximation costs relative to per-head correction.
