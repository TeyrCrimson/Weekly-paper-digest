# Learning to Discriminate Scene Structures Makes Self-Supervised Depth Learning Scalable

**Authors:** Mengtan Zhang, Yuchuan Cui, Yu Ma, Zizhan Guo, Wei Ye, Rui Fan

**arXiv:** [2610.23600](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23600)

**Categories:** cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-10-16

**Relevance:** 8/10 — matched: Metric and temporally-consistent monocular depth

---

## TL;DR
Self-supervised monocular depth estimation (SSMDE) models trained on diverse, mixed datasets actually get *worse* than models trained on a single dataset, because they learn dataset-specific "structural biases" instead of transferable geometry. The paper proposes MaSS, which splits features into a contextual pathway (VFM-anchored) and a structural pathway (trained with a contrastive "scene-structure discrimination" objective) so the model learns generalized layout cues instead of dataset shortcuts. MaSS beats prior SSMDE methods on 4 training datasets and generalizes markedly better on 8 unseen datasets, and is reported as the first SSMDE method that actually improves when trained on more diverse mixed data.

## First-principles breakdown

**The underlying task.** Monocular depth estimation predicts a per-pixel depth map from a single RGB image. "Self-supervised" here means no ground-truth depth is used; instead, the model is trained by warping one video frame into another viewpoint using the predicted depth + estimated camera motion, and penalizing photometric mismatch (novel-view synthesis supervision). This is attractive because it needs only ordinary monocular video, which is abundant — in principle it should scale like other self-supervised learning.

**Why it doesn't scale in practice (the paper's core diagnosis).** Photometric supervision is a noisy, indirect proxy for depth: it breaks down at occlusions, textureless surfaces, and lighting changes, and its gradients don't reliably point toward true depth error — so training is prone to local minima. On a single dataset, a network can partially compensate for this weak, noisy signal by exploiting that dataset's own statistical regularities (e.g., "in KITTI driving scenes, depth increases toward a vanishing point in the image center"). This acts like a crutch that makes a mediocre supervisory signal sufficient to converge. The authors show this directly: an encoder trained on driving data has a feature-activation pattern that just tracks "distance from vanishing point" rather than true scene geometry (Fig. 2a) — i.e., it memorized a dataset-specific shortcut, not generalizable depth cues. When you mix datasets (indoor rooms, highways, adverse weather), these shortcuts conflict with each other — a room has no vanishing-point bias, a highway does — so the network can't settle on any single consistent bias, and overall performance drops relative to single-dataset training. This is framed as "cross-domain optimization conflict."

**The fix: decouple "what is in the scene" from "what shape is the scene."** The intuition: two photos can look contextually similar (same objects: a desk, a wall) but have very different spatial layouts (corridor vs. open room), and conversely visually dissimilar scenes (an indoor hallway, an outdoor street) can share the same layout (both are "narrow corridors receding to a vanishing point"). If you train a single feature pathway to capture both appearance/context and layout/structure together, these two signals interfere and the model collapses toward the easiest-to-fit one (dataset-specific bias). So MaSS explicitly splits the representation into two parallel pathways:
- **Contextual pathway**: object-level, appearance/semantic information useful for depth (e.g., "this pixel is part of a car," "this is foreground vs. background").
- **Structural pathway**: global scene layout / perspective geometry, decoupled from the above.

**How the architecture produces these two pathways.**
1. *Backbone*: a frozen pretrained vision foundation model (VFM, DINOv2) supplies strong, generalizable semantic features, but frozen VFM features alone lack sufficient geometric/depth cues (shown empirically — Fig 2b). Fine-tuning the VFM directly under noisy self-supervision would corrupt its good pretrained representations.
2. *Bidirectional interaction*: borrowed from ViT-Adapter, a trainable conventional depth encoder (CNN/ViT) and the frozen VFM exchange information via cross-attention in both directions — the depth encoder injects geometric cues into VFM tokens, and VFM tokens inform the depth encoder. This lets the frozen VFM's features become geometry-aware without destabilizing its pretrained weights.
3. *Two projectors*: the interacted VFM features are fed into a "contextual projector" and a "structural projector," each upsampled into multi-scale feature pyramids for the depth decoder.
4. *Anchoring-decoupling constraint (Lac)*: this is a regularizer with two terms — (a) an "anchor" term forcing pairwise similarities among contextual descriptors (across a batch) to match the pairwise similarities of the original frozen VFM descriptors — this keeps the contextual pathway faithful to VFM's generalizable semantics rather than drifting into dataset-specific shortcuts during adaptation; (b) a "decoupling" term that penalizes correlation between the contextual and structural descriptor of the *same* image, pushing the structural pathway to encode something complementary (not redundant with context).

**The novel learning signal: scene-structure discrimination via contrastive learning.** You can't directly supervise "structure" with a label — there's no ground truth for abstract layout similarity. Instead, the paper defines a self-computed similarity metric between pairs of images in a training batch, based on their *predicted* depth maps:
- Downsample the predicted depth map and bin it into a histogram over depth values (20 bins), using "soft binning" (sigmoid-based smooth assignment, not hard bin counts) to avoid being overly sensitive to local noise/artifacts.
- Compare two images' depth histograms using symmetric KL divergence — a standard way to measure how different two probability distributions are.
- Convert this into a similarity score; any pair above a threshold (γ=0.95) is treated as a "positive pair" (similar global layout), everything else is "negative."
- Train the structural feature descriptors with a multi-positive InfoNCE contrastive loss (same family as e.g. CLIP's contrastive loss): pull positive pairs' structural features together, push negative pairs apart, in a batch.

This is a form of self-distillation/bootstrapping: the model's own (imperfect but globally meaningful) depth histogram is used as a pseudo-label to organize an *intermediate representation* that then feeds back into producing better depth. To bootstrap this safely, they first run one "initialization epoch" with normal photometric loss only, since early random depth predictions would give meaningless histograms.

**Final depth prediction**: the contextual and structural pyramids are concatenated channel-wise, compressed with a 1×1 conv, and passed to a standard depth decoder.

## Key insights

- **Diagnosis is the main novel contribution, arguably more than the fix**: identifying that SSMDE's high-level encoder features encode dataset-specific bias rather than geometry (shown via feature activation maps, Fig. 2) is a clear, falsifiable claim backed by a direct visualization comparison — this is a genuinely useful framing for why mixed-domain self-supervised training has failed before.
- **"First to benefit from mixed-domain training" is a real, demonstrated result**, not just a claimed one: Table 1 shows four prior SOTA methods all degrade under mixed-dataset training relative to single-dataset training, while MaSS improves (0.094→0.092 Abs Rel on KITTI, etc.) — this comparison is apples-to-apples (same mixed-training protocol applied to all methods), which is good evidence.
- **Ablations (Table 4) support each component being necessary**: removing contrastive learning, removing representation decoupling, or removing the bidirectional interaction architecture each degrades performance, especially on zero-shot datasets — supporting the causal story (not just correlation) that structural decoupling aids generalization.
- **Zero-shot generalization results are strong and the evaluation is unusually broad** (8 additional datasets spanning driving, indoor, and explicitly out-of-distribution sets DIML/DIODE) — this is more thorough than the "supplementary only" generalization evaluation the authors say is typical in prior SSMDE papers.
- **Scalability evidence is explicitly labeled preliminary by the authors themselves**: adding 50K TartanAir samples (150K→200K) gives small, mixed gains (even slightly negative Abs Rel change on some "similar to training domain" sets) and the biggest win is only on the hardest OOD sets. The authors state this is far below the data scale of supervised depth foundation models and that true large-scale scalability "remains largely unexplored."
- **Efficiency claim**: MaSS reportedly outperforms prior large-scale SSMDE baselines (KBR, KBR++) while using substantially less training data (150K vs. 1.9M/2.2M) and comparable or lower FLOPs/params (Table 3) — supports a "smarter not just bigger" framing, though it's a single comparison point.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Scalability claim is self-acknowledged as preliminary** — the paper explicitly states the TartanAir expansion experiment (Sect 4.5) only gives "preliminary evidence," at a data scale "far smaller" than supervised foundation models like Depth Anything, and that behavior at larger scale "remains largely unexplored and is left for future research." (stated)
- **Indoor performance still lags supervised foundation models** — the authors admit MaSS underperforms supervised DFMs on standard indoor datasets, attributing this to simpler/more homogeneous indoor depth distributions favoring supervised pretraining. (stated)
- **Evaluation-metric sensitivity acknowledged**: the authors note that reported performance gaps vs. DepthAnythingV2 and inconsistencies with KBR/KBR++'s originally published numbers are partly artifacts of alignment-protocol choice (median vs. least-squares, depth-domain vs. inverse-depth-domain), and show in the supplement that switching alignment protocol substantially changes the numbers favoring different methods — meaning single-protocol comparisons in the main table should be read cautiously. (stated)
- **Hyperparameters tuned empirically without sensitivity analysis shown in main text**: γ=0.95 threshold for positive pairs is described as "based on empirical tuning," and K=20 bins, τh, τc are fixed values with no ablation sweep presented over these specific values (only qualitative examples in supplement, not a quantitative sensitivity study). (inferred)
- **Training/compute scale is modest relative to the field** — single RTX 4090, ~2.5 days, max 200K training samples — which is a reasonable academic-scale setup but caps how strongly the "scalability" claims generalize beyond the tested regime; the field's actual foundation models use tens of millions of samples (noted in paper's own Table 3: DA V2 uses 62M). (inferred, though partially acknowledged by authors regarding future scaling)
- **No stated uncertainty/variance across runs** — all reported numbers appear to be single-run point estimates (no error bars, no repeated-seed variance reported for the main comparison tables), making it hard to judge how much of the improvement margin is reproducible vs. noise, especially for close calls (e.g., DIML/DIODE numbers where MaSS sometimes just barely beats or loses to baselines). (inferred)
- **Mixed-dataset baselines reproduced by the authors themselves** ("we reproduce several SoTA methods... with publicly available training code") rather than using original authors' mixed-training numbers — reasonable given no prior work did mixed training, but introduces risk that baseline reproduction/hyperparameter choices were not as thoroughly tuned as the authors' own method. (inferred)
- **"Not addressed in the paper"**: no discussion of inference-time computational cost/latency trade-off versus simpler single-pathway baselines in deployment contexts (only FLOPs/params are reported, not runtime); no discussion of robustness to camera intrinsics variation across the many datasets used; no failure-case analysis showing where MaSS itself still fails qualitatively (only comparative success cases are shown).
