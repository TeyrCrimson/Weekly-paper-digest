## TL;DR
This is Part 1 of a two-part survey on infrared small target detection (IRSTD), covering ~1050 papers from 1994–2026 and focusing on "traditional" (filter/statistical) methods and low-rank-plus-sparse-decomposition (model-based) methods; Part 2 (not included here) covers deep learning. The paper's core argument is that no existing method handles all real-world challenges simultaneously, and that this is due to fragmented frameworks, insufficient exploration of newer representation models (e.g., graph/hypergraph learning), lack of a realistic large-scale benchmark dataset, and an "evaluation gap" where current metrics are purely post-hoc (result-oriented) rather than measuring a priori task difficulty. It is a review/taxonomy paper, not a paper proposing a new detection algorithm.

## First-principles breakdown

**The underlying problem.** Infrared sensors detect heat radiation rather than reflected light, so they work day/night and independent of illumination — useful for military tracking (missiles, aircraft) and civilian surveillance (ships, vehicles). But because targets are often imaged from very long range, they occupy very few pixels (by convention, smaller than 9×9, or <0.12% of image area) and carry weak, noisy signal ("dim") with no real shape or texture — just a blob of intensity that fades outward. The core technical challenge is: pull a few-pixel, low-contrast blob out of a cluttered, noisy background (clouds, sea glint, urban textures, vignetting, sensor noise) with as few false alarms as possible, often in real time.

**Why this is hard.** Standard object detection relies on shape, texture, and distinguishable local structure — none of which small IR targets have. Background clutter (cloud edges, sea waves, building edges) can mimic the directional/edge signatures that would otherwise help detection. So algorithms must lean on statistical/geometric priors about how targets differ from background, rather than on recognizable appearance.

**Three families of methods (the paper's taxonomy).**
1. *Mathematical/traditional unsupervised models* — these assume the background varies "smoothly" and is locally self-correlated/predictable, while a target is a local anomaly that breaks this correlation. Concretely:
   - *Filtering methods* (wavelet/contourlet, Top-Hat morphological filters, Least-Mean-Square predictive filters): decompose or predict the image from its neighborhood; subtract the prediction from the actual image — a target shows up as high prediction error, since smooth background is easy to predict but a bright isolated target isn't. Top-Hat filters specifically use a structuring element (shape of expected clutter) to subtract a morphologically "opened" version of the image, isolating bright anomalies; much work went into adaptively choosing that structuring element (genetic algorithms, neural nets) because a bad one causes false positives or missed targets.
   - *Local-contrast methods*: compute an intensity-vs-surroundings contrast measure in small windows — targets create strong local contrast peaks.
   - *Non-local prior / global frequency-domain methods*: instead of comparing a pixel to its immediate neighbors, exploit long-range spatial-frequency structure (sparsity/continuity across the whole image) to separate target from background when clutter is too large-scale for local windows to handle.
   - *Clustering/classification methods* (density peak clustering, Riemannian-manifold clustering, hyperbolic density clustering): treat target-vs-background separation as an unsupervised clustering problem, sometimes mapping features onto curved (non-Euclidean) manifolds because real clutter distributions are "heavy-tailed" and don't cluster well in flat Euclidean feature space — mapping to hyperbolic/Riemannian space supposedly better preserves hierarchical/structural differences between target and clutter.
2. *Low-rank and sparse decomposition models* (the paper's second focus, only briefly covered in this truncated excerpt): built on robust PCA-style reasoning (Candès et al. 2011) — model the image (or a stack of frames as a tensor) as "low-rank background + sparse target + noise," then solve an optimization problem to separate the sparse (target) component from the low-rank (background) component. This became the dominant model-based paradigm (2012 through early deep-learning era) because it needs no labeled training data and handles more clutter types than simple filters, at the cost of being an iterative optimization (slower, harder to tune) rather than a single filtering pass. Many of the cited 2023–2026 papers extend this with tensor decompositions, graph/hypergraph regularization, Tucker decomposition, dynamic step-size adaptation, etc.
3. *Deep learning (data-driven) models* — explicitly deferred to Part 2; this paper only notes they now represent ~51% of published IRSTD papers (553 of ~950+ papers surveyed), reflecting the field's shift toward supervised, learned representations.

**The "evaluation gap" argument.** The authors claim that benchmarking has focused almost entirely on *post-detection* metrics (precision/recall-style, signal-to-clutter-ratio-gain, etc.), which tell you how well an algorithm did, but not how intrinsically hard the input scene was. Without an a priori difficulty measure, it's hard to know if a reported improvement is due to a better algorithm or an easier test set — they cite this as a key reason the field's progress looks stagnant despite heavy publication volume.

## Key insights

- **Claimed contribution**: First survey to span the *entire* history (1994–2026) of IRSTD across all three modeling paradigms (mathematical, low-rank/sparse, deep learning) in one unified taxonomy, versus prior surveys that each covered a narrower slice (e.g., only wavelet/filter methods, or only low-rank decomposition, or only recent deep learning with ~90 papers). The paper backs this with a literature-coverage comparison table (Table 1) claiming ~1050 reviewed papers vs. 30–90 in prior surveys — this scale claim is verifiable only by the bibliography size, not by independent evidence in the text.
- **Genuinely useful organizing move**: classifying methods along two orthogonal axes — (a) modeling tool (mathematical/ML/signal-processing) and (b) single-frame vs. multi-frame, static vs. moving target — which gives readers a map of "what assumption does this family of methods make about target-background separability."
- **A real, substantive observation** (not just a literature count): the field has duplicate/colliding acronyms across 2023–2026 papers (e.g., multiple unrelated papers both named "SANet" or "LESPS"), which is good evidence of extremely rapid, possibly uncoordinated publication growth — this is presented as symptomatic evidence for the "lack of common framework" complaint, and is one of the more concrete, checkable claims in the excerpt.
- **The "evaluation gap" / a priori difficulty metric point** is presented as a genuine conceptual contribution (citing ref 57 for a "target detectability metric"), but this paper itself does not introduce or validate a new metric — it summarizes an existing critique and cites a hybrid-level-metrics paper (66) and the detectability-metric paper (57) as "partial solutions," i.e., this survey synthesizes existing proposals rather than creating an evaluation methodology itself.
- Distinguishing claims from evidence: much of the "contributions" list (comprehensiveness, dataset/code website, challenge classification) are *descriptive/organizational* value, appropriate for a survey, not empirical findings — there are no new experiments, benchmarks, or quantitative comparisons run by the authors in this excerpt; all performance numbers (e.g., "PA-AHDPC achieves 5–10dB SCRG improvement") are reported secondhand from the surveyed papers, not independently verified.
