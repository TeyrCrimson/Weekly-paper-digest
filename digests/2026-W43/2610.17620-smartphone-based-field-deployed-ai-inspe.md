# Smartphone-Based, Field-Deployed AI Inspection System for Power Distribution Infrastructure

**Authors:** Sampath Balaji Masabathula, Srija Vanapalli, Gangadhar Kanchi, Sreenivasa Rao V. A. N, Vijaya Lalitha, Sasank Chilamkurthy

**arXiv:** [2610.17620](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17620)

**Categories:** cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-10-13

**Relevance:** 6/10 — matched: Metric and temporally-consistent monocular depth

---

## TL;DR
This paper describes a smartphone-based AI pipeline (YOLO12m detection/pose, DINOv3 perspective classifiers, Mask2Former segmentation, and monocular depth estimation) that inspects power distribution poles, crossarms, voltage insulators, vegetation clearance, and transformer-ground clearance from field photos. It was piloted on five live feeders in an Indian utility (APEPDCL), flagging 1,222 defects of which 84 were rectified during the study. The authors explicitly state the data do not support a claim of improved feeder reliability — the paper's contribution is the deployed workflow and a released dataset (PD-Defect), not a reliability result.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Utilities must periodically check poles, crossarms, wires, and transformers for safety/reliability issues (leaning poles, tilted crossarms, trees too close to live conductors, transformers losing ground clearance due to soil buildup). Doing this manually at scale is inconsistent — it depends on each inspector's judgment — and prior automated approaches use drones or specialized sensors (good for transmission lines, but impractical for routine, dispersed distribution-feeder inspection by regular field staff). The paper's goal: let ordinary field workers use ordinary Android phones, and have software do consistent, validated condition-assessment.

**Why naive photo + model doesn't work.** A single photo taken at a bad angle gives misleading geometry (a vertical pole can *look* tilted from an off-center shot), and two objects (a tree branch and a wire) can look like they're touching in a 2D photo while actually being meters apart in depth. So the system layers several checks before trusting any detection:

1. **Quality gate**: rule-based checks (resolution, blur, lighting) reject unusable images outright.
2. **Pole detection & selection**: YOLO12m finds candidate poles; a hand-designed score `S = w1/d_c + w2*h_p` picks the most likely "target" pole (closest to image center, tallest) — basically a heuristic for "which pole did the photographer mean to shoot."
3. **Perspective validation (DINOv3)**: a separately fine-tuned vision transformer (DINOv3 ViT-B/16, pretrained self-supervised backbone) does binary classification of "valid angle" vs "distorted/off-axis" — run three times, once on the full image, once on the cropped pole-top, once on transformer/plinth images. This prevents angle artifacts from being misread as real defects (e.g., a photo taken from the side making a straight pole look leaned).
4. **Task-specific inspection modules**, now operating only on validated images:
   - *Pole lean*: YOLO12m classifies leaned/straight; a geometric formula converts two keypoints (top/bottom of pole) into an image-space tilt angle via `atan2`, thresholded at 10°. Note: this is an "apparent" angle in pixel space, not a true physical lean angle — camera perspective still partially confounds it, which the authors acknowledge.
   - *Crossarm/top-cleat tilt*: Similar idea — detect pole axis and component axis via keypoints, compute the angle between them, compare to an "expected" mounting angle (90° for crossarms since they should be perpendicular to the pole, 0° for top-cleats since they should align with it). Deviation beyond 15° = defect.
   - *Voltage classification*: YOLO12m classifies insulator configuration visible near the crossarm as High-Tension or Low-Tension, then geometrically matches that label to the right crossarm using box-overlap rules.
   - *Vegetation clearance*: The hardest problem, because 2D proximity in an image doesn't mean real-world proximity. Solution: segment vegetation and conductor pixels (Mask2Former), estimate *relative* depth per-pixel (Depth Anything V2 — a monocular depth model that ranks "near vs far" without absolute scale), then reason geometrically: if vegetation and conductor masks overlap in the image, check whether their depth values are actually similar (truly close) or different (just a 2D illusion); if they don't overlap, check whether they're close in both pixel distance (scaled by depth, since far objects look smaller) and depth value anyway. These features feed a logistic regression classifier whose decision threshold is tuned to require ≥85% precision on the "risky" class, picked via cross-validated precision-recall curves.
   - *Transformer-ground clearance*: Uses the geometric fact that if ground level rises (e.g. resurfacing) and both pole and plinth lose the same absolute height x, the ratio of pole-box-height to plinth-box-height increases predictably. So a simple pixel-measured ratio R = (pole height)/(plinth height) in the photo, compared to utility-database-recorded true pole height, flags excessive ratio as "risky" or "very risky" — no absolute distance measurement needed, just ratio geometry assuming pole and plinth share a ground plane.
5. **Field workflow integration**: flagged defects get logged, routed to maintenance for physical rectification, then re-verified with a follow-up photo through the same validation pipeline — closing the loop from detection to confirmed fix.

Each module is trained on its own curated/annotated dataset (sizes in the thousands of images, with manually defined annotation protocols e.g. bounding boxes excluding insulators, keypoints at pole top/bottom, etc.), and evaluated with standard detection/classification metrics (mAP, precision/recall/F1).

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new/useful**: the specific *combination* — smartphone-only capture (no drones/special sensors) + explicit image/perspective quality gating + geometric reasoning layered on top of deep-learning detections + a closed-loop rectify-and-verify workflow — deployed in a real utility's operational process rather than a lab/demo setting. The emphasis on validating *viewing geometry before* trusting defect geometry (via dedicated DINOv3 classifiers) is a practical engineering insight prior literature (mostly drone/aerial-focused) doesn't address.
- **The vegetation-clearance depth-geometry trick** (combining monocular relative depth with segmentation to disambiguate 2D-adjacent-but-3D-far cases) is a reasonably clever, cheap way to avoid needing stereo or LiDAR, though it's a heuristic (empirically-tuned thresholds and a single global reference depth/pixel-length pair, `Z_ref=28.38`, `420px`) rather than a calibrated metric method.
- **What the evidence actually supports**: module-level accuracy/F1 numbers (e.g., 97% mAP for pole detection, ~85-95% F1 for most classification tasks) are reasonably supported by held-out test sets. The *operational* claim — "defects were found and some rectified" — is supported (1,222 defects, 84 rectified). The claim implied by the paper's own framing ("may contribute to reducing feeder interruptions") is explicitly **not** supported by the data — the authors are careful to say this themselves.
- **Dataset release (PD-Defect)** is a concrete, verifiable contribution independent of model performance claims — useful for reproducibility in a space with little public field-captured data.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **No demonstrated reliability improvement** (stated): authors explicitly say interruption records don't provide sufficient evidence to attribute any reliability change to the system; only 84/1,222 defects were actually rectified during the study window due to line-clearance permission constraints.
- **Small rectification/verification sample** (stated/inferred): with only 84 rectified cases out of 1,222 flagged, any downstream claims about maintenance-outcome effectiveness are statistically thin — inferred as a real limitation on generalizability.
- **Pole-lean/tilt angles are image-space, not physical** (stated): the paper explicitly notes the computed "lean angle" and "tilt angle" are apparent/image-space measures that "do not correspond directly to the physical lean angle," meaning results partly still depend on how well the perspective-validation gate works — if that gate misses a distorted image, the angle estimate is directly wrong.
- **Depth estimates are relative, not metric** (stated): Depth Anything V2 outputs are explicitly non-metric; thresholds like `Z_ref=28.38` and `420px` are "heuristic reference" values derived from a small set of manually measured reference objects, not a calibrated depth-to-distance mapping — this is a soft, image-set-dependent calibration that may not transfer to other camera models/distances (inferred risk, acknowledged indirectly by authors as "heuristic").
- **No comparison against any baseline method or prior system** (inferred): all evaluation is internal (train/val/test splits on the authors' own datasets); there is no comparison to existing UAV-based or manual-inspection baselines, so claims of being more "practical" than prior work are argued qualitatively, not measured head-to-head.
- **Conductor segmentation quality is weak** (stated numerically): Mask2Former conductor IoU is only 40.77% and F1 57.92%, far below vegetation IoU (87.64%) — the authors note pixel-level segmentation quality doesn't fully determine final image-level risk decision (because of the downstream geometric/logistic-regression correction), but this is still a materially weak component feeding a safety-relevant decision.
- **Vegetation-risk false negative rate was high in validation (21.84%)**, though better on the field production test (6.56%) (stated): the gap between validation and field-production performance (and the fact the threshold was tuned to prioritize precision ≥0.85, accepting lower recall) means a non-trivial share of truly risky vegetation conditions may have been missed, especially in the validation phase — a safety-relevant caveat the authors report but don't dwell on.
- **Labels partly subjective** (stated): vegetation-clearance field labels come from field personnel "reviewed" by experts but are acknowledged to "involve some degree of subjective judgment" — ground truth itself may be noisy.
- **DTR–ground clearance limited to plinth-mounted transformers** (stated): pillar- and pole-mounted configurations aren't evaluated due to insufficient annotated field data; the ratio formula is only validated for one mounting type despite being proposed as generalizable.
- **Single-utility, five-feeder pilot** (inferred): all results come from one DISCOM (APEPDCL) in Andhra Pradesh over one short observation window (baseline Apr–Jun, survey Jul, observation Aug–Sep); generalization to other utilities, climates, pole/asset types, or phone hardware is not addressed in the paper.
- **No ablation studies on the pipeline design choices** (inferred): e.g., no comparison of performance with vs. without the perspective-validation gate, no sensitivity analysis on the 10°/15° thresholds or the `w1,w2` pole-selection weights — these thresholds are stated as "empirically selected" without reporting the sensitivity or robustness of results to different choices.
- **Interruption analysis didn't filter by cause** (stated): the authors note interruption-type fields are operator-entered and unreliable, so they analyzed raw totals without isolating inspection-related causes — reasonable transparency, but it also means the headline interruption numbers (Table V) are essentially uninterpretable as evidence either way, which somewhat undercuts including them prominently in the results.
