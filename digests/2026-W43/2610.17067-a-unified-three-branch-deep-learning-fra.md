# A unified three-branch deep learning framework for automated detection and counting of wildlife in aerial imagery

**Authors:** Zeyu Xu, Tiejun Wang, Andrew Skidmore, Megan Claase, Harry Wells, Isla Duporge, Ramiro D. Crego, Richard Lamprey

**arXiv:** [2610.17067](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17067)

**Categories:** cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-10-12

**Relevance:** 7/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
This paper introduces a three-module pipeline (WildSeg, WildMatch, WildDet) that automates the three manual bottlenecks in aerial wildlife surveys: delineating the valid "counting strip" between aircraft-mounted rods, detecting and removing duplicate animals in overlapping successive photos, and detecting/counting animals themselves. Tested on ~2,000 oblique aerial images of four antelope species in South Sudan, each module beats its respective baseline (U-Net, a heavy RoMaV2 teacher matcher, and YOLO11) on accuracy, with WildMatch also being ~30x faster/lighter than its teacher on CPU. The three networks are trained and run independently (not jointly optimized), connected only by passing masks/outputs between stages in a fixed workflow.

## First-principles breakdown

**The underlying problem:** Aerial wildlife surveys (Systematic Reconnaissance Flights) fly a plane with cameras along transects to photograph the ground, then count animals to estimate population size. But raw detections overcount for two structural reasons: (1) only animals between two physical rods mounted on the wing struts count as "in the sample strip" — everything else is observational noise outside the statistical design; (2) successive photos (taken every ~2 sec) overlap by a variable amount depending on wind-driven aircraft speed, so the same animal can appear in two consecutive frames and get counted twice. Historically, humans did this strip-identification and duplicate-removal manually, which doesn't scale to ~330,000 images per survey. No prior framework automated both of these spatial corrections alongside detection.

**WildSeg — finding the counting strip:** The rods appear as thin, elongated, sometimes-interrupted bright/dark lines across the image (disrupted by sun glare, non-parallel to image edges due to aircraft tilt). A plain semantic segmentation network (U-Net) tends to produce fragmented, discontinuous predictions for such thin elongated objects, since conv nets have local receptive fields and nothing in standard U-Net encourages continuity along a strip. WildSeg modifies this with "learnable offset-sampling" — modules that learn horizontal/vertical pixel offsets (bounded by tanh) and use them to bilinearly sample features from elsewhere in the image, letting the network pull together evidence along the rod's length rather than just locally. After getting a rod-probability mask, it doesn't just threshold pixels — it keeps only connected components spanning ≥80% of image width (filtering out background clutter that happens to look rod-like), then fits a quadratic curve to the rod boundaries (not just treating pixels as the final answer) so the counting strip boundary is smooth and geometrically coherent even where segmentation has gaps. The whole thing runs on a resized, low-resolution image (since rod position is a global/structural property, not a fine-detail one) for speed.

**WildMatch — removing duplicate counts between frames:** To know which part of frame N+1 overlaps frame N, you'd ideally use GPS, but survey GPS logs are often too imprecise or missing. So the paper falls back to pure image matching: find pixel correspondences between two successive photos, then fit a homography (a geometric transformation mapping one image's content onto the other) to compute how much of the previous image's "counting strip" lands inside the current image — that projected region is the overlap to exclude. Classical matchers (SIFT, ORB) and even modern learned matchers (SuperPoint, LoFTR) struggle in savanna scenes because the terrain is visually repetitive (grass texture looks like more grass texture everywhere) — there isn't much distinctive structure to lock onto, unlike cityscapes. The authors' solution is knowledge distillation: take a very large, accurate but computationally expensive "teacher" matcher (RoMaV2) and use its correspondence predictions and confidence scores as training targets for a much smaller "student" network (ResNet18 truncated backbone + VGG19 refinement stages at decreasing strides, going coarse-to-fine: first establish a rough global correspondence via softmax-weighted similarity, then refine it progressively at finer resolutions using local correlation windows). The loss combines coordinate error (weighted by teacher confidence), a confidence-prediction loss, and a "corner projection" term that checks the predicted homography maps the four image corners correctly against ground truth when available. Result: a model with 8M params (vs. 425M) that's ~30x faster on CPU and nearly matches teacher accuracy.

**WildDet — detecting and counting animals:** Standard detectors (YOLO) weren't designed with this specific problem's two quirks: animals are camouflaged against savanna backgrounds (contrast matters more than usual), and YOLO's standard multi-scale detection grid doesn't match the actual range of animal sizes seen in this oblique aerial imagery. WildDet customizes a YOLO-style backbone (C3k2/C3k blocks, SPPF, attention) by: (a) only running detection heads at two feature scales (P3, P4) matched to actual animal sizes, rather than three; (b) fusing features bidirectionally between scales with learned, normalized weights so the network can emphasize whichever scale's features are more useful per-image; (c) adding a parallel "density branch" that predicts a heatmap of expected animal density (trained against Gaussian blobs placed at each annotated box center) — this gives the model an auxiliary supervisory signal about "how many/where roughly" independent of exact box regression, useful for crowded/cryptic cases; (d) adding an "empty-image gate" — a cheap classifier on pooled P5 features that predicts whether an image patch has any animal at all, and if confidence is below a threshold, skips the expensive detection/density heads entirely (since most aerial survey images are empty landscape, this saves compute at inference).

**How it fits together:** All three networks are trained separately on their own objectives/data. At deployment, WildSeg produces a strip mask per image, WildMatch produces an overlap mask between consecutive images, and WildDet produces animal boxes; a detection only counts if its box center falls inside the current strip mask AND outside the overlap-with-previous-image mask. This is a rule-based combination step, not a learned one.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: This appears to be the first automated (rather than manual) pipeline that handles rod-based counting-strip delineation, since the paper explicitly claims "no existing framework has automatically addressed these issues" and "this study presents the first automated implementation of this process" for rod/strip extraction (stated by authors).
- **Genuinely new**: Applying teacher-student distillation from a modern dense matcher (RoMaV2) specifically to solve GPS-free overlap detection in low-texture savanna imagery, achieving near-teacher accuracy (97.94% vs 98.30%) at ~30x lower CPU cost — a reasonably convincing and well-isolated result since it's measured against the teacher directly with a clear error taxonomy (acquisition failure vs. region error).
- **Supported but modest**: WildDet's improvement over YOLO11 is real but incremental — mAP +1.4%, F1 +1.9%, with the bigger practical win being in counting-error reduction (MAE -21%, mean bias -27.8%) rather than raw detection AP. The authors are reasonably careful to flag that detection-metric gains and counting-metric gains don't move in lockstep (e.g., Reedbuck's AP barely improved but its RMSE dropped 26%), which is an honest and useful distinction rather than a cherry-pick.
- **Claimed but not fully demonstrated**: The paper frames the three modules as an integrated "framework" solving the end-to-end survey-counting problem, but each module is only evaluated in isolation (rod accuracy, overlap accuracy, detection accuracy separately). There's no reported end-to-end evaluation showing final population-count accuracy with all three corrections combined versus, say, raw uncorrected counts or the fully manual process — the paper itself notes WildDet's table "describe[s] the detector comparison; they do not quantify the combined effect of strip masking and overlap removal" (stated).
- **Efficiency claims are well-supported** by direct compute/memory/latency tables (params, GFLOPs, CPU time, memory) for WildMatch vs. RoMaV2 variants — this is one of the more rigorously quantified parts of the paper.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **No end-to-end accuracy evaluation of the full framework** — each module (WildSeg, WildMatch, WildDet) is validated independently; there's no reported metric for final corrected population counts versus ground truth across a full survey run. (stated — explicitly noted for WildDet's table, and implied throughout since no combined-pipeline results table exists)
- **Single geography, single survey, single aircraft/camera setup** — all data comes from one 2023 SRF survey in South Sudan with two specific Cessna 182 + Sony camera configurations and four antelope species. Generalization to other landscapes, rod configurations, camera systems, or species (especially non-ungulates or denser forest/wetland backgrounds) is untested. (inferred)
- **Modules trained and evaluated on a relatively small subset** — only 100 images were used to train WildSeg out of the full training set, and only 2,065 total annotated images were used overall; deep learning detection results (WildDet) rely on patches from 1,564 training images. This is a modest dataset for generalizing detection claims across "large aerial image collections." (inferred, though training-set sizes are stated explicitly)
- **No comparison against other modern lightweight matchers or segmentation baselines** — WildSeg is only compared to vanilla U-Net (a 2015 architecture), and WildMatch is only compared to its own teacher, not to other dense matchers like LoFTR or SuperPoint/SuperGlue that were discussed in the introduction as alternatives. This leaves open whether the gains are from the specific architectural choices or just from distillation vs. a weak baseline. (inferred)
- **WildDet baseline is YOLO11 only** — no comparison to other wildlife-detection-specific architectures cited in their own related work (e.g., WildlifeMapper, Delplanque et al.'s crowd-to-herd counting method), despite those being discussed as relevant prior art. (inferred)
- **No ablation study** — the paper doesn't isolate which WildDet component (dual-scale heads, feature fusion, density branch, empty-image gate) drives the reported gains over YOLO11; all are bundled together in one comparison. (inferred)
- **Rod-detection accuracy metric is narrowly defined** — "accuracy" for WildSeg is defined as continuity + width-within-5% criteria, not pixel-wise IoU/F1 against the ground-truth rod mask, and the paper explicitly notes this "does not" reflect accuracy of the final counting-strip mask. (stated)
- **Modules trained separately, not jointly** — the authors state explicitly that "the framework is a staged workflow rather than a jointly optimized three-network model," meaning errors in one stage (e.g., a missed rod or failed match) propagate uncorrected into the final count rather than being jointly learned/corrected. (stated)
- **Overlap "acquisition failure" rate (~2%) is non-trivial at scale** — across ~250,000 on-transect images, a ~2% failure rate implies thousands of image pairs where overlap can't be automatically determined, with no stated fallback procedure described for these cases beyond flagging. (inferred)
- **Detection counts derived from box counts, not density-map integrals** — the paper notes density-branch outputs were not used for the reported counting metrics ("Predicted counts were obtained from the number of detected bounding boxes, rather than from density-map integrals"), raising the question of what the density branch actually contributes to final survey numbers beyond auxiliary training signal. (stated, but the practical payoff of the density branch is left unaddressed — not addressed in the paper)
