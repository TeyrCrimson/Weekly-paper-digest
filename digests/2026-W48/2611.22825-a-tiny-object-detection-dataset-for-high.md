# A tiny object detection dataset for high-altitude open-water search and rescue

**Authors:** Hui Xu, Chenfei Hou, Henglai Wei, Hao Wang, Yidong Jin, Xiulin Niu, Haoyang Zhang, Hui Zhang, Tianshi Xu

**arXiv:** [2611.22825](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22825)

**Categories:** cs.CV, cs.AI

**Submitted:** 2026-11-17

**Relevance:** 10/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
TinyWOD is a new annotated image dataset (11,359 images, 19,505 instances) purpose-built for detecting tiny lifebuoy-like targets in high-altitude (1.5–2.0 km) open-water search-and-rescue imagery, where objects average just 12.7 pixels across. The authors use scaled-down lifebuoys photographed at closer range to simulate what a full-size target looks like from high altitude, avoiding the cost/legality of actual high-altitude flights. They also benchmark seven detectors and propose a new metric, APtiny, showing existing detectors still localize these tiny targets poorly.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** In open-water search and rescue, aerial platforms want to fly high (1.5–2.0 km) because that covers more ocean per flight — better coverage efficiency. But at that altitude, a floating target like a person or lifebuoy occupies only ~3–15 pixels in the image. Standard object detection struggles with "small objects" generally, but tiny objects (a handful of pixels) are a more extreme case: there's almost no texture/shape information, and water surface noise (waves, glare, reflections, color variation) can look similar to or swamp the target signal. This is harder than typical "small object detection" benchmarks, which usually involve objects of 20–50 pixels.

**Why existing data doesn't help.** You can't just reuse existing maritime datasets (SeaDronesSee, AFO, MOBDrone, SeaPerson, IWHR, Airbus Ship Detection) because they were collected at low altitudes (UAVs under a few hundred meters, or satellite images with different target/sensor characteristics) or in calm/high-contrast water. Their target sizes (tens of pixels) and imaging conditions don't match what a real 1.5–2 km-altitude camera actually sees. Training/testing on them doesn't tell you how a detector will behave at the pixel scale and noise level that matters for high-altitude SAR. One closer dataset (SearchWing, up to 750m) exists but is small and closed-source.

**The core trick: physical scale substitution.** Instead of actually flying at 1.5–2 km (expensive, restricted airspace), the authors shrink the lifebuoy to ~11% of its real size and photograph it from a much shorter distance. This works because of simple pinhole-camera geometry: the apparent pixel size of an object is proportional to (focal length × physical size) / (distance). If you shrink the physical size by the same factor you shrink the distance, the projected pixel size stays the same. So a mini lifebuoy at, say, 160m produces the same pixel footprint as a real lifebuoy at 1.5km — letting them capture "high-altitude-equivalent" imagery using legal, cheap UAV flights (≤120m) and fixed shore cameras (5m up), with zoom lenses tuned so most targets land at 3–15 pixels.

**Data collection and curation.** They filmed in three Zhejiang water bodies with different water colors (green, yellow, blue) and surface states (calm, rough), day and night, totaling 371 hours of footage. From raw video, they adaptively sampled frames (1–15 fps depending on how much motion/change was present, to avoid near-duplicate frames) and manually discarded ~31% of extracted frames for redundancy or low quality, ending with 11,359 images. A two-stage annotation process — four independent annotators label disjoint subsets, then two reviewers jointly check everything — produced 19,505 bounding boxes in COCO, YOLO, and VOC formats. Splits (70/20/10 train/val/test) were stratified by source video so frames from the same video don't leak across splits (preventing inflated performance from near-duplicate train/test frames).

**The APtiny metric.** Standard COCO metrics include APsmall, which covers any object under 32×32 pixels (1024 px²) — but since 99.75% of TinyWOD's objects already fall under that threshold, APsmall basically equals overall AP and tells you nothing special about the extreme tail of tiny objects. So the authors define APtiny, restricted specifically to instances with equivalent side length (sqrt(width×height)) below 15 pixels, averaged over the standard 10 COCO IoU thresholds (0.5 to 0.95). This isolates performance on the hardest, smallest instances within an already-tiny-object dataset, while still using the standard COCO matching/ignoring rules (rather than literally re-scoring a filtered dataset).

**Benchmarking.** They ran seven detectors spanning one-stage (YOLO26), two-stage (Faster R-CNN variants), and end-to-end transformer-based (DINO, H-DETR, RT-DETR, OWRT-DETR) architectures, evaluating AP, AP50, AP75, APsmall, APtiny, precision, recall, and AR100. Across all models, AP50 >> AP75, meaning detectors can roughly tell "something is there" but struggle to localize it precisely — expected since a 1–2 pixel bounding-box error has an outsized effect on IoU when the box itself is only ~13 pixels wide.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new contribution**: a dataset explicitly targeting the 3–15 pixel object-size regime for maritime SAR, which is a real gap — prior overwater datasets cluster at 20–175 pixel average box sizes (per their own Table 1 comparison).
- **Genuinely clever methodology**: the physical-scale/distance substitution trick (shrink the target, shrink the distance proportionally) is a legitimate and cheap way to approximate high-altitude imaging characteristics without needing actual high-altitude flights — this is a real contribution to how such datasets could be built, not just a bigger label effort.
- **APtiny is a reasonable, if incremental, metric contribution** — addressing a real limitation (APsmall saturating) of the standard COCO small-object bucket when objects are this extreme.
- **What the evidence actually supports**: the benchmarking shows all seven tested detectors can achieve non-trivial AP/APtiny (e.g., OWRT-DETR reaches AP 0.512, APtiny 0.440), which the authors use to claim the dataset is "usable" for training/evaluation. This is a modest, defensible claim (the dataset isn't un-learnable) — it does **not** establish that any detector solves high-altitude tiny-object SAR well, and the authors appropriately note AR100 (0.351–0.596) and AP75 are low across the board, i.e., detectors still struggle with recall and precise localization.
- The claim that this generalizes to real high-altitude imagery (not just simulated-scale imagery) is **not demonstrated** — it's an assumption underlying the whole construction, explicitly flagged by the authors themselves as unverified (see caveats).

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Stated**: The lifebuoys are scaled-down physical replicas designed to match *projected pixel size*, not real high-altitude appearance (lighting/atmospheric scattering/sensor response at true 1.5–2km distance may differ) — "transferability of detection results to such imagery therefore remains to be evaluated."
- **Stated**: Single object category only (lifebuoys) — no persons, boats, debris, or other SAR-relevant targets.
- **Stated**: Geographic coverage limited to three water bodies in Zhejiang Province, China; not validated across other regions, oceans, or open-sea (vs. inland/coastal) conditions.
- **Stated**: Nighttime images are underrepresented relative to daytime.
- **Stated**: Frames were adaptively sampled and manually screened, so released images are not a uniform/representative video sequence — sequence-based or tracking analyses should account for this.
- **Inferred**: The entire premise rests on pinhole-camera geometry equivalence (shrinking object size and distance proportionally) — this does not account for atmospheric haze/attenuation, sensor noise, motion blur, or optical aberrations that genuinely differ between a short-range shot of a small object and a true long-range shot of a full-size object at 1.5–2km through more atmosphere; the paper doesn't address or test this physical-equivalence assumption empirically (no real high-altitude validation set is compared against).
- **Inferred**: Benchmarking uses only standard/mainstream detectors without hyperparameter tuning details specific to tiny-object settings (e.g., no mention of anchor-scale tuning, tiling/slicing inference, or tiny-object-specific loss functions like RFLA/NWD cited in their own related work) — so the reported numbers may understate what's achievable with tiny-object-specialized techniques, making this a baseline rather than an upper-bound demonstration.
- **Inferred**: No cross-dataset generalization test (e.g., train on TinyWOD, test on SeaDronesSee or vice versa) is reported, so "reusability and utility" claims rest solely on in-distribution benchmark numbers from TinyWOD's own train/val/test split.
- **Inferred**: The dataset authors are also the benchmark-paper authors and one cited top-performing detector (OWRT-DETR, ref [1]) appears to be their own prior work, which did best on AP/APtiny — a potential (mild) conflict of interest in the comparison, though not necessarily a flaw in the dataset itself.
- **Stated (implicit in framing)**: Negative images (6.53%, no object) are a small minority, and most positive images contain exactly one object (76.73% have ≤1), which may limit evaluation of dense multi-target scenes or false-positive robustness in cluttered multi-object conditions.
