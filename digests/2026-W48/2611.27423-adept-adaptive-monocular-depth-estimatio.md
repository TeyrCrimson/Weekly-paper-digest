# ADEPT: Adaptive Monocular Depth Estimation via Model-In-The-Loop Photometric Tuning with Capture Risk Prediction

**Authors:** GiHyun Kim, JinMo Yang, Hyung-Sin Kim

**arXiv:** [2611.27423](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27423)

**Categories:** cs.RO, cs.AI

**Submitted:** 2026-11-20

**Relevance:** 8/10 — matched: Metric and temporally-consistent monocular depth

---

## TL;DR
ADEPT trains a lightweight neural "risk predictor" that scores how good a camera's exposure/gain setting is for a *frozen* monocular depth model, using disagreement with the best-of-group reference capture (RRD) as a learnable proxy for ground-truth depth error. A contextual Gaussian-process bandit then uses this score (plus motion/photometric safety constraints) to pick exposure and gain online, on a real mobile robot, without ever seeing ground-truth depth at deployment. Across nine scene/illumination/motion conditions, it beats built-in auto-exposure by 14.6% and the best prior learned exposure controller by 10.0% in depth accuracy (Abs_rel).

## First-principles breakdown

**The underlying problem.** Monocular depth estimation (MDE) infers a depth map from a single RGB image. The quality of that inference depends heavily on how the image was captured: exposure time and sensor gain. Too little exposure/gain → dark, noisy image, low signal. Too much exposure → motion blur (especially on a moving robot) or saturation (blown-out highlights with no information). There's a genuine physical tradeoff: longer exposure collects more light but is more vulnerable to blur when the camera is moving or turning. The "right" setting depends on the scene, the lighting, and the current motion — it's not static.

**Why prior approaches fall short.** Three broad families existed:
1. *Classical image-statistics controllers* (entropy, gradient sharpness, brightness histograms) optimize for a "nice-looking" image, not necessarily what the specific downstream depth model needs. A sharp, well-exposed image by generic metrics may still confuse a particular neural depth model.
2. *Learned task-aware controllers* (e.g., reinforcement learning policies rewarded by visual odometry performance or feature-matching counts) tie the reward to a different downstream task (SLAM/VO), not depth accuracy, and they're trained once rather than reasoning about the specific depth model's blind spots.
3. *LENS* (prior frozen-model-feedback method) uses a frozen classifier's confidence as a signal, but only in quasi-static settings (not a continuously moving robot with motion-dependent constraints), and confidence from classification doesn't translate directly into "how wrong will depth be."
4. *Depth uncertainty estimators* (gradient-based, test-time augmentation) estimate uncertainty about the network's own prediction but aren't necessarily predictive of ranking *physical camera settings* against each other — the paper shows empirically these correlate poorly with actual error differences across camera settings.

**ADEPT's core idea — Reference-Relative Disagreement (RRD).** Instead of trying to predict absolute depth error (which requires ground truth, unavailable at deployment), they predict something constructible offline and usable without ground truth online:
- During data collection, for a fixed robot viewpoint ("scene state" s), they capture images under many exposure/gain settings and know ground truth depth for all of them (offline only).
- For each state, the setting with lowest actual depth error becomes the "reference" c*(s).
- Excess error Δ Abs_rel(s,c) = how much worse any other setting's error is relative to that reference. Because the scene-dependent terms cancel out when comparing within the same fixed state, this excess error preserves the *relative ranking* of settings — so you don't need to know the absolute best setting, just rank candidates sensibly against the best-known one.
- RRD, D(s,c), is defined similarly but using the *model's own predictions* instead of ground truth: how much the candidate's depth prediction disagrees with the reference setting's depth prediction (normalized, pixel-averaged). This is still constructible offline (needs the reference capture), but crucially it can be *predicted* at test time by a network that has learned the mapping from image+context to disagreement — without needing the actual reference image.

**The predictor.** A small FiLM-conditioned network (165K parameters) takes: frozen MDE encoder features from the current image, plus camera settings, motion state, and illumination state. It outputs a predicted mean and variance of RRD, trained with Gaussian negative-log-likelihood (so it also learns how confident/uncertain it is) plus a ranking (listwise cross-entropy) loss that explicitly teaches it to order candidate settings correctly, not just predict the right magnitude. The ranking loss matters a lot — removing it increases validation regret by ~33%.

Why does this work without ground truth online? Because once trained, the network has learned a general mapping from "features + context" → "how risky is this capture for depth," calibrated against many reference-relative examples, and can apply that to a brand-new, previously-unseen capture.

**The controller.** At each timestep, the predictor turns the current image into a risk-sensitive score q_t = predicted-mean-RRD + κ·predicted-std (a conservative, uncertainty-penalized cost). This is fed into a contextual Gaussian Process (GP) bandit — a model that, given a history of (setting, context, score) tuples, predicts the expected score and uncertainty for untried settings, balancing exploitation (pick low expected risk) against exploration (try uncertain settings) via a UCB-like rule. The GP's kernel factors in camera setting similarity, motion state, illumination state, and recency (temporal decay), so it adapts its "model" of risk as conditions change. On top of this, hard safety constraints are applied: a motion-dependent cap on exposure time (to prevent blur during fast motion/turns) and a photometric guard (to prevent severe over/under-exposure, based on projected saturation/shadow ratios). The final setting is chosen by minimizing (GP mean − exploration bonus + switching penalty) among admissible settings — the switching penalty discourages needless flip-flopping.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: the RRD surrogate target — supervising a risk predictor using disagreement with a *data-driven best-reference capture* rather than direct ground-truth error or generic uncertainty. This sidesteps the need for ground truth or reference images at deployment while still being empirically grounded in real depth-error rankings.
- **Genuinely new**: treating camera control for MDE as a *sequential contextual bandit* problem with a frozen model's own feedback, combined with physically motivated admissibility filters (motion-based exposure caps, photometric saturation guards) — rather than static image-quality heuristics or task-agnostic RL policies.
- **Evidence supports**: the learned predictor correlates much better with actual ground-truth-based error rankings than training-free uncertainty baselines (TTA, gradient-based) — e.g., Pearson correlation 0.95 vs 0.31 and -0.14 for Metric-DAv2 on validation. This is a fairly strong, well-isolated result (Table II) and the ablation (Table IV) supports that RRD (vs. direct ground-truth supervision) actually *generalizes better* to validation scenes (lower regret), which is a nontrivial and interesting claim — a weaker/noisier training signal transfers better than the "ideal" one.
- **Evidence partially supports**: end-to-end online improvement over auto-exposure (14.6%) and over the best prior learned controller, Zhang et al. (10.0%), is shown via one robot, one trajectory, 9 condition combinations, 5 repeated trials each. The improvement is real but the margin over the next-best baseline (Zhang et al.) is modest and not uniform — ADEPT loses to Zhang et al. in some normal-illumination scenes (stated in the text: "Zhang et al. performs best in Validation Scene 1... under normal illumination").
- **Claimed but secondary**: the admissibility filters and switching penalty measurably help (Table V, ~4.7% Abs_rel reduction combining both filters vs. neither), supporting that naive GP exploration alone would occasionally pick bad settings.
- Runtime: ADEPT is faster than the other learned controllers (298ms vs 322–349ms per frame) but still slower than doing nothing special (built-in AE at 206ms), so there's a real compute cost being traded for accuracy.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Single robot, single environment layout family** (stated): all data from one AgileX LIMO Pro robot traversing a fixed trajectory in a lab-like setting with three training/three validation "scene configurations" that are described as sharing structural layout but differing in object identity/arrangement — not fundamentally different environments (e.g., no outdoor, no different robot platforms). Generalization beyond this setup is untested.
- **Small, discrete action space** (stated/inferred): only 16 exposure×gain combinations (4×4 grid) were evaluated; real cameras often have finer or continuous control ranges, and it's unclear how well the GP/admissibility approach scales to larger or continuous action spaces. Not addressed in the paper.
- **Illumination categorization is manual** (stated): "illumination states are manually assigned" — this is not an automatically sensed input in the reported experiments, raising a question about full autonomy of the system as deployed (though the abstract/method imply illumination is part of context the model could sense). The paper doesn't clarify how illumination state would be obtained automatically in a general deployment — not addressed in the paper.
- **Small evaluation scale for online experiments** (inferred): Table III results are based on 5 repetitions per condition across 3 validation scenes × 3 illumination levels — a relatively small sample for drawing strong statistical conclusions, and no significance testing (e.g., p-values, confidence intervals beyond std) is reported for the online comparisons.
- **Baseline currency/strength** (inferred): the main learned-controller baseline, Zhang et al. (2024) "DRL-Feat," is relatively recent and reasonably strong (it actually wins in some conditions), which is good; however, Shin et al. (2019) is an older classical method and a weaker baseline — the paper's headline comparison is against auto-exposure (14.6%) and the single best other learned method (10.0%), and it's worth noting ADEPT doesn't uniformly dominate Zhang et al. across all 9 conditions (only 8/9 lowest Abs_rel, 7/9 highest δ1.25, per the text).
- **Frozen depth model dependency** (stated/inferred): results are reported with Depth Anything v2 (Small) only; the offline selection experiments use both relative and metric variants, but online robot experiments only use the affine-invariant variant. Whether gains transfer to other MDE backbones (different architectures, larger/smaller models) is not addressed in the paper.
- **Supervision ablation nuance** (stated but double-edged): the paper frames RRD's better validation-regret performance (vs. direct ground-truth Δ Abs_rel supervision) as a strength ("transfers without assuming mathematical equivalence to the desired objective"), but it's also a slightly surprising/unintuitive result — a proxy target outperforming the "true" target on held-out scenes — and the paper doesn't deeply investigate *why* this generalization gap exists beyond noting it empirically; could partly be an artifact of limited scene diversity (inferred).
- **Latency/compute overhead** (stated): ADEPT's control pipeline adds ~66ms/frame vs <1ms for built-in AE, and total pipeline runs at only ~3.35 FPS versus 4.86 FPS for AE — a real-time cost that could matter for higher-speed robots or tighter control loops; the paper doesn't discuss implications for faster motion profiles than tested (max 1.0 m/s).
- **Reference registration process could introduce selection bias** (inferred): the "best evaluated reference" per scene state is chosen only from the 16 discretely sampled settings, which may not include the true optimal exposure/gain; also registration required strict overlap/ECC thresholds and discarded failed registrations, meaning the training/evaluation data excludes harder-to-register (e.g., fast motion) cases, potentially biasing results toward favorable conditions. Not explicitly discussed as a limitation in the paper.
