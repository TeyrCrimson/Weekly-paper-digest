# Aerial Person Detection under Edge Constraints: Held-Out Evaluation and Edge-Ground Verification

**Authors:** Raghu Venkat, Tricha Anjali

**arXiv:** [2611.20127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20127)

**Categories:** cs.CV, cs.RO

**Submitted:** 2026-11-15

**Relevance:** 8/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
The paper is a measurement study, not a new detector: it benchmarks person detection for search-and-rescue drones under realistic data splits, finding that proper held-out evaluation roughly halves reported recall versus the leaked public splits. A standard detector (YOLO11n) beats a custom 46K-parameter edge detector on accuracy but costs far more compute and still blows through its false-alarm budget at new locations; sending YOLO's top-16 crops to a small ground model for re-scoring recovers some recall (+8.8 points) without a statistically clear reduction in false alarms. A still person in earth-toned clothing was almost never detected by either model at 60m+.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** A search drone looking for a missing person from 100m+ altitude sees that person as a handful of pixels. The drone has a small onboard computer (Raspberry Pi-class), limited battery, and a narrow radio link back to base — so you can't just run a huge model on every frame or stream raw 4K video. The paper asks three questions: (1) how well does a tiny onboard detector actually generalize to places/recordings it wasn't trained on, (2) how does a standard, heavier detector compare and what does it cost, and (3) if the small onboard detector isn't good enough, how much does forwarding a few candidate image crops to a bigger "ground" model help, and what does that cost in bandwidth?

**Why prior evaluation is misleading (data leakage).** Public aerial-SAR datasets like HIT-UAV (thermal) and HERIDAL (color mountain-search images) are typically evaluated with "frame-level" splits — train/test split at the level of individual frames. But frames from the same flight, taken seconds apart, are near-duplicates (same background, same lighting, same person in nearly the same pose). If such near-duplicate frames end up on both sides of the split, the test set isn't really "unseen" — the model has effectively already seen that scene. The authors checked this directly: in HIT-UAV, they inferred discrete "recording blocks" from frame IDs (one block = one lighting/altitude session) and found every block appears in both train and test in the official split. In HERIDAL, they found 66 of 103 published "test" images are byte-identical to training images (and some "different" test locations are secretly aliases of training locations). This is the classic leakage problem: a model memorizes the specific scene rather than learning to generalize.

**The fix: held-out blocks/locations.** Instead of splitting by frame, they split by whole inferred recording block (thermal) or whole geographic location (HERIDAL), so no scene/location appears on both sides. This is a stricter, more realistic test of generalization — it simulates what actually happens operationally: a drone flying over ground it has never seen. The effect is dramatic: recall at a fixed false-alarm rate drops from 32.7% (leaky split) to 12.3% (held-out blocks) — i.e., the "real" generalization performance is less than half what the leaky benchmark suggested.

**The cue-gated small detector.** Rather than running a neural net densely over every pixel of every frame (expensive), they first run a cheap, non-learned "cue": a local-contrast/blob-size filter that flags small regions that stand out from their background (a proxy for "something that might be a person-shaped warm/bright blob"). Only these ~128 candidate regions get passed to a tiny 46,000-parameter CNN classifier (a handful of 3×3 conv layers) that scores each crop. This is a classic cascade/attention mechanism (like Viola-Jones face detection): cheap filter first, expensive-ish model only on survivors. This "gated" approach finds more people (10.4%) at a similar false-alarm rate than running the same tiny network everywhere/densely (5.7%), while using about a fifth of the compute — because the cue lets the network operate at a lower, more sensitive threshold without flooding the false-alarm budget.

**Standard detector comparison.** YOLO11n (a modern, 2.6M-parameter single-class object detector family, pretrained on COCO and fine-tuned per-fold) is trained on the exact same held-out folds. It dramatically outperforms both the dense and gated tiny-network arms (54.5% recall vs. 10.4%/5.7%), but at ~11x the arithmetic cost of the gated detector. On 4K field footage, at high altitude it needs to run multiple passes per frame (tiling the image into 24 sub-windows, or upscaling input resolution) to find small distant people — each of these "passes" costs roughly as much compute as one full dense inference, so their best high-altitude configuration costs 4–24x a single pass.

**Threshold transfer problem.** A detector's "score cutoff" (threshold) for flagging a detection is tuned on some validation location to hit a target false-alarm rate (e.g., 1 false alarm per frame). The paper shows that this threshold, when frozen and applied to new, unseen locations, badly overshoots the target (1.7–3.4x more false alarms than intended) — because background clutter (rocks, bushes) varies by location. This means a detector can look good on paper but spam an operator with false alerts in a genuinely new environment.

**Edge–ground split (the "verification" step).** Instead of sending full video over the narrow radio link, the drone sends only its top-16 highest-scoring crops (small JPEGs, ~154KB total per frame) to a ground station. A second, still-small (842K-parameter) CNN on the ground re-scores just those 16 crops — it's a second-opinion / cascade-verification stage, trained independently on the same splits, with its threshold frozen on a separate validation location. This is standard "split computing" / edge-cloud inference, but used here as a verification cascade rather than to split one network's layers. Re-scoring with the ground model raised recall (44.9%→53.7%) over the same 16 candidates, confirmed by a paired bootstrap analysis across 15 locations (+8.8 points, CI [+2.4,+12.5], not crossing zero). However, the parallel drop in false alarms (0.86→0.64 per frame) was not statistically distinguishable from zero — i.e., recall improved reliably, but "fewer false alarms" is not established.

**Evaluation hygiene.** The authors pre-registered ("declared before they ran") their primary experiments and explicitly flagged exploratory/post-hoc analyses (e.g., the HIT-UAV leaky-vs-held-out comparison and some HERIDAL curve read-outs) as such — an unusually rigorous practice for this kind of applied CV paper, closer to pre-registration norms in experimental science.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new/useful contribution: an evaluation-integrity audit.** Demonstrating concrete leakage in two widely-used SAR benchmarks (HIT-UAV block overlap, HERIDAL duplicate/alias images) and quantifying how much it inflates reported recall is a real, actionable finding for the field — not just a claim, they show byte-identical duplicates and provide a public "truth register" of the leakage.
- **Claimed vs. supported: gated tiny-detector advantage.** The claim that cue-gating improves a small detector over a dense pass is supported with a paired statistical comparison (+4.7 points, CI excludes zero) — this is solid evidence, though the absolute recall (10.4%) is still low.
- **Claimed vs. supported: ground verification helps recall but not clearly false alarms.** The paper is careful here — they explicitly call out that the recall gain (+8.8 pts) is statistically supported via paired bootstrap, but the false-alarm reduction is not (CI crosses zero), and one single location contributes about half the recall gain. They avoid over-claiming a "two birds one stone" result, which is a mark of honest reporting rather than cherry-picking.
- **Standard detector wins on raw accuracy but the paper does NOT claim it's deployable as-is** — it explicitly flags that YOLO11n's power/latency on the actual target hardware (Raspberry Pi) was never measured, and its false-alarm threshold doesn't transfer across locations without help.
- **Operationally important negative result:** still people in earth-toned clothing are detected by both models in 0–7.5% of frames at ≥60m altitude — a practically crucial limitation for real search-and-rescue (most of the paper's "accuracy" numbers are about walking/moving or brightly-dressed people, which is a much easier case).
- **The paper does not propose a new architecture or validate an autonomous search system** — this is explicitly scoped as a measurement/evaluation paper, not a systems contribution.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Thermal recording blocks are inferred, not verified** (stated): block boundaries in HIT-UAV are guessed from frame-ID patterns, not validated against actual flight logs, so held-out "blocks" aren't confirmed to be independent flights or sites.
- **Field footage is extremely small** (stated): one site, two afternoons, one or two people total, with sample sizes per condition as low as n=5–83 and "no interval was computed" for some still-person groups.
- **Post-hoc / exploratory analyses mixed with pre-registered ones** (stated): several key comparisons (e.g., the leaky-vs-held-out HIT-UAV comparison, the HERIDAL top-16 board-score row, the paired ground-model analysis) are explicitly labeled exploratory or post hoc — meaning the headline "+8.8 points" effect has not been replicated in an independently pre-declared run.
- **Confounded still-person experiment** (stated): altitude, clothing, pose, occlusion, resolution, and training data all vary together across the still-person clips, so the paper cannot attribute the near-zero detection rate to any single cause.
- **No power/latency measurement for the actual comparison baseline** (stated): YOLO11n, the best-performing detector, was never run on the target Raspberry Pi hardware — all power numbers are for the authors' own tiny network only, under one governor setting, on a bench, not in flight.
- **Software/version inconsistency across runs** (stated): the "Y2" YOLO run used different PyTorch/Ultralytics versions and a different machine than earlier runs, and the authors warn these shouldn't be mixed, which fragments the evidence base across experiments.
- **Single software environment, unreplicated** (stated): the paired ground-verification gain comes from "one run in one software environment" and "has not been repeated" — i.e., no independent replication or multiple seeds yet.
- **False-alarm metric doesn't map to operator experience** (stated): "false alarms per frame" isn't the same as distinct alerts an operator would see, since one background object can trigger repeatedly across frames — the paper admits this gap is unresolved.
- **No comparison to a compressed-full-frame alternative** (stated): the claimed bandwidth benefit of sending only 16 crops is not actually validated against the realistic alternative (sending compressed full frames), so "bandwidth savings" is asserted but not measured against the right baseline.
- **AGPL-3.0 licensing of YOLO11n** (stated) makes it unusable as-is for closed/commercial deployment — a practical caveat on the paper's own recommended baseline.
- **Small-model baseline comparison could be seen as weak** (inferred): the custom 46K-parameter detector is compared mainly against a tiny dense version of itself and against YOLO11n, but no mid-sized or other modern lightweight architectures (e.g., YOLO-nano variants, MobileNet-based detectors) are benchmarked, so "best cheap option" is not established, only "better than naive dense of itself."
- **No thermal imaging tested on the still-person-in-earth-tones failure case** (stated as future work) despite the authors noting thermal (AOS) has succeeded where color failed in prior work — meaning the paper's most actionable finding (color fails on still/camouflaged people) is not followed up with the one method (thermal) known to help, within this paper.
- **Cherry-picked-looking headline numbers** (inferred): the abstract foregrounds the statistically significant recall gain (+8.8 points) prominently while the companion false-alarm result (not distinguishable from zero) is mentioned but less emphasized — though to the authors' credit this is also stated plainly in the body and discussion, so it's a presentation caveat rather than a hidden one.
