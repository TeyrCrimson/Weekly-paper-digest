# What the Averages Hide: Monocular Metric Depth Models Still Fail in Everyday Scenes, Mostly on Scale

**Authors:** Hualie Jiang, Yujie Luo, Ziyang Song, Ximeng Li, Zhiqiang Lou, Rui Xu

**arXiv:** [2611.20518](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20518)

**Categories:** cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-11-16

**Relevance:** 10/10 — matched: Metric and temporally-consistent monocular depth

---

## TL;DR
This paper argues that the standard way of evaluating zero-shot monocular metric depth models — averaging accuracy scores across datasets — hides whether models actually fail outright on many images, and if so, why. By keeping per-image results and decomposing error into "structure" (shape) vs. "scale" (absolute size), the authors show that even the best of 18 model/intrinsics combinations still fails outright on 7.7% of everyday images, and that the vast majority of failures are pure scale errors, not structural ones. They further show that a disproportionate share of each model's error comes from a small set of images that nearly all models fail on together ("shared failures"), concentrated in specific HAMMER and DIODE scenes.

## First-principles breakdown

**The task:** Monocular metric depth estimation means predicting, from a single RGB image, the actual real-world distance (in meters) to every pixel — not just relative depth ("closer/farther") but absolute scale. This is fundamentally hard because a single 2D image is scale-ambiguous: a small object close to the camera can look identical to a large object far away (this is the classic size/distance confound). Recent models (Metric3D, Depth Pro, MoGe-2/3, UniDepth, UniK3D, etc.) try to solve this zero-shot — i.e., generalize to new scenes without retraining — often by exploiting camera intrinsics (focal length) as auxiliary information, since focal length breaks part of the size/distance ambiguity.

**How these models are normally evaluated, and why that's misleading:** The standard practice computes two numbers per dataset: (1) "metric" accuracy (δ1 = fraction of pixels within 25% of ground truth, Rel = mean relative error) using the raw predicted depth, and (2) "scale-aligned" accuracy, where the prediction is first rescaled by an optimal scalar s* (computed by comparing to ground truth) before measuring error. The gap between these two tells you roughly how much error is "scale" vs. "structure," but then these numbers are averaged across many images and many datasets into one or two final numbers per model. The problem: a mean doesn't tell you if errors are evenly spread (every image slightly wrong) or concentrated (most images fine, a few catastrophically wrong) — and catastrophic failures matter more for real applications than small average errors. Also, the scale factor s* itself is discarded after use, throwing away information about whether the model is systematically over- or under-estimating depth, and whether that bias is consistent or erratic.

**What this paper does differently — three fixes:**
1. **Failure rate as the headline metric**: define an image as a "failure" if metric δ1 < 0.5 (fewer than half the pixels within 25% of truth). This directly counts how often the task catastrophically breaks, rather than hiding it in an average.
2. **Separate structure from scale, with richer scale statistics**: Structure error = scale-aligned δ1/Rel (how good is the shape once you let it rescale optimally). Scale error = statistics of s* itself: *bias* (mean log(1/s*), tells you systematic over/underestimation direction), *dispersion* (how inconsistent s* is across images — a wide spread means no single correction factor would fix it), and *absolute error* (mean |ln s*|, so overestimate and underestimate don't cancel out).
3. **Cross-model failure comparison**: Instead of evaluating one model in isolation, run 11 different architectures on the same 4,406 images and count, per image, how many architectures fail on it (k). Images where almost all models fail (k≥6) are called "shared failures" — a genuine unsolved difficulty; images only one or two models fail are "model-specific" — a weakness of that particular model, since others solve it fine.

**What they found by applying this:** Running these 11 architectures (with/without ground-truth intrinsics, 18 total configurations) across 7 standard everyday-scene benchmarks (NYUv2, KITTI, ETH3D, iBims-1, DDAD, DIODE, HAMMER — the same ones used in the MoGe-2 paper), they find failure rates between 7.7% and 44%. When they correct the scale on failed images using the ground-truth-derived s* (an idealized, best-case correction), 98.3% of failures disappear — meaning almost all the remaining error is a scale problem, not a structural/shape problem. They also find that 7.3% of images are "shared failures" that most/all models get wrong — concentrated in specific scenes (HAMMER empty-table shots lacking size-cue objects, and some DIODE outdoor/scale-ambiguous scenes) — and these few images account for over a third of the leading models' error, far more than a random sample of the same size would.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: The per-image, cross-model failure-rate framework itself — treating "does this image fail" as the primary unit of analysis instead of averaged scores — plus the decomposition of scale error into signed bias/dispersion/absolute-error statistics (rather than discarding s* after alignment). This is an evaluation methodology contribution, not a new depth model.
- **Supported by evidence**: The claim that most metric failures are scale failures (78.8% pure scale, 98.3% fixable by ideal scale correction) is well supported by the large-scale per-image analysis across 18 model evaluations (79,308 image×model pairs).
- **Supported by evidence**: Shared failures are real and non-random — a permutation test shows models correlate on which images they fail (φ ≈ 0.23–0.24 vs ~0 after shuffling), and a held-out test (selecting "hard" images using only models from other architecture families) confirms these images carry disproportionate error (34-40% vs 14-18% for random images) — this isn't just circular self-selection.
- **Claimed but softer**: The HAMMER "missing objects hurt scale estimation" finding (adding objects reduces scale error for 10/11 models) rests on a one-sided sign-flip test with only 6 scenes (p=0.016 to 0.11 depending on model) — directionally consistent but statistically thin given the tiny scene count.
- **Practical recommendation** (not just a finding): report failure rates, structure/scale separately, and stratify results by how many models fail on each image — a call to change reporting norms in the field, though whether the field adopts it is unverified/future work.
- Larger model size mostly improves *scale* estimation, not structure (structure is already near-ceiling at small sizes) — but this is based on only 2-3 sizes per family and isn't always monotonic (two families got worse metric δ1 at the largest size despite better structure).

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **(stated)** The size-scaling conclusions are confounded: "each family has only two or three sizes, and we evaluated only the one set of weights released by the authors for each size, so size itself cannot be separated from training settings that change with size."
- **(stated)** Bootstrap confidence intervals for the per-size comparisons and HAMMER scene-level comparisons are acknowledged to be "optimistic" / "unstable" because they resample only 6-7 recorded scenes, and because images within a recorded scene are highly similar (violating the independence assumption of per-image resampling in Appendix I).
- **(stated)** Training-data overlap: DDAD, for instance, appears in the training data of 5 of the 11 models (DepthVLM, Metric3D-v2, DA3METRIC, MA-DepthMap, MA-PointMap), so results on DDAD are not purely zero-shot for those models — the authors flag this explicitly when interpreting DDAD results.
- **(stated)** The sign-flip test for the HAMMER object/no-object comparison is based on only 6 scenes, limiting achievable statistical power (smallest possible one-sided p = 1/64 ≈ 0.016); several models' results (e.g., UniDepthV2, DepthVLM) don't reach conventional significance.
- **(stated)** The "per-image best" reference (0.2% failure rate if you always pick the best of 18 evaluations per image) is explicitly called "optimistic," since a model might appear to succeed on an image only because its global bias happens to coincidentally fit that image, not because it correctly estimated scale.
- **(inferred)** Evaluation is restricted to 7 "everyday-scene" benchmarks explicitly chosen to exclude aerial, endoscopic, or other specialized domains (stated as a scoping decision), so the "metric depth is not solved" conclusion may not generalize beyond this scene distribution — though this is a deliberate, disclosed scope limit rather than a hidden one.
- **(inferred)** Whether the scale ambiguity on hard images (e.g., HAMMER empty-table shots) is in principle recoverable from the image at all is explicitly left unresolved by the authors themselves ("left open by these results") — meaning part of the paper's central "difficulty" framing is diagnostic, not a solved or even fully characterized problem.
- **(inferred)** The ground-truth depth itself is taken as given without independent verification ("we take the released ground truth of each benchmark as given and do not check it independently" — stated), so any residual sensor noise or calibration error in e.g. DIODE's laser scanner or HAMMER's robot-arm rig could inflate apparent model "failures," though the TUM RGB-D consistency check (reprojection error far smaller than observed model scale errors) partially mitigates this for the diagnostic sets.
- **(inferred)** No new method or fix is proposed for the scale-estimation problem identified — the paper is purely diagnostic/evaluative; practitioners get a better way to measure the problem but not a better model to solve it (not addressed in the paper beyond recommendations for future reporting).
