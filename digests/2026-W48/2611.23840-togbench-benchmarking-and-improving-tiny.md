# TOGBench: Benchmarking and Improving Tiny-Object Generation Fidelity in Aerial Imagery

**Authors:** Chanhwi Jeong, Dongmin You, Seungwook Kim

**arXiv:** [2611.23840](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23840)

**Categories:** cs.CV, cs.AI

**Submitted:** 2026-11-18

**Relevance:** 8/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
TOGBench is a new benchmark that measures how faithfully layout-to-image generators render *tiny* objects (vehicles, ships, aircraft) in aerial imagery — something existing metrics (FID, CLIPScore, human-preference scores) completely miss because tiny objects occupy too few pixels to register. The authors diagnose three specific mechanisms by which a state-of-the-art generator (FLUX.1-dev + OminiControl adapter) loses the layout signal for small objects, and propose two fixes — Layout-Weighted Loss (training-time) and Condition-Layout Attention Gain (inference-time, training-free) — that together raise their Detectability score from 72.2 to 85.3 without hurting appearance quality. The evaluation is confined to one generator, one dataset (AI-TOD-v2), and includes a controlled validation study showing the metric tracks known degradations.

## First-principles breakdown

**The underlying generation task.** Diffusion models generate images by learning to reverse a noise-adding process: start from a clean image, add Gaussian noise over many steps, and train a network to predict the noise at each step so it can be removed at generation time. "Layout-to-image" generation adds a constraint: besides a text prompt, the model is given a *layout* — a list of bounding boxes, each labeled with an object class (e.g., "ship" at this box) — and must generate an image that places exactly those objects, of the correct class, at those coordinates. This is useful for aerial/satellite imagery because labeled aerial data is scarce, so synthetic layout-conditioned images could augment detector training sets.

**Why aerial imagery is special.** Aerial/satellite photos are taken from far overhead, so real-world objects like cars or boats are often only 8–20 pixels wide in an 800×800 image. Standard generative models were built and evaluated on scenes where the "interesting" objects are large and prominent (a face, a dog). When the interesting content is a handful of pixels, two separate things go wrong: the model has very little visual information to work with, and — critically for this paper — nobody was actually measuring whether it got those objects right at all.

**Why existing evaluation misses this.** FID-style metrics compare the *statistics of entire images* (feature distributions over whole pictures) between real and fake sets; a few dozen malformed pixels in an 800×800 image barely move the aggregate statistic. Prompt-following metrics (CLIPScore, GenEval) and human-preference reward models (PickScore, ImageReward) judge global semantic plausibility of a scene, not pixel-level correctness of a specific tiny region. The paper empirically confirms this blind spot: CLIPScore and PickScore barely move across generator variants that differ by 25+ points on the paper's own tiny-object metric.

**TOGBench's mechanism.** Instead of scoring the whole image, TOGBench scores *each individual object* that the layout asked for, by comparing it to real objects of the same class. It splits fidelity into five axes:
- *Presence*: was an object detected near the requested box at all? (matching done via a size-scaled distance tolerance, so small boxes aren't unfairly penalized for strict matching, then greedy nearest-first assignment between layout boxes and detector outputs.)
- *Localization*: for objects that were found, how well does the detection's box overlap the requested box (IoU)?
- *Class*: among matched detections, was the predicted class correct?
- *Size*: is the detected object's size close to the requested box's size (ratio must be within a tolerance band)?
- *Appearance*: crop the (real vs. generated) object region and embed it with a satellite-pretrained self-supervised vision model (DINOv3); measure the average distance of a generated crop to its nearest real crops of the same class. This captures "does it *look* like a real ship" independent of whether a detector found it.

Each detection-based axis is normalized relative to how real objects score on the same axis (using the same detectors), so that a detector's own blind spots on real images don't get blamed on the generator — only the *gap* between real and generated performance counts. The four detection axes are then combined with a "non-compensatory" power mean (p=2), meaning a generator can't hide a bad axis by being great on another — all four must be decent to get a high score. This combined number is "Detectability." Appearance is reported as a separate number since it captures something different (texture/shape realism vs. whether the object was drawn/placed at all), and the authors show the two scores respond to different kinds of corruption (e.g., inpainting tanks Detectability but barely touches Appearance; blur does the opposite).

**Validating the benchmark.** Before trusting TOGBench, the authors run controlled sanity checks: degrade real images with JPEG compression, blur, noise, or inpainting at increasing severity, and confirm both scores monotonically worsen. They also perturb only the layout (drop boxes, rescale boxes, relabel classes) and confirm each axis responds specifically to the perturbation that should affect it (e.g., dropping boxes raises only the Presence-failure score), showing the axes are measuring what they claim to measure, not some confounded artifact.

**Diagnosing why tiny objects fail.** The authors trace the generator's failure to three mechanical causes in a specific architecture (FLUX.1-dev + OminiControl LoRA adapter, which injects the layout as a stream of "condition tokens" that join a single shared self-attention with image/text tokens):
1. *Training objective dilution*: the denoising loss is averaged uniformly over all pixel/latent locations. Since object-covered regions are only ~1.5% of the image area, they get only ~1.5% of the training signal's weight — even though those locations are measurably harder to denoise than background.
2. *Attention starvation*: image tokens split their attention across text, image, and condition (layout) tokens. Despite the condition stream having as many tokens as the image stream, images only pay ~6.6% of their attention to the condition (vs. ~72% to themselves) — an 11x imbalance that gets worse in later network blocks.
3. *Token resolution limits*: each layout condition token represents a 16×16 pixel cell. Over half of AI-TOD's real objects are smaller than that single cell, so their "representation" in the condition map is diluted/mixed with background inside that one token — a global fix can't help because it scales all cells equally.

**The two fixes.**
- *Layout-Weighted Loss (training-time)*: multiply the loss weight on any latent location covered by a box by a constant W (they use W=3), renormalizing so it remains a proper weighted average. This directly counteracts cause #1 by forcing the optimizer to care more about getting those regions right.
- *Condition-Layout Attention Gain / CLAG (inference-time, training-free)*: at generation time, add a constant offset to the attention logits before softmax, done in two ways: (a) a global gain x>1 applied symmetrically to all condition-stream keys/queries, boosting how much image/text tokens read the layout overall (countering cause #2); (b) an extra gain y>1 applied only to the small subset of condition cells containing sub-token-sized objects (countering cause #3), affecting under 1% of cells so it barely touches overall appearance. Mathematically, adding log(x) to a key's logit before softmax multiplies the *odds* of attending to that key by exactly x — a clean, measurable quantity they verify empirically tracks the predicted odds ratio.

Both techniques are complementary: training makes the model inherently better at following layouts (reducing Presence/Class errors), while attention gains correct remaining Localization/Size errors and recover the smallest objects — combining them gives the best result.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: the core contribution — a benchmark that evaluates per-object, class-conditioned fidelity for tiny objects, decoupled into interpretable axes with a real-anchored, non-compensatory aggregation — fills a real gap. The authors demonstrate convincingly (via degradation/perturbation studies) that no existing metric category (FID-style, CLIP-style, preference-reward-style) is sensitive to this failure mode, and their own scene-level metrics (CLIPScore, PickScore) stay essentially flat while Detectability swings by >25 points across methods — directly supporting the "existing eval is blind to this" claim.
- **Diagnosis is a genuine mechanistic insight, not just an empirical patch**: they don't just show the generator fails on small objects; they localize *why* (loss weighting, attention allocation, token granularity) with direct measurements (Figure 2's per-location error curves, attention-mass breakdown), and their two proposed fixes map 1:1 onto two of those three causes. This is a stronger standard of evidence than typical "we tried X and it helped."
- **The fixes work and are cheap**: Layout-Weighted Loss (+9.0 Det4) and CLAG (+8.0 Det4) each help independently and combine to +13 points more than either alone (72.2 → 85.3), with CLAG adding zero inference cost (no extra steps/resolution) versus the naive baseline of doubling resolution, which costs 4.9x compute for a smaller gain (+3.2) and *worse* Appearance.
- **What's only "evidence-supported" rather than fully proven**: the claim that rankings are robust is supported only by checking three detectors' individual rankings agree (Table S8) — a meaningful but limited robustness check, not independent-dataset validation. The claim that W=3 is a principled choice rests on a 3-run sweep with explicitly reported ±2.9 point training variance — the authors are appropriately modest, stating only that W=3 is "cheaper," not "better," than W=10.
- Per-class results reveal the benchmark surfaces real, structured weaknesses (e.g., "bridge" is uniformly the hardest class across all methods, and the paper rules out a trivial detector-bias explanation by noting real bridges are detected at 0.70 recall) — this specificity is a point in favor of the benchmark's diagnostic value.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Single dataset, single viewpoint regime** (stated, §Limitations): TOGBench is built and validated only on AI-TOD-v2, whose scenes are mostly near-nadir aerial views (plus a VisDrone subset for oblique/person/vehicle classes). Generalization to other sensors, resolutions, or viewpoints is explicitly left to future work.
- **Single generator architecture evaluated** (stated, §Limitations): only one layout-to-image generator (FLUX.1-dev + OminiControl LoRA) is analyzed and improved; whether the diagnosed failure modes (loss dilution, attention starvation, token granularity) and the proposed fixes generalize to other architectures (e.g., models with dedicated cross-attention for conditioning, or non-diffusion generators) is untested.
- **No human-judgment or downstream-task validation** (stated, §Limitations): the benchmark is validated only via synthetic degradation/perturbation ladders, not against human raters or against actual downstream object-detector training performance (despite motivating the whole project partly via "synthetic data augments detectors"). The paper cites prior findings that bad synthetic data can *degrade* detectors but never closes the loop by testing whether TOGBench-improved generations actually help a downstream detector.
- **Residual gap acknowledged** (stated): even the best combined method (85.3 Det4) doesn't close the gap to real objects (100), and the authors explicitly note sub-token objects remain fundamentally limited by latent/token resolution — a structural ceiling the proposed fixes cannot remove.
- **Hyperparameter selection methodology is somewhat post-hoc** (inferred): the specific (x, y) attention-gain values used in the main table are chosen as "the knee of the Det4–Appearance trade-off" from a grid sweep done on a single seed, then validated on 5 seeds — this is a reasonable but still somewhat manual/eyeballed selection procedure rather than an automatic criterion, and was done on the same 500 benchmark scenes used for final reporting (not a held-out validation split), raising a mild risk of overfitting the gain settings to this exact test set.
- **Baselines for comparison are narrow** (inferred): the main table's comparison points for "common remedies" are limited to varying classifier-free guidance scale and brute-force 2x resolution — reasonable strawman baselines, but no comparison against other published layout-to-image or aerial-specific generators (e.g., GeoDiffusion, AeroGen, CRS-Diff, cited in related work) is shown on TOGBench itself, so it's unclear how this generator/fix combination stacks up against other architectures people might actually use.
- **Small-sample statistics for Appearance axis** (stated, implicitly, in supplementary): the authors justify using k-NN distance over Fréchet-style distances specifically because per-class sample counts are small — a sound methodological choice, but it also means the absolute Appearance numbers rest on limited per-class real/generated crop counts (e.g., swimming-pool class has only 249 objects total in the 500-scene benchmark).
- **"Non-compensatory" aggregation choice (p=2) is a design decision, not derived** (inferred): the power-mean exponent and severity clamp σ=1.5 are fixed by the authors without an ablation showing results are robust to this choice; different aggregation choices could change relative rankings, and this isn't tested.
