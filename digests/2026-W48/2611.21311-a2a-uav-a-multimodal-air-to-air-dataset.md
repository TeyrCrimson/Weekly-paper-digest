# A2A-UAV: A Multimodal Air-to-Air Dataset for UAV Detection and Identification

**Authors:** Hongtao Liang, Yiyao Wan, Kezhi Zhu, Jiayi Zhang, Zequn Jiang, Ziyu Zhou, Jiahuan Ji, Fuhui Zhou, Qihui Wu

**arXiv:** [2611.21311](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21311)

**Categories:** cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-11-16

**Relevance:** 8/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
A2A-UAV is a new multimodal dataset for detecting and identifying non-cooperative drones from an airborne (drone-mounted) vantage point rather than the ground, pairing RGB, thermal, 360° panoramic, RF spectrum, and mmWave radar observations of 15 individual UAVs (13 commercial models) across 105k time-aligned samples with 209k manually-checked bounding boxes. The authors also run baseline detectors (Faster R-CNN, Cascade R-CNN, DINO, etc.) and RF-based classifiers (ResNet, EfficientNet, Swin, MobileNet) to show the data is usable and to establish reference numbers. It's primarily an infrastructure/benchmark contribution (a dataset paper), not a new algorithm.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Cheap commercial drones can fly into restricted airspace, and defenders need to detect and identify (recognize the model of) a non-cooperative UAV that isn't broadcasting its identity. Multiple sensor types are useful for this: regular cameras see shape, thermal cameras see heat signature (works in the dark), radar measures distance/velocity via radio reflections, and passive RF receivers can "overhear" the control link between the drone and its remote, which carries model-specific fingerprints (frequency hopping pattern, bandwidth, timing).

**Why prior datasets fall short.** Two issues:
1. *Viewpoint*: Almost all existing public datasets (Drone-vs-Bird, DUT Anti-UAV, Anti-UAV, DroneRF, RFUAV, etc.) were recorded by sensors fixed on the ground, looking up. This is a different visual/statistical regime than looking *from* a drone *at* another drone — backgrounds shift constantly (sky, ground, buildings), viewing angle and apparent target size change continuously, and there's no fixed camera geometry to exploit. Models trained on ground-based data don't transfer well.
2. *Modality coverage*: The few existing air-to-air datasets (Det-Fly, MOT-FLY, MMFW-UAV) only have camera images. None combine imaging with RF and radar, so nobody can study how these heterogeneous signals complement each other, or build RF/radar identification systems validated against visual ground truth, from the air.

**What this paper actually does.** They built a flying sensor rig: a second UAV carries an RGB+thermal gimbal camera, a 360° panoramic camera, an RF spectrum analyzer tuned to 2.42 GHz (the drone control-link band), and a 77 GHz FMCW mmWave radar. This "observer" drone chases/watches a "target" drone (one of 15 individually-tracked units across 13 commercial models) at three outdoor sites, day and night. All four sensor streams get mined into 105,294 time-aligned "samples" — a tricky engineering step because each sensor has different frame rates and data volumes:
- RGB/thermal/panoramic frames are extracted at synchronized 4 Hz timestamps.
- Radar returns are sparse (a drone is a small, weak radar reflector), so they attach *all* radar frames within a ±120 ms window to each sample rather than requiring exact alignment.
- RF is the trickiest: the analyzer streams IQ data at 500 MB/s, too much to record continuously, so they capture discrete 0.125 s snippets and convert each into a spectrogram image via short-time Fourier transform (STFT). Since RF signatures reflect the *transmission protocol* of the drone model rather than its instantaneous position, they just attach the nearest-in-time RF spectrogram to each sample rather than requiring tight synchronization — a reasonable design choice since RF content doesn't depend on exact relative geometry.

Humans then manually drew tight bounding boxes around the (mostly tiny) drone in RGB and panoramic images — no auto-labeling or tracking-assisted propagation, followed by a cross-check/refinement pass and automated sanity checks (missing modality, duplicate, out-of-bounds box, bad label). Data is split by *recording* (not by individual sample) into train/val/test so that frames from the same continuous flight don't leak across splits — a basic but important precaution against overly optimistic benchmark numbers.

Finally, they run off-the-shelf detectors (two-stage CNNs and transformer-based detectors like DINO/DDQ) on the RGB and panoramic images to localize drones, and off-the-shelf image classifiers (ResNet, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt, Swin, MobileNet) on the RF spectrograms to identify which of the 15 drone units is present. These aren't new methods — they're sanity-check baselines showing the data supports training and reveals a meaningful task difficulty gradient (e.g., panoramic detection is harder than RGB because targets are smaller and the panorama has projection distortion).

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: this is, per the authors' claim, the first air-to-air UAV dataset with five time-aligned modalities (RGB, thermal, panoramic, RF, radar) and individual-UAV identity labels, as opposed to prior work that is either air-to-air-but-vision-only or multimodal-but-ground-based. This fills a real, legitimate gap shown clearly in their comparison table (Table I) — no overlap of "air-to-air" + "RF/radar" + "ID labels" exists elsewhere.
- **The RF identification result is the most interesting empirical finding**: the system can tell 13 *different* drone models apart reasonably well (best macro-F1 74%), but struggles specifically to distinguish units/models sharing the *same transmission protocol* — three identical DJI Mini 4 Pro units get confused with each other, and DJI Air 3 vs Air 3S (same O4 system) get confused ~39% of the time. This supports a real claim: protocol-level RF features saturate, and finer hardware-level fingerprinting would be needed to go further. This is evidence-backed, not just claimed.
- **Detection baselines reveal a genuine, quantified difficulty axis**: panoramic detection (best AP≈0.20) is substantially harder than RGB (best AP≈0.29) because of smaller relative target size and equirectangular distortion; two-stage detectors (Faster/Cascade/Dynamic R-CNN) beat transformer-based detectors (DINO, DDQ) on this small-object task — a useful empirical data point for people building small-drone detectors, though it's a single dataset/setup, so generality is unproven.
- **What's claimed vs. supported**: the dataset's "value for multimodal fusion" is an aspirational use case, not something demonstrated — the paper never actually runs a fusion experiment combining RF+radar+vision; it only runs single-modality baselines (vision-only detection, RF-only identification). So the fusion claim is a design intention supported only by the fact that data is time-aligned, not by any fusion result.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **No multimodal fusion experiments were actually performed** (inferred) — despite being pitched as the headline benefit ("multimodal fusion for UAV perception"), no experiment combines two or more modalities; all reported results are single-modality (vision-only detection, RF-only classification). Thermal and radar data are provided but never benchmarked at all.
- **Small number of distinct physical sites and limited target population** (inferred) — all data collected at three outdoor sites on a single university campus in Nanjing, with 15 physical UAV units (13 models). Generalization to other environments, weather, countries' RF band allocations, or other drone brands/models (e.g., non-DJI) is untested; 12 of 13 models are DJI, so findings about "model confusion" may be specific to DJI's O-series protocols rather than general.
- **Detection accuracy is quite low in absolute terms** (stated implicitly via results) — best AP is only ~0.20 (panoramic) / ~0.29 (RGB), and AP75 is far lower than AP50 (e.g., 0.12 vs 0.50), meaning localization precision is poor even when detection "succeeds." The paper frames this as reflecting genuine task difficulty (tiny targets, panoramic distortion) rather than a shortcoming of the data, but it also means the benchmark baselines are weak and there's room to question whether labels/scale are optimal for these off-the-shelf detectors.
- **No tracking or temporal baselines despite continuous recordings** (inferred) — the data is organized as continuous recordings/flights but all detection/identification is evaluated frame-by-frame; no multi-object tracking, temporal fusion, or sequence-based baseline is attempted, even though motion/velocity (radar) and continuity seem well suited to it.
- **RF association is approximate, not frame-exact** (stated) — each sample is assigned the *nearest-in-time* RF spectrogram rather than a time-synchronized one, justified by the argument that RF signature depends on protocol not position; this is a reasonable but unverified assumption (it is not empirically tested that RF content is in fact invariant to the observer-target geometry over the relevant time windows).
- **1,049 samples kept without any bounding box** (stated) — justified as the target leaving one camera's FOV while visible in the other, but this is a label-quality edge case that could affect downstream training if not handled carefully by users.
- **Annotation process relies entirely on manual, non-automated bounding boxes** (stated as a quality strength, but also a scale caveat/inferred) — with ~210k images requiring manual annotation, there is inherent risk of annotator fatigue/inconsistency on very small objects (median box area ~3.6×10⁻⁴ of image area for RGB), even though the authors did cross-check/refine.
- **Full dataset not yet released** (stated) — only a 200-sample preview subset is currently available; the full 2.5TB dataset release is contingent on paper acceptance, so the dataset's claims cannot currently be independently verified by a reader.
- **Simulation/controlled-flight setting, not adversarial real-world conditions** (inferred) — both the "intruder" and the "observer" drones were flown by licensed, cooperating pilots maintaining safe separation at university-controlled sites; this is a cooperative, scripted data-collection scenario rather than an adversarial intrusion scenario (e.g., no evasive maneuvers, jamming, or genuinely unauthorized flight behavior), which may limit realism for actual counter-UAV deployment.
- **RF frequency scope is narrow** (inferred) — the spectrum analyzer was centered at a single fixed frequency (2.42 GHz), covering only part of the 2.4 GHz band; drones using 5.8 GHz links or other bands are not represented, limiting generality of RF identification claims.
