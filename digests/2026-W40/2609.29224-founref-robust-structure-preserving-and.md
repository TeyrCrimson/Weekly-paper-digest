# FounRef: Robust, Structure-Preserving, and Fast Metric Refinement of Frozen Monocular Foundation Priors with Sparse Anchors

**Authors:** Dan Halperin, Mirko Mählisch

**arXiv:** [2609.29224](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29224)

**Categories:** cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-09-24

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: Metric and temporally-consistent monocular depth

---

## TL;DR
FounRef is a training-free pipeline that turns any frozen monocular depth-foundation model (e.g., MoGe-2) into a dense metric depth map by calibrating it against sparse metric anchors (e.g., LiDAR points), using a validation step to reject bad anchors and a bilateral-grid solver to propagate corrections without distorting surface geometry. Against DMD3C, a state-of-the-art trained depth-completion network, it reports up to 24% lower depth error, 92% lower surface-normal noise, and ~15x faster inference on out-of-domain data. The core claim is that decoupling "get good geometry" (foundation model) from "get correct scale" (sparse anchors + lightweight solver) beats end-to-end learned depth completion on generalization, structural fidelity, and speed simultaneously.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** A single RGB image alone cannot tell you true metric distances — it's inherently scale-ambiguous (a toy car photographed close up looks identical to a real car far away). Two families of methods try to fix this:
1. *Monocular foundation models* (MoGe-2, DepthPro, Metric3D-V2, etc.) are trained on huge, diverse image collections and learn to predict very detailed, sharp relative geometry (good edges, good surfaces), but their absolute metric scale is unreliable, especially far from the camera or on unfamiliar cameras/scenes.
2. *Depth-completion networks* (DMD3C, OMNI-DC, NLSPN, etc.) are trained end-to-end to fuse an RGB image with sparse metric measurements (e.g., a LiDAR scan sparsely projected onto the image) into a dense metric map. Because this fusion is learned, it's tied to the training distribution — sensor type, scene type, and the exact sparsity pattern seen during training. Move to a new sensor rig or a new domain and accuracy/structure can degrade badly, and retraining is expensive. Worse, some of these networks improve pointwise error by literally imprinting the LiDAR's scan pattern into the predicted surface — the depth map becomes locally "bumpy" around each anchor even though the aggregate error metric looks better.

**Why prior approaches trade off badly.** The paper's framing: you can't just look at RMSE. A model can win on point accuracy while producing garbage surface normals (visible artifacts, ringing near anchors) or being too slow for large-scale batch/online use. No existing method was shown to be good on all three axes (accuracy, structural coherence, speed) across domains at once.

**FounRef's mechanism**, step by step:
1. **Frozen prior, no retraining.** Run a monocular foundation model (MoGe-2) to get a dense but metrically-wrong depth map `D_prior`, plus a validity mask (e.g., masking sky).
2. **Global calibration (log-space linear fit).** Since foundation model errors are roughly multiplicative (scale errors), take logs of both predicted and true (anchor) depths — this turns a scale error into an additive shift. Fit a robust (Huber-loss) line `y = αt + β` between the log-prior depth and log-anchor depth at anchor locations. Because a single line still isn't perfect across all distances, they additionally bin anchors by depth into 24 bins and add a small per-distance correction curve on top — like a piecewise recalibration of a "reliability curve."
3. **Build a trusted reference surface (`D1`).** To catch bad anchors (not just projection artifacts but also things like moving objects, temporal misalignment, or a LiDAR point landing on a windshield/mirror that the RGB image doesn't "see" the same way) you need a local reference better than the crude global fit. So they run a lightweight version of their own local solver (below) on the fitting anchors to get a locally-refined depth surface `D1`.
4. **Anchor validation.** Test each anchor against `D1`: if the anchor's log-depth disagrees with `D1` at that pixel by more than a threshold τ (dataset-specific — looser for clean KITTI, stricter for noisier nuScenes/GOOSE), discard it. This is the paper's "semantic" insight: geometry-only occlusion filters (like the RePLAy baseline, which uses epipolar geometry to detect projective occlusion) can't catch cross-modal disagreement like a LiDAR return through foliage that the camera doesn't register, or scan-to-scan drift. Testing against a locally-refined dense reference catches a broader class of errors.
5. **Structure-preserving local solve (final `D2`).** Now solve for a smooth additive correction field `b(x)` in log-depth space, fit to the surviving (validated) anchors, regularized by a "bilateral Laplacian" — a graph built on a downsampled bilateral grid where two pixels are connected only if they're close in image position *and* similar in prior depth. This means corrections spread along a coherent surface (e.g., correcting a pole) but don't leak across depth discontinuities into the background. It's solved efficiently (half-resolution, Jacobi-preconditioned conjugate gradients) and then the correction (not the whole depth map) is upsampled and applied, so the original prior's fine detail is untouched except for the scale correction. This is essentially the "fast bilateral solver" (Barron & Poole, 2016) applied to a residual correction field, chosen over alternatives like TGV or screened Poisson refinement because those fit anchors more aggressively and create local "bumps" (anchor imprinting) in the surface.

**Why this design choice matters mechanically:** by only ever adding a smooth correction to a frozen, already-good geometric prior — never re-predicting geometry from scratch — the method structurally cannot introduce the kind of anchor-pattern artifacts that plague learned completion networks (visible in their Figure 11, where some baselines reproduce the LiDAR's scan-line pattern as fake surface geometry).

## Key insights

- **Decoupling metric alignment from geometry prediction is the central bet**, and the evidence (Tables 1–4, Figure 6/11) supports it reasonably well: FounRef achieves markedly lower surface-normal dispersion across all 5 tested datasets than every learned baseline, while staying metrically competitive and 10–20x faster than most.
- **No learned baseline is uniformly good across domains** — DMD3C wins on KITTI (its likely near-training-distribution) but degrades elsewhere; this cross-domain instability of learned completion is a genuinely useful empirical finding, not just a claim, since it's shown across 5 datasets with identical anchor inputs.
- **Anchor validation against a locally-refined reference, rather than geometry-only filters,** is a real methodological contribution — Table 4 shows it flags different (and more/differently reliable) anchors than the RePLAy epipolar baseline, particularly useful for cross-sensor/temporal noise that pure geometric occlusion reasoning can't detect.
- **The paper is honest that its own solver has a tunable accuracy/structure knob** (σs bandwidth) — smaller cells reduce RMSE but reintroduce anchor-imprinting artifacts (Figure 9), which is an important nuance: the headline low-normal-dispersion numbers depend on a specific operating point choice, and the "free lunch" of accuracy without structural cost is not unconditional.
- **Modularity is claimed but only partially demonstrated**: the paper shows swapping the prior (Table 3, 6 different foundation models) and the solver (Table 2, 3 solvers), which is decent support. It does not demonstrate swapping the anchor source (e.g., stereo, radar, structured light) — that generality is asserted, not tested.
- The method's accuracy is fundamentally capped by the prior's geometry — the paper is explicit that FounRef cannot recover structure the prior misses or misrepresents (e.g., GOOSE vegetation see-through), which is a meaningful, self-aware limitation rather than a hidden one.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Prior-bounded ceiling (stated).** FounRef "remains limited by the quality of its prior" and "cannot reliably recover geometry that the prior omits or interprets incorrectly" — explicitly demonstrated with the GOOSE vegetation example where LiDAR sees through foliage but the prior doesn't, hurting pointwise accuracy there.
- **Anchor range capping to 50m (stated, methodological choice).** Anchors are capped at 50m because "monocular priors become substantially less reliable at longer ranges." This sidesteps the hardest regime for the method's core assumption (that the frozen prior gives decent structure) and could inflate reported gains relative to real-world unbounded-range deployment; far-range numbers are reported separately but the main comparison excludes the hardest cases.
- **Evaluation targets are partly self-referential (inferred/stated tension).** For nuScenes/Waymo/GOOSE, since no clean dense ground truth exists, the authors build the evaluation target by filtering accumulated LiDAR points using an independent Metric3D-V2 prior's structural consistency — i.e., "ground truth" itself is prior-model-filtered. They argue this avoids bias toward FounRef (which uses MoGe-2, a different prior) but it still means the benchmark's notion of "correct" is defined by another monocular foundation model's opinion of the scene, not independent ground truth — a circularity risk on non-KITTI/NYU datasets.
- **Threshold τ is dataset-specific and hand-tuned (stated).** The anchor-validation threshold τ is selected per-dataset from "a validation operating curve" (0.45 for KITTI, 0.2 for nuScenes) — this is manual, dataset-specific calibration, which cuts against the "no task-specific training" framing; it's not training, but it is tuning, and the paper doesn't report sensitivity/robustness to picking τ wrong on a genuinely new dataset without labels to build such a curve.
- **Accuracy/structure trade-off is real and the headline config is a choice (stated).** Their own ablation (Figure 5, Table 7, Figure 9) shows a more aggressive solver setting (σs=4) improves RMSE 21–33% but reintroduces anchor-imprinting "blobs" — the paper picks the conservative default for headline claims, and readers should note the reported 24%/92% improvements are for one specific operating point on their Pareto frontier, not a dominant option.
- **Structure metric (normal dispersion) may itself be insensitive to localized damage (stated).** The authors explicitly note the median-based normal-dispersion metric "cannot report" the localized blob damage at σs=4, since a median is robust to a sparse minority of corrupted pixels — meaning their own headline structural metric has a known blind spot they had to describe qualitatively instead.
- **Small sample sizes for reported main-table numbers (inferred).** Headline comparisons use only 500 validation frames per dataset (and prior/solver ablations sometimes only 100), which is modest for claims of "up to 24% lower error" across diverse domains; no confidence intervals or variance across seeds are reported for the main benchmark table.
- **Baselines run without dataset-specific recalibration (stated as protocol, but a fairness question, inferred).** All completion baselines are evaluated "using publicly released checkpoints, without dataset-specific recalibration," which is reasonable for testing zero-shot transfer, but also means baselines that could plausibly be lightly fine-tuned or recalibrated per-sensor in real deployments are being tested in a deliberately disadvantaged regime relative to how they might actually be used in practice.
- **Compute environment is a single high-end GPU with fp16 where possible (stated), and "most models cannot run at fp16 precision"** in the reported worst case — meaning the reported runtime comparisons partly reflect implementation/precision differences across methods, not purely algorithmic cost, though the paper does flag this limitation itself.
- **No comparison of anchor sources beyond LiDAR (inferred).** Despite the modularity claim in the abstract ("anchor source... can be replaced independently"), no experiments substitute a non-LiDAR anchor source (radar, stereo, sparse SLAM points) — this generalization claim is untested in the provided text.
- **GenAI-assisted writing (stated).** The authors disclose generative AI was used for language editing, with authors taking responsibility for technical content — a minor disclosure but worth noting per the paper's own "Use of Generative AI" section.
