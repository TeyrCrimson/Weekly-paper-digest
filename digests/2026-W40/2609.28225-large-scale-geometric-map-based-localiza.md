# Large-Scale Geometric Map-Based Localization of UAVs in GNSS-Denied Urban Environments

**Authors:** Garth Terlizzi, Kaveh Fathian

**arXiv:** [2609.28225](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28225)

**Categories:** cs.RO

**Submitted:** 2026-09-23

**Relevance:** 6/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
The paper introduces a UAV localization system for GNSS-denied cities that matches building patterns from downward-facing drone camera imagery against a Microsoft Building Footprints reference database, using a geometry-driven descriptor (Delaunay triangle structure plus per-building shape features) instead of visual appearance matching. It achieves 100% Recall@1 at ~113 km² and ~254 km² search areas and 71.4% at ~452 km² across seven real-world flights, while both compared baselines (BRM, NetVLAD) collapse to 0% Recall@1 at 254 km² and beyond. The core claim is that encoding spatial/shape geometry of buildings, rather than pixel appearance, is what allows discriminability to survive at metropolitan scale.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Drones need to know their global position, but GNSS (GPS-like satellite positioning) can be jammed, spoofed, or blocked in cities ("urban canyons"). The alternative is absolute visual localization (AVL): use the camera plus a pre-built reference map to figure out "where am I" without satellites.

**Why prior approaches struggle.** Most AVL systems either (a) match a UAV camera image directly against satellite images of the area ("image retrieval"), or (b) learn visual feature embeddings (e.g., NetVLAD) to compare query images to reference tiles. Both rely on visual appearance — but drone imagery and satellite/reference imagery differ in season, lighting, shadows, and viewing angle, so appearance-based matching degrades as those differences grow. Crucially, as the search area (the region of candidate locations) expands, there are more visually similar-looking places, so retrieval becomes ambiguous and accuracy collapses. Prior graph/geometry-based methods (e.g., matching tree-trunk positions in forests) exist but were built for sparse, shapeless landmarks (points), not dense urban buildings where many local geometric patterns look alike.

**This paper's mechanism, step by step:**
1. **Segment buildings**: A downward-facing camera captures frames; a SegFormer-B5 semantic segmentation network (trained on aerial imagery) labels which pixels are "building" vs. background, producing a binary mask per keyframe.
2. **Build an internal map**: Since one frame is noisy/incomplete, consecutive keyframes are stitched together. Feature matching (LoFTR) plus RANSAC estimates how each new frame aligns (rotate/translate/scale) with the last, and a pose-graph optimization (like adjusting a chain of estimated positions to be mutually consistent, including "loop closures" when the drone revisits an area) corrects drift. The result is one cleaned-up local map of observed building footprints.
3. **Triangulate**: The centroids (center points) of detected buildings are connected via Delaunay triangulation — a standard geometric construction where you connect points into triangles such that no other point lies inside any triangle's circumscribed circle. This produces a natural "neighbors" graph without arbitrary distance thresholds, and importantly, this graph structure stays the same even if the whole point set is rotated, translated, or uniformly scaled (a property called similarity-invariance) — useful because the drone's exact orientation/scale relative to the map is unknown upfront.
4. **Star descriptors**: For each building, take its Delaunay triangle plus the three adjacent triangles ("a star") — this local cluster of 4 triangles is turned into a 32-dimensional numeric fingerprint. Each triangle contributes: 3 sorted interior angles + perimeter (basic shape of the triangle) plus 4 shape metrics averaged over its three corner buildings — compactness, elongation, rectangularity, convexity (basic descriptors of how "blob-like," "stretched," "boxy," or "concave" a building footprint is). The key innovation over prior work (Li et al., used for tree-trunk matching) is adding these building-shape features, because in dense cities many triangles are nearly identical in pure geometry ("near-congruent"), so shape breaks the ties.
5. **Matching**: Every star descriptor from the UAV's internal map is compared against a giant precomputed database of star descriptors built the same way from the reference building footprints. A cheap filter first discards obviously incompatible pairs (angle/perimeter/shape too different), then survivors get a similarity score weighting angle agreement most, then perimeter and shape equally.
6. **Voting**: Each matched descriptor pair implies a candidate 2D transform (rotation/scale/translation) mapping the drone's local map onto the world map — i.e., a hypothesized drone position. All these hypothesized positions are dropped into 100m×100m grid bins and accumulate weighted "votes." Correct matches cluster in the same bins; wrong matches scatter randomly, so simple vote-counting suppresses outliers without needing to explicitly reject each bad hypothesis.
7. **Candidate refinement/selection**: Top vote-getting bins are re-scored using "mutual nearest-neighbor" matching between predicted and actual reference buildings (a Jaccard-style overlap score) to pick the best coarse location.
8. **Fine correction**: A final stage re-matches raw recent frames directly to the reference map and computes a rigid correction (rotation+translation, via SVD, a standard "point cloud alignment" technique) to remove residual drift from the stitching stage, giving the final position estimate.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new**: augmenting Delaunay-triangle-only geometric descriptors (previously used for sparse point landmarks like tree trunks) with per-building shape features, specifically to restore discriminability in dense urban settings where triangle geometry alone becomes ambiguous. The ablation ("Ours w/o shape") directly supports this: it consistently underperforms the full method, and the gap widens as search area grows (R@1 drops to 3/7 at 9–12km vs. 5/7 for the full method) — evidence the shape features specifically help at scale, not just geometry in general.
- **Evidence-supported claim**: the system scales to far larger search areas on real drone footage than prior work evaluated on real imagery (largest prior real-drone benchmark cited is 100 km²; this paper reaches ~452 km²), and clearly outperforms two implemented baselines (BRM, NetVLAD) which both hit 0/7 Recall@1 beyond 6km radius.
- **Claimed but less rigorously supported**: that the approach is broadly "robust to appearance variation." This is architecturally plausible (geometry vs. pixels) but not stress-tested — evaluation is under fairly uniform daytime, mostly clear-weather conditions (paper's own admission), so appearance-invariance is asserted more than demonstrated across diverse conditions.
- **Practically useful detail**: incremental/cached re-matching (only re-scoring stars whose geometry changed) keeps per-query runtime to sub-2-second range on consumer GPU hardware even against a 277,000-building database, suggesting real-time feasibility, though this excludes offline reference preprocessing.
- **Voting + mutual-NN Jaccard scoring** is a reasonably elegant way to suppress false positives without hand-tuned per-hypothesis rejection thresholds — a design contribution, though not novel in the outlier-rejection literature generally (RANSAC-style voting is standard; the novelty is applying it to this descriptor).

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Small sample size** (stated implicitly, inferred as limitation): only 7 flights across 4 municipalities in a single metropolitan area (Denver). Recall@1 fractions like "5/7" or "71.4%" rest on very few trials, so precision of the headline percentages is low — one more failed/succeeded flight would shift results by ~14 points. `inferred`
- **Residual localization error is nontrivial**: final errors range 4.3–40.7m even on "successful" trials, and success is defined loosely as within 100m of ground truth — a large tolerance matching the 100m voting-grid bin size, which the authors acknowledge is a "deliberate design choice" that bounds coarse-retrieval evaluation rather than reflecting fine-grained accuracy. `stated`
- **Ground truth itself is uncertain**: ground-truth trajectories come from "onboard consumer-grade GPS logs," which the authors admit "contribute some horizontal uncertainty to all reported errors" — meaning the yardstick used to judge success/failure is itself imprecise. `stated`
- **Sensitivity to reference data completeness**: the method depends entirely on the Microsoft Building Footprints database; the authors state reliability "drops when footprints are missing or displaced enough to alter local Delaunay topology." `stated`
- **Segmentation errors directly corrupt geometry**: the Flight 7 case study shows snow-covered ground misclassified as buildings, injecting "phantom vertices" that corrupt descriptors and delay convergence — illustrating a structural fragility where segmentation mistakes don't just add noise but can change the topological graph itself. `stated`
- **Narrow evaluation conditions**: authors state evaluation "assumes nadir imagery under mostly daytime, clear-visibility conditions with accurate inter-frame alignment," so performance under night, heavy weather, or strong seasonal appearance change is untested despite the paper's broader claims about robustness to appearance variation. `stated`
- **No systematic ablation of design choices beyond the shape-feature one**: authors explicitly list as future work "systematic ablations of star-descriptor components and candidate-pruning strategies," meaning robustness/efficiency tradeoffs of key design parameters (τ thresholds, weighting 2:1:1, grid bin size, top-K=20, N=20 refinement frames) are not empirically justified in this paper — these look like hand-picked hyperparameters. `stated` (future work) / `inferred` (weak ablation coverage)
- **Baseline implementations may not be strongest possible**: BRM and NetVLAD are reimplemented/adapted by the authors (e.g., NetVLAD retrained with a custom dual-branch ResNet-18 on UAV-VisLoc), not run as originally published/tuned systems; NetVLAD is also given compass-heading assistance that the proposed method and BRM are denied, which is a partial handicap disclosed by the authors but also raises questions about baseline comparability. `inferred` (fairness of comparison), `stated` (heading-assistance asymmetry)
- **Scalability characterization is incomplete**: authors state "the star-descriptor and pruning design's robustness–efficiency sensitivity is not yet systematically characterized," and note candidate count/comparison/voting load grows with search area and density — i.e., there's no stated bound on how this scales past 452 km² or in denser mega-cities. `stated`
- **LLM-assisted writing**: the authors disclose using GPT-5.2 for "minor language editing," which doesn't affect technical validity but is worth noting for transparency. `stated`
- **Single sensor/platform**: all flights use one drone platform (DJI Mini 4K) at similar altitude range (97–116m); generalization to other platforms, altitudes, or camera characteristics is not addressed in the paper. `inferred`
