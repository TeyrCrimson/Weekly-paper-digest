# FoCal: Frequency-Oriented Cross-Modal Interaction and Spectral Calibration for Aerial Visible-Infrared Object Detection

**Authors:** Ben Liang, Chao Sui, Junqi Bai, Yuan Liu, Chunlai Li, Xiubao Sui, Qian Chen

**arXiv:** [2609.29125](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29125)

**Categories:** cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-09-24

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
FoCal is a lightweight RGB-infrared aerial object detector that treats low- and high-frequency image components differently during cross-modal fusion: low frequencies are merged into a shared "consensus" representation while high frequencies exchange information selectively to preserve modality-specific detail. It also adds a module that measures how much the two modalities' frequency spectra disagree and uses that disagreement (rather than raw features) as a gating signal to enhance, preserve, or suppress a jointly-fused spectrum. On three aerial RGB-IR benchmarks (DroneVehicle, ESCVehicle, ATR-UMOD) it reports state-of-the-art mAP50 with far fewer parameters (3.0M) and higher speed (113.6 FPS) than compared methods.

## First-principles breakdown

**The underlying problem.** Detecting objects (mostly vehicles) from drone/aerial cameras using both a visible (RGB) and infrared (IR) camera is useful because RGB struggles in poor lighting/weather while IR is illumination-invariant but lacks fine texture. Combining them ("multimodal fusion detection") should give a detector that's robust in both day and night, foggy or clear conditions. The core sub-problem is: how do you combine two feature maps (one from each modality) that partially agree (e.g., both show the outline of a car) and partially disagree (e.g., RGB has fine paint/texture detail, IR has heat-signature noise) into one representation that keeps the useful parts of each without amplifying the unreliable, modality-specific noise?

**Why prior approaches fall short.** Most multimodal detectors fuse RGB and IR features directly in the spatial domain (concatenation, attention, cross-attention) — this doesn't distinguish "this pixel region is structurally consistent across modalities" from "this pixel region reflects modality-specific texture/noise" because everything is mixed together in one representation. A newer line of work moves fusion into the frequency domain (via FFT or wavelet transforms), splitting an image into low-frequency components (coarse shapes/layout) and high-frequency components (edges/fine detail/noise), and then applying different processing operators to each band. But even these methods (FreFusion-style) still fuse same-frequency components from both modalities the same way — i.e., once you've separated frequencies, low and high bands are each fused using a generic fusion module. The paper's central critique: the *processing operator* differing by frequency band isn't enough — the *interaction topology itself* (how many-to-one vs. how much individuality is preserved) should differ by frequency, because low-frequency structure genuinely agrees across modalities (so it's safe to collapse into one shared representation) while high-frequency detail is often modality-specific and collapsing it risks discarding real signal or spreading sensor noise from one modality into the other.

**FoCal's mechanism, step by step.**

1. *Frequency decomposition*: Each modality's feature map is decomposed via Haar Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) into one low-frequency approximation (A) and three directional high-frequency detail maps (horizontal/vertical/diagonal), which are summed into one high-frequency map (H) per modality.

2. *Frequency-Aware Dual-Domain Calibration (FADC)* — the paper's first module, with two different interaction rules:
   - **Low-frequency "consensus"**: The two modalities' low-frequency maps are concatenated, passed through a small conv+Softmax gate to get complementary weights, and blended into a single shared representation (a weighted average, not a fixed 50/50 average). This is a "many-to-one" merge — both modalities contribute to one output.
   - **High-frequency "selective exchange"**: Instead of merging, each modality keeps its own high-frequency stream, but each stream can selectively absorb useful detail from the other via a learned "reception mask" (computed from pooled descriptors of both modalities through a conv+Sigmoid gate). This is a "two-to-two" interaction — RGB gets an update mixed in from IR (gated by how much RGB wants to receive) and vice versa, but neither collapses into a shared representation.
   - These calibrated low/high components are recombined per modality (shared low + modality-specific high), and that combined signal is used only as a *reliability estimate* — spatial and channel "trust" masks derived from it are combined with a joint bimodal prior to decide how much of the counterpart modality's *original* (not frequency-decomposed) feature to inject back into each modality's original stream, followed by a residual "calibration" step. In other words, frequency analysis isn't the final output; it's used to compute where/how much cross-modal information should be trusted and merged into the original spatial features.

3. *Discrepancy-Guided Spectral Modulation (DGSM)* — the second module, applied on top. It takes the (calibrated) RGB and IR features, projects them into a shared space, FFTs them, and computes a normalized relative discrepancy between the two amplitude spectra: |A_rgb − A_ir| / (A_rgb + A_ir). Naively, large discrepancy could just mean noise at low-energy frequencies (two near-zero signals can still have a large relative difference), so the authors weight this discrepancy by an "energy confidence" term (based on how strong the combined spectral energy is at that location relative to the average) — so discrepancies at low-energy/likely-noise locations get down-weighted. This confidence-weighted discrepancy is passed through a small conv + tanh to produce a bounded signed gate in [−1, 1]. Separately, RGB and IR are concatenated/compressed into a single "joint" spectrum via FFT. The signed gate then multiplicatively modulates this joint spectrum (Z_joint × (1 + γ·gate), γ=0.5) — i.e., depending on whether the learned gate is positive or negative, that frequency location's joint response gets boosted, left alone, or attenuated. A small residual transform is applied to the real/imaginary parts, then inverse-FFT brings it back to spatial features, which are added residually to the concatenated input.

4. *Overall architecture*: FoCal extends a YOLO (Ultralytics-based) dual-stream backbone, inserting FADC+DGSM blocks at multiple pyramid scales, feeding a PAN neck and detection head. A "redundancy reduction" (RR) design choice shares deeper (P5) computation between modalities instead of running two full independent backbones to P5, and concentrates final predictions on P4/P5 while P3 still contributes fine spatial detail to multi-scale aggregation — this alone cuts params from 4.01M to 2.63M with a modest accuracy gain (ablation Table IV), before FADC/DGSM are even added.

## Key insights

- **Claimed novel idea**: cross-modal interaction *topology* (not just processing operator) should be frequency-dependent — low frequency → many-to-one consensus, high frequency → two-to-two selective/individuality-preserving exchange. The ablation in Table V is the direct evidence for this claim: symmetric strategies (both bands using exchange, or both using consensus) underperform the proposed asymmetric assignment, and — notably — *reversing* the assignment (consensus on high-freq, exchange on low-freq) also underperforms, at matched parameter count. This is a reasonably clean, controlled test of the core hypothesis and it does support the claim, at least on this one dataset (DroneVehicle) with this one architecture.
- **Discrepancy-as-control rather than discrepancy-as-content**: rather than directly fusing the spectral difference between modalities into the representation (which risks amplifying noise), DGSM uses the discrepancy only to gate a separately-computed joint spectrum. The ablation (Table IV) shows DGSM alone adds ~0.7–0.8 mAP50 over the RR-only baseline for a small parameter/FLOPs cost, and combined with FADC gives the best result — supporting that the two modules are at least partly complementary (additive gains), though this is only demonstrated on one dataset (DroneVehicle) for the component-level ablation.
- **Efficiency claim is well-supported by the data given**: 3.0M params / 8.4 GFLOPs / 113.6 FPS with best-in-table mAP50 across all three benchmarks is a real and notable efficiency-accuracy combination relative to the specific baselines shown (COMO, CrossWeaver, FusionMamba, WaveMamba, etc.), assuming those numbers are measured comparably (same GPU, batch size 1, provided in Table VI for two comparators).
- **Much of the accuracy gain plausibly comes from the RR architectural change, not the novel frequency modules**: Table IV shows RR alone moves mAP50 from 80.8%→81.9% (+1.1) and cuts params nearly in half, while FADC adds another +1.0 and DGSM alone adds +0.7, with both combined adding +1.6 over RR alone. So the headline "3.0M params, 83.5% mAP50" achievement is a combination of a fairly standard backbone-sharing/redundancy-reduction trick plus the frequency modules — worth noting when assessing how much credit belongs to the paper's namesake contributions (FADC/DGSM) versus the RR redesign.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Component ablations run on one dataset only (DroneVehicle)** — `inferred`. Tables IV and V, which isolate RR/FADC/DGSM contributions and validate the core frequency-topology hypothesis, are only reported on DroneVehicle. No ablation evidence is given for ESCVehicle or ATR-UMOD, so it's unclear whether the same component breakdown (or the reversed-topology comparison) holds on the other two benchmarks.
- **No cross-dataset or cross-architecture generalization test of the frequency-topology claim** — `inferred`. The "low-freq consensus / high-freq exchange" result rests on a single controlled ablation table (Table V) on one dataset with one backbone; it's a plausible and clean single result but not replicated elsewhere in the paper.
- **All training is from scratch, no pretrained weights, single-GPU, single-seed** — `stated` (implied by "trained entirely from scratch without relying on any pre-trained weights" and "single NVIDIA RTX 4090"). No mention of multiple runs/seeds or variance/confidence intervals for reported metrics, so small mAP differences (e.g., +0.5–1.5 points over the next-best method) lack statistical uncertainty estimates — `inferred` caveat regarding reproducibility of the margins.
- **Some baseline numbers are self-reproduced ("†")** — `stated` in table footnotes (YOLO11n, YOLO11n-Dual, C²DFF-Net, COMO, CrossWeaver marked with †). Reproducing competitor baselines under one's own training pipeline instead of using each paper's original reported numbers is a common but real source of potential bias (settings could inadvertently favor the proposed method); the paper doesn't discuss whether reproduced numbers matched originally-published numbers.
- **FPS/latency comparison only reported against two competitors (COMO, CrossWeaver)** — `inferred`. Table VI (the speed/latency comparison) doesn't include most of the other baselines from Tables I–III (e.g., M²D-LIF, FusionMamba, WaveMamba, C²DFF-Net), so the "favorable accuracy-efficiency trade-off" claim is only quantitatively benchmarked against a subset of compared methods.
- **All evaluation is on aerial vehicle-detection datasets with relatively similar imaging setups (drone-mounted RGB-IR pairs)** — `inferred`. No evaluation on ground-level or pedestrian-oriented RGB-IR benchmarks (e.g., KAIST, LLVIP, common in this literature) is presented, so generalization beyond aerial/vehicle scenarios is untested in the paper.
- **No failure-case analysis** — `inferred`. The qualitative visualizations (Fig. 5–7) show only cases where FoCal succeeds and competitors miss detections; no examples of where FoCal itself fails are shown or discussed.
- **Future-work items are explicitly deferred**, including hardware-aware/embedded deployment (mixed precision, TensorRT) and extension to temporal/more diverse multimodal settings — `stated` in Discussion/Conclusion, indicating these are acknowledged as not yet demonstrated.
- **γ (modulation magnitude in DGSM) is fixed empirically at 0.5** — `stated`, but no sensitivity/ablation over this hyperparameter is shown, so its robustness is untested within the paper.
