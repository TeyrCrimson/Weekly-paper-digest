# PrefixBench-H100: Characterizing Prefix Reuse and Time-to-First-Token in H100 LLM Serving

**Authors:** Omkar Shewale, Deepak Kumar, Divakar Kumar Yadav

**arXiv:** [2609.19657](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19657)

**Categories:** cs.PF, cs.LG

**Submitted:** 2026-09-17

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
PrefixBench-H100 is a reproducible benchmark that measures when KV-cache prefix reuse actually helps LLM serving latency on a single H100 GPU, comparing vLLM and TensorRT-LLM under matched workloads. Its main finding: cache hit-rate effectiveness is essentially tied between runtimes and insensitive to concurrency or output length, but the *runtime that wins on latency flips depending on arrival pattern* (burst vs. steady-rate), meaning the real cross-runtime differentiator is the scheduler above the cache, not the cache mechanism itself. The paper deliberately introduces no new algorithm — it's a measurement/characterization study meant to give operators a tuning-decision map.

## First-principles breakdown

**The underlying problem.** LLM inference has two phases: *prefill* (processing the whole input prompt in parallel, compute-bound, dominates time-to-first-token/TTFT) and *decode* (generating tokens one at a time, memory-bandwidth-bound, dominates per-token latency). Many real workloads (chat systems, RAG pipelines, agents) send many requests that share a long identical prefix (e.g., a system prompt or a RAG template) followed by a unique suffix. Recomputing the prefill for that shared prefix every time is wasteful — this is what "prefix caching" (storing the shared prefix's key/value activations to reuse later) avoids.

**Why prior understanding is incomplete.** Prefix caching (via vLLM's PagedAttention, which stores KV cache in fixed-size reusable blocks, and TensorRT-LLM's analogous `enable_block_reuse`/`tokens_per_block` mechanism) was designed under older GPU assumptions (A100-era, where GPU memory was scarce). On H100 GPUs with 94GB of HBM3, memory is much less scarce, so it's unclear whether the old scarcity-driven tuning knobs (like block size) still matter, and whether the "cache" is even the bottleneck anymore versus other factors like request scheduling.

**What the paper actually does.** It builds a workload-driving harness (`load_gen.py`) that sends *identical* JSONL-defined requests through both vLLM and TensorRT-LLM's OpenAI-compatible APIs, varying five axes: shared-prefix length, suffix diversity, request arrival pattern (burst vs. fixed-rate), concurrency, output length, and cache configuration (e.g., block size). It measures TTFT, inter-token latency, end-to-end latency, throughput, cache hit rate, GPU memory, and power draw. Two workload generators are used: pure synthetic prefix+suffix traces (to isolate reuse effects cleanly) and RAG-style templated traces (to check realism). Everything runs on a single H100 NVL with a 7B model (Qwen2-7B-Instruct) as the primary test subject, with follow-up checks using a 32B model and a different architecture (Mistral-7B) to test generality.

**Key mechanism/finding logic, step by step:**
1. *Sequential reuse test* (concurrency 1): turning on prefix caching cuts TTFT by 5–6.5× at long (8192-token) shared prefixes, and both runtimes cache ~92% of tokens equally well — the residual ~8ms gap between runtimes exists even with zero shared prefix, meaning it's fixed per-request overhead, not caching skill.
2. *Burst concurrency test*: as concurrency rises, vLLM's TTFT blows up faster than TensorRT-LLM's (2.4× worse at concurrency 32) — but cache hit rate stays ~equal (~83-85%) across this whole range, ruling out "the cache broke" as the explanation. Instead, vLLM's own queueing-delay metric shows scheduling wait time growing sharply — pointing to the scheduler, not the cache, as the culprit.
3. *Fixed-rate (steady) arrival test*: under steady load, the ranking flips — vLLM now dominates TensorRT-LLM by up to 9.2× on tail (p95) latency, because TensorRT-LLM's distribution becomes bimodal (some requests hit a slow ~200ms "plateau", suggestive of scheduler batching behavior).
4. *Block-size ablation*: TensorRT-LLM's documented tuning knob (`tokens_per_block`, trading cache-granularity vs. kernel efficiency) shows no measurable effect (<7ms difference) on H100's PyTorch backend — a stated "null result," meaning old tuning advice may be stale for this hardware/backend.
5. *Cache pressure test*: since 94GB is large relative to a 7B model, the cache essentially never fills up naturally at 7B, so the authors artificially induce pressure two ways — using a bigger model (32B, which leaves less HBM for KV cache) and by directly capping the KV cache size on the 7B model. Both approaches independently reproduce a real eviction regime where hit rate collapses (e.g., to 3.6%) and TTFT regresses several-fold, but crucially the *two runtimes degrade equally* under pressure — reinforcing that cache mechanism parity holds even under stress; only the scheduler-driven flips differ.
6. *Cross-architecture/cross-model checks*: re-running the burst experiment on Mistral-7B and on a 32B model confirms the TTFT-leadership flip and cache-parity findings aren't artifacts of one specific model.
7. *Energy measurement*: sampling GPU power draw shows prefix reuse also cuts energy per request 24-31%, since avoided prefill both shortens wall-clock time and lowers average power draw.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new/useful**: A carefully controlled, reproducible, black-box comparison isolating *where* in the serving stack (cache vs. scheduler) cross-runtime performance differences originate. The "leadership flip" result — that neither runtime is unconditionally faster, and the winner depends entirely on arrival pattern (burst vs. steady) — is a concrete, actionable, and non-obvious finding for practitioners choosing/tuning a serving stack. The operating-map figure (prefix length × concurrency) showing this flip as a smooth surface (not a single threshold) is a novel visualization of this effect.
- **Also new**: The demonstration that TensorRT-LLM's documented `tokens_per_block` tuning tradeoff is not detectable in its current PyTorch backend on H100 — this is a "tuning-knob obsolescence" finding of direct practical value (stop wasting tuning effort here).
- **Distinguishing claims from evidence**: The claim that scheduling (not caching) explains the burst-mode flip is *directly measured* for vLLM (via its own queueing-delay counter) but for TensorRT-LLM it's an *inference* from elimination (cache hit rate doesn't drop) plus CDF-shape evidence — the authors themselves flag this as "the best-supported hypothesis, not a fully instrumented causal claim." This is an important asterisk on the paper's central mechanistic story.
- The claim that cache-effectiveness parity holds "everywhere, including under eviction" is well-supported by multiple independent pressure-inducing experiments (model-scale change and direct KV-cap change) that agree closely, which is a stronger and more convincing form of validation than a single test.
- Energy savings (24-31%) are a secondary, less central contribution and rest on a smaller, less varied experimental setup (single prefix length/concurrency cell).

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Fixed-rate ≠ Poisson arrivals** (stated): the "steady load" experiments use deterministic, evenly-spaced arrivals rather than realistic Poisson traffic with burstiness/clustering; authors explicitly note this likely understates real-world tail latency and makes their burst-vs-steady contrast wider than production traffic would show.
- **Limited model/scale coverage** (stated): only two model scales tested (7B and 32B, both BF16); no 70B-class models, no multi-GPU serving, no FP8 KV cache — all of which could shift when the cache-pressure regime kicks in.
- **Single GPU / single HBM configuration** (stated): only tested on one 94GB H100 NVL; unknown whether the pressure-onset ratio differs on smaller-HBM Hopper variants (e.g., 80GB H100 PCIe/SXM).
- **TensorRT-LLM backend scope limited** (stated): primarily evaluates the PyTorch backend; the block-size ablation is PyTorch-only, and the legacy "engine" backend is checked with only a single cell, not a full re-validation.
- **Causal mechanism for the runtime flip is not fully instrumented** (stated): the scheduling explanation for TensorRT-LLM's burst-mode advantage rests on elimination + indirect CDF evidence, since TensorRT-LLM exposed no comparable scheduler counters in the tested version — the authors explicitly call this an unproven "best-supported hypothesis."
- **Fixed transport overhead** (stated): ~2-3ms of HTTP overhead from the OpenAI-compatible transport affects absolute TTFT numbers (though authors argue it doesn't affect relative cross-runtime comparison).
- **Single representative Nsight trace only** (stated): deeper profiling traces are deferred to future work, so the scheduler-vs-cache story lacks fine-grained kernel-level confirmation.
- **Black-box treatment of runtimes** (stated/inferred): both systems are used "as shipped" at default-recommended configurations without source modification — reasonable for practitioner-facing characterization, but means findings could be sensitive to configuration choices/version pinning (vLLM 0.21.0, TensorRT-LLM 1.2.1) that may already be stale by the time readers apply this; version-specific numeric results (e.g., "5.75× at this corner") may not generalize to future releases (inferred).
- **Reported cross-runtime reproducibility variance** (stated): an independent artifact-evaluation re-run on different H100 hardware reproduced the ordering of results but with a different magnitude (smaller ratio) for the bimodal TensorRT-LLM tail-latency effect — the authors caveat that "the multiplier is specific to this hardware and configuration," suggesting the paper's precise numeric magnitudes should be treated cautiously even though qualitative conclusions are more robust.
- **No new caching algorithm / narrow scope claim** (stated): the paper explicitly says it is characterization, not system building, so it offers no solution to the problems it identifies (e.g., the scheduler-driven latency flip), only diagnosis and tuning guidance.
- **Energy experiment is narrow** (inferred): the energy-savings result (24-31%) is derived from just one workload cell (8192-token prefix, concurrency 1, 40 requests) rather than swept across the paper's main axes, so its generality to other concurrency/arrival regimes is unverified.
