# GrowMTP: Can RL Grow Its Own Draft Head?

**Authors:** Minghua He, Lingzhe Zhang, Yuan Liu, Xiao Zhou, Aiwei Liu

**arXiv:** [2609.16648](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16648)

**Categories:** cs.LG, cs.CL

**Submitted:** 2026-09-15

**Relevance:** 10/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
RL post-training for LLMs spends most of its wall-clock on autoregressive rollout generation, and speculative decoding (a "draft head" that proposes tokens for the big model to verify) is the standard fix — but existing draft heads require expensive offline pretraining before RL even starts. GrowMTP shows that RL rollouts themselves supply everything needed to train a draft head from scratch, online, inside the RL loop, using the verification signals the RL process already produces. On models with no draft head, a weak head, and a strong pretrained head, GrowMTP delivers rollout speedups of 2.13x/1.93x/1.36x and end-to-end speedups of 1.60x/1.41x/1.20x, at lower total compute than offline-trained heads.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** RL fine-tuning of LLMs (e.g., GRPO-style) alternates between "rollout" (the model generates responses token-by-token, autoregressively) and "update" (turning rewards into a policy gradient step). Because autoregressive decoding emits one token per forward pass, rollout dominates wall-clock time (70-90% of each step).

**Why speculative decoding helps, and why it's usually unavailable here.** Speculative decoding breaks the one-token-per-pass bottleneck: a cheap "draft" model or head proposes K tokens, the big "target" model checks all K in a single parallel forward pass, and a rejection-sampling rule decides which draft tokens to keep — mathematically guaranteed to reproduce the same output distribution as plain autoregressive decoding. The catch: draft heads (lightweight modules that reuse the backbone's hidden states, e.g., EAGLE-style or native multi-token-prediction/MTP heads) are normally trained beforehand — either via a large offline distillation stage (~5,000 GPU-hours for a 70B model) or baked in during pretraining (trillions of tokens). If your base model never shipped a draft head, you have no cheap way to add speculative decoding to RL without paying this large upfront cost — a cost incurred before you get any RL speedup benefit.

**The key observation.** The authors note that RL rollouts have two properties that make on-the-fly draft-head training feasible where general-purpose pretraining wouldn't be: (1) RL rollouts are narrow — the policy repeatedly samples from one task distribution (e.g., math problems), so a head doesn't need to generalize broadly, just fit this distribution; (2) every verification step in speculative decoding already computes the target model's probability distribution and accept/reject decision at each draft position — this is free supervision that's normally thrown away.

**The mechanism (GrowMTP).** Three engineering problems had to be solved to turn this observation into a working online-training method:

1. *State consistency ("draft-path reconstruction")*: The draft head, when drafting position k in a cycle, conditions on its own earlier drafts (not on the ground-truth tokens). A naive approach ("teacher forcing") would instead train the head on the final, committed response — which only matches what the head actually saw at inference time up to the first rejected token; after that point, teacher forcing trains the head on hypothetical states it will never encounter at inference. GrowMTP fixes this by recording the actual draft trajectory during rollout and replaying it exactly during the update phase, so training states match inference states bit-for-bit.

2. *Depth-coupled acceptance (DCA) loss*: A draft token at depth k only "counts" (helps end-to-end speed) if all k-1 tokens before it were accepted — depths are chained, not independent. The authors build a differentiable surrogate for expected accepted length: chain together the per-position acceptance probability (1 minus the total-variation distance between the head's proposal distribution and the target's verified distribution) across depths, then take a negative log-transform of this chain-product ("DCA loss"). The log transform reweights training so that cycles where the head is currently doing poorly (small chain product) get proportionally larger gradients — the paper shows analytically that this preserves the same per-cycle gradient direction as a prior TV-chain loss but changes the *relative weighting across cycles in a batch*.

3. *Verify-gated masking (VGM)*: Once a draft is rejected at position j, everything drafted at positions >j never actually happened (the sequence continues from a different, resampled token) — so training on those positions teaches the head on discarded, wrong-context data. VGM simply truncates the loss's depth-chain sum at the first rejected position per cycle, so only genuinely-realized training signal is used.

Throughout, gradients into the draft head are detached from the backbone, so head training doesn't interfere with the actual policy optimization objective. Because the first draft position always yields valid signal (no chain dependency issue at depth 1), training can literally start from a randomly initialized head with no warm-up schedule.

**Why this can start from scratch and still pay off.** Early in training a random head accepts almost nothing, so speculative overhead (drafting cost without acceptance payoff) actually slows rollout slightly. But because the head trains for free on signal RL was generating anyway, this overhead is small and short-lived — the paper reports per-step time falling below the no-draft-head baseline within ~30 steps, after which every subsequent step is faster.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new framing**: draft-head *capability* doesn't need to be general-purpose or pretrained — it only needs to match the narrow rollout distribution of a specific RL run, and RL already generates the exact supervision (target distributions + accept/reject decisions) needed to train it, at zero marginal cost. This reframes speculative decoding for RL as a "grow it as you go" component rather than a prerequisite.
- **Draft-path reconstruction vs. teacher forcing** is shown (via both a formal argument in Appendix G and an ablation, Table 5) to matter increasingly with drafting depth K — teacher forcing trains the head on states it will never see at inference for k>1 positions after any rejection, and the gap between the two training states grows with depth even though aggregate acceptance-length metrics look similar (differ by only 0.03), because the deepest positions contribute least to the aggregate metric.
- **DCA + VGM combination is empirically validated component-by-component**: joint (non-detached) training reaches highest acceptance length but produces policies scoring *zero* on held-out benchmarks — the head training can actively degrade the policy if gradients aren't detached, a real and concrete failure mode, not just a footnote. Detached DCA beats detached CE and KL objectives, with the gap widening at greater depth (consistent with DCA's rationale, since CE/KL ignore the chain dependency across depths). VGM independently helps.
- **Compute is a real (not just latency) win**: matched to an offline-trained head reaching the same eventual acceptance length (2.91 vs 2.92), GrowMTP uses 502.8 GPU-hours vs 555.3 for offline+RL combined — a 9.4% total-compute reduction, because the offline route must first generate its own training rollouts before any acceleration exists, while GrowMTP reuses rollouts RL needs anyway.
- **Drafting depth doesn't monotonically help end-to-end**: acceptance length keeps climbing with K (up to 7 tested), but end-to-end step speedup peaks at K=5 and falls at K=7, because deeper drafting/verification costs grow while marginal acceptance gains shrink — a useful practical tuning note, not a free lunch from "just add more depth."
- Claimed vs. supported: the paper claims GrowMTP is broadly "modular" and works across models with no head, weak head, and strong head — this is supported by experiments on three specific models (Qwen3-4B, MiMo-7B-SFT, Qwen3.5-4B-Base) in two domains (math, code), not a general guarantee across architectures/scales.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Distribution specialization (stated)**: the grown head is tailored to the RL run's specific rollout distribution and shows lower acceptance when applied out-of-domain; the authors explicitly say broad/general deployment of the grown head is a "distinct setting" not covered by this work.
- **Evaluation scope is narrow (stated)**: all experiments use 4B–7B models on math and code reasoning tasks, on a single node of 8×H800 GPUs. The authors state larger models, multi-node systems, and asynchronous RL setups "may have different compute and communication costs" and require direct (unmeasured) verification.
- **Scaling projections are assumptions, not measurements (stated)**: longer-rollout/more-step projections (referenced as "Appendix D.2") are explicitly called projections "under assumptions, rather than measured performance."
- **Net savings depend on run length (stated)**: the method incurs an initial overhead phase (random head performs worse than no-head baseline briefly) before turning net-positive; the authors argue typical RL runs (hundreds–thousands of steps) provide "ample time to amortize" this, but this is an assumption applied to their own 500-step (or 200-step for other experiments) runs, not verified at the scale of production frontier-model RL training (which can run far longer or shorter, with different step costs).
- **One-step policy lag is bounded but not eliminated (stated, via Appendix H)**: because the head trains on the previous RL step's verification signals but drafts for the next step's (slightly updated) policy, there's a theoretical bound on how much this staleness can hurt acceptance, tied to the per-update policy shift (Fisher-metric argument). The paper reports a proxy metric (fixed-reference KL <0.007 nats) as "contextual evidence" but explicitly notes this does not directly estimate the actual consecutive-policy shift term in their bound — i.e., their own empirical check doesn't fully verify their own theoretical claim.
- **Held-out quality comparisons are described as "comparable" without strict statistical testing (inferred)**: Mean@16/Pass@4 scores across methods are reported as similar, but the paper doesn't report variance/confidence intervals or significance tests, so "comparable" is a qualitative judgment from the authors rather than a statistically established equivalence.
- **Single seed / limited runs (inferred)**: training data order is fixed via a single seed (seed 1) across comparisons, and the paper doesn't report multi-seed variance for the main results (rollout/step speedups, acceptance length) — so the precision of the reported point-estimate speedups (e.g., 2.13x) is unclear.
- **Optimizer-dependent theoretical claims (inferred)**: the paper's argument that the DCA log-transform doesn't affect single-cycle gradient direction is proven for SGD-like reasoning but the Adam-optimizer discussion is qualified with "ignoring Adam's stabilizing constant," a simplification the authors themselves flag, meaning the practical training dynamics under the Adam optimizer they actually use are not fully covered by the theoretical analysis.
- **Baseline for "offline-trained head" comparison (inferred)**: the compute comparison against an offline-trained head (Table 2) uses a head trained to match GrowMTP's final acceptance level from the same initial checkpoint/dataset — a reasonable apples-to-apples construction, but it is a baseline built by the authors for this comparison rather than a widely-used pre-existing offline-trained head, so it may not reflect the efficiency of real production offline pretraining pipelines (e.g., DeepSeek-V3's native MTP trained across 14.8T pretraining tokens, mentioned but not directly compared against for compute cost).
