# Watermarkable Multi-Draft Speculative Sampling via Poisson Processes

**Authors:** Yanxiao Liu, Sicheng Wan, Zhan Gao, Deniz Gündüz

**arXiv:** [2609.21858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21858)

**Categories:** cs.CR, cs.LG

**Submitted:** 2026-09-18

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
This paper designs a multi-draft speculative sampling algorithm for LLM decoding built on Poisson process theory (a "multi-sample Poisson Functional Representation") that is simultaneously fast and natively watermarkable, sidestepping the previously proven trade-off between the two. It claims to be the first drafter-invariant, multi-draft scheme that preserves both watermark strength and sampling speed, backed by theoretical acceptance-rate bounds and experiments on Qwen/Llama models over CNN/DailyMail and ELI5.

## First-principles breakdown

**The two problems being combined.** LLMs generate text one token at a time, which is slow because each token requires a full forward pass through a large model. *Speculative sampling* speeds this up: a small, cheap "draft" model proposes several future tokens, and the large "target" model checks them all in one parallel pass, accepting as many as match what the target model would have generated anyway. Because the acceptance rule is designed via a matching/coupling procedure, the final output distribution is mathematically identical to what plain (slow) target-only sampling would have produced — you get speed for free, with no quality loss.

Separately, *watermarking* embeds a hidden, statistically detectable signal into generated text (without visibly changing it) so that later someone with a secret key can test "was this text produced by my model?" Standard *unbiased* watermarking schemes (e.g., the "Gumbel watermark") replace the model's internal randomness with a *pseudorandom* function of a secret key and the preceding tokens, so the output text still looks like it came from the same distribution, but the choices are secretly correlated with the key.

**Why combining them is hard.** Speculative sampling's efficiency depends on how *loosely coupled* draft and target token choices can be (any coupling scheme that reduces communication/dependence between the two sides tends to raise the acceptance rate). Watermarking, on the other hand, depends on tokens being *tightly, reproducibly* tied to secret pseudorandomness so a detector can recover the signal. Prior work (Hu & Huang, 2024) proved a "no-go" theorem: for a fixed drafting setup, you provably cannot have both maximal speculative efficiency and maximal watermark strength — pushing on one degrades the other, and existing schemes (MWS/MSE) sit at opposite endpoints of that trade-off frontier. A follow-up (He et al., 2026) shifted the frontier a bit by changing how watermark strength is measured, but didn't remove the trade-off.

**This paper's mechanism.** Instead of trying to move along that trade-off curve, they add a new axis: the number of drafts. Their core tool is the **Poisson Functional Representation (PFR)**: to sample from a target distribution P given a "cheaper" proposal distribution Q, generate points via a rate-1 Poisson process, with each point independently marked by a draw from Q and assigned an inflated arrival "time" scaled by 1/(dP/dQ) (i.e., how likely that point is under the target relative to the proposal). Picking the point with the smallest adjusted time yields an *exact* sample from P. For discrete vocabularies this reduces to a simple "exponential race": draw one Exp(1) noise variable per token, divide by that token's probability, and the smallest score wins (closely related to the Gumbel-max trick used in the earlier Aaronson watermark).

The key extension is **multi-sample PFR (MPFR)**: instead of taking just the single smallest-score point, take the B smallest-score points. By the "mapping theorem" for Poisson processes, these B points are provably i.i.d. samples from the target distribution — this gives an *exact*, multi-draft generalization with no distortion. Crucially, everything is *keyed*: the Poisson process itself is generated deterministically from a secret key and the current context (`G(k, context)`), so the same "race" that determines which draft tokens get accepted is also the mechanism that embeds the watermark — watermarking isn't bolted on afterward, it's baked into the coupling itself. This is why embedding the watermark costs (in their experiments) essentially nothing in acceptance rate: PFR and PFR-NOWM (no watermark) have nearly identical speed.

Another structural point is **drafter invariance**: in ordinary speculative sampling, swapping the draft model can change the actual tokens generated even under a fixed random seed, because the coupling procedure directly depends on the draft distribution. This is undesirable both for reproducibility and for watermarking (since the detector doesn't know which draft model was used, if watermark strength depends on drafter identity, detection becomes unreliable). The paper defines a new, intermediate notion — "stopping-time drafter invariance" — that's weaker than a prior "strong" notion (which hurts efficiency) but stronger than a competing "conditional" notion (used by Rowan et al. 2025, which lets output tokens secretly depend on the realized draft sequence). Their algorithm indexes the Poisson process by *context* rather than by *draft identity*, so once the block length is fixed, the emitted tokens depend only on target-side keyed randomness — not on which specific drafts were tried — making the watermark's statistical signal stable even if the drafter changes.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new mechanism, plausibly real contribution:** using a keyed Poisson process as the *coupling* mechanism itself (rather than watermark-as-post-hoc-reweighting) is a novel unification — the same randomness source drives both draft/target coupling and watermark keying, which is why the acceptance rate doesn't need to degrade to embed a signal.
- **Multi-draft dimension as a new lever on the trade-off:** the paper is careful to state its result does *not* contradict the earlier no-go theorem for a *fixed* number of drafts — it improves the frontier along the additional axis of draft count B. This is a fair, non-overclaiming framing, and the theoretical acceptance-rate lower bound (Theorem 5.1) formalizes why more drafts help.
- **Evidence supports the core efficiency/watermark-parity claim reasonably well within their tested setup:** AATPS (accepted tokens per step) is comparable to or better than the strongest baselines (INVARIANT, GLS) while watermark detectability (ANLPPT, TPR@1%FPR) stays close to the unspeculated watermark ceiling (BASIC-UWM/MWS) — a genuinely favorable Pareto position in their plots.
- **Drafter-substitution robustness is a distinguishing, testable claim:** PFR/MPFR's TPR drifts <1 point when the drafter is swapped, versus 12–22 points for MSE and 6–11 for MSE-PSEUDO. This is a meaningful practical advantage if it generalizes, since real deployments frequently update draft models.
- The watermark-score-agnosticism claim (robustness across Aaronson/Li et al./Lattimore scoring variants) is a genuine plus for comparability with prior watermarking literature, though it's more a design/engineering advantage than a new theoretical result.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Stated:** The paper explicitly says "a fundamental characterization of the trade-off among target-draft communication, watermark strength, and speculative sampling efficiency remains unclear" — i.e., they have not fully solved or theoretically closed the problem, only empirically/partially improved it.
- **Stated:** The current implementation involves "substantial coupling and watermark bookkeeping" that "slightly slows down the algorithm" — meaning the reported token-rate (TR) numbers likely understate the algorithm's ideal performance and current engineering isn't optimized.
- **Inferred — narrow model/task coverage:** Headline results center on one target/draft pair (Qwen2.5-7B/0.5B) on two summarization/QA datasets (CNN/DailyMail, ELI5), with a second pair (Llama-3.1-8B/3.2-1B) and a third (Vicuna-7B/vicuna-68m) added in appendices. No evidence is given for larger-scale target models, code-generation tasks, chat/instruction-following at scale, or non-English text — generality beyond these specific model families is untested.
- **Inferred — baselines may be self-selected/narrow:** Comparisons are against MWS/MSE/MSE-PSEUDO (same lineage as the "no-go theorem" papers) and Rowan et al.'s INVARIANT/GLS decoder; it's not clear whether other recent multi-draft or watermarking schemes outside this specific research thread were considered, so the "state of the art" comparison set is somewhat self-referential to the two papers this work positions itself against.
- **Inferred — theoretical guarantee (Theorem 5.1) is a lower bound, not a tight characterization:** the acceptance probability bound only shows "at least" some value; actual empirical gains over INVARIANT are described as "small cell-dependent deviations," i.e., the practical speed advantage over the strongest existing multi-draft baseline appears modest in some cells rather than uniformly large.
- **Inferred — quality/distortion auditing is indirect:** "distribution unbiasedness" is checked via log-perplexity (LPPL) under the target model and ROUGE-L, which are proxies, not a direct statistical test that the exact token distribution is unchanged; deviations of up to ~0.013 nats/token are called negligible but no formal significance test is reported in the main text.
- **Inferred — variance/seed sensitivity:** results are averaged over only 3 seeds and 1000 prompts per cell; some reported differences between methods (e.g., AATPS deviations) are within a similar magnitude to the reported standard deviations, so statistical significance of some comparisons is unclear from the given tables.
- **Not addressed in the paper:** wall-clock/hardware deployment cost of the "substantial coupling and watermark bookkeeping" overhead is not quantified beyond a qualitative mention; no discussion of robustness to adversarial watermark removal/paraphrasing attacks, nor of computational/memory cost of maintaining per-context Poisson sources at scale.
