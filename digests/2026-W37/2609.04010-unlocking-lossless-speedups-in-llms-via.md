# Unlocking Lossless Speedups in LLMs via Discrete Diffusion

**Authors:** Subham Sekhar Sahoo, Lingjie Chen, Khiem Pham, Jonathan Geuter, Chaitanya Dwivedi, Varad Pimpalkhute, Yash Akhauri, Alexander Moreno, Mikhail Yurochkin, Zhenting Wang, Mostafa Elhoushi, Nolan Dey, Shane Bergsma, Joel Hestness, John Thickstun, Eric Xing, Zhengzhong Liu

**arXiv:** [2609.04010](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04010)

**Categories:** cs.LG

**Submitted:** 2026-09-03

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
The paper introduces "diffusion-augmented LLMs" — standard autoregressive (AR) LLMs augmented with lightweight LoRA "diffusion weights" that draft multiple future tokens in parallel, which are then verified losslessly against the original AR model via speculative-decoding-style rejection sampling. The resulting models (Uno) match the exact output distribution of the base AR model while achieving up to 3x speedup, beating both speculative-decoding baselines (EAGLE-3, DFlash) and lossy diffusion LLMs (DiffusionGemma, Mercury 2) on throughput and quality across all tested batch sizes. Uno can be trained from scratch or bolted onto an existing open-weight AR model (Qwen3-8B) using a cheap distillation phase that needs orders of magnitude fewer tokens than pretraining.

## First-principles breakdown

**The core problem.** Autoregressive (AR) LLMs generate one token at a time because each token's prediction conditions on all previous tokens — this is what makes them high-quality language models, but it also makes inference inherently sequential and slow, especially since GPU inference is often memory-bound (moving weights/KV-cache dominates over compute). Longer reasoning traces and RL rollouts multiply this cost.

**Why prior fixes fall short:**
- *Speculative decoding* (e.g., EAGLE-3): a smaller separate "draft" model proposes several tokens quickly, and the big model verifies them in one parallel forward pass, accepting the longest correct prefix (this exactly preserves the target distribution because rejected tokens are resampled from a corrected residual distribution — a mathematical guarantee from the original speculative decoding papers). The catch: you need a well-aligned, separately-trained/maintained draft model, and gains often shrink at large batch sizes (which is where production serving actually lives, because verification competes for the same compute the draft model needs).
- *Diffusion LLMs (d-LLMs)*: models trained to denoise/refine an entire masked or noised sequence in parallel steps, natively supporting multi-token generation. But converting an AR model into a diffusion model changes its weights and thus its output distribution — this is lossy, typically degrading quality relative to the AR model it was derived from. Their speedup advantage also tends to vanish at large batch sizes.

**This paper's mechanism.** The key trick is to *stop trying to replace the AR model* and instead *decouple* two roles into two separate weight sets living in the same architecture:
1. **AR weights (θ_AR)** — trained the normal way (pretraining, SFT, RL) and frozen once training is done. These alone define the "ground truth" distribution and do the final verification.
2. **Diffusion weights (θ_Δ)** — small LoRA adapters (rank 128) attached to every AR weight matrix, trained afterward to draft several tokens at once by approximating what the AR model would have generated sequentially, but produced via a single diffusion-style denoising step over a block of tokens.

Because the draft pathway (θ_AR + θ_Δ) and the verify pathway (θ_AR alone) share the same base weights, and verification is exact speculative-decoding rejection sampling, the *output distribution is provably unchanged* — this is the "lossless" claim. It's more tightly coupled than a separate draft model (better acceptance rates) but unlike self-speculative decoding (which perturbs θ_AR itself to also do diffusion), it never touches θ_AR, so quality can't degrade.

**Training the diffusion weights ("Diffusion Distillation"):** They adapt "Discrete Consistency Distillation," collapsing a full multi-step diffusion trajectory into a *single* denoising step per block (cheap — no need to simulate/store intermediate noise states). Within a block, a KL-distillation loss (L_DCD) pushes the diffusion model's per-position clean-token distribution to match the frozen AR model's distribution over the same block; an auxiliary total-variation loss (L_TV) is added because what actually matters for speed is not KL fit per se but the length of prefix that survives rejection sampling — TV distance directly bounds the acceptance probability (via a known result from Leviathan et al. 2023). A "gated LoRA" mechanism lets one forward pass compute both the clean teacher logits (AR-only) and noisy-position student logits (AR+diffusion) simultaneously by toggling the adapter on/off per token position.

**Sampling (Ψ-Spec).** At inference, a block of B tokens is drafted: the very first token always comes straight from θ_AR (so it's automatically "accepted"), and the rest come from the diffusion pathway. Two sampler variants trade off differently: a *Linear* sampler (one candidate sequence, cheap, best for maximizing aggregate system throughput when GPUs are compute-bound at high batch size) and a *Tree* sampler (multiple branching candidates verified together via tree attention, good for low-batch/memory-bound regimes where spare compute exists). Verification is standard speculative-decoding rejection sampling — same guarantee, no separate draft model, shared KV cache.

**Why RL post-training still benefits despite frozen adapters:** Even though RL updates θ_AR while θ_Δ stays fixed (so the draft distribution could theoretically drift from the verifier), they empirically find speedup is retained (only ~6% TPF drop after extensive RL), which is presented as a surprising positive result.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new framing**: separating "quality" parameters from "speed" parameters within one shared architecture, rather than either training a wholly separate draft model (speculative decoding) or converting the whole model into a diffusion model (d-LLMs). This sidesteps the fundamental quality-speed tradeoff that both prior families face.
- **Losslessness is a real structural property**, not just an empirical claim — it follows from the same rejection-sampling argument underlying standard speculative decoding, given that θ_AR is frozen and used unmodified as verifier. The paper's comparison against I-DLM (Suppl. C.5) is a nice piece of evidence: they show a claimed-lossless competitor is actually NOT lossless in practice due to a sampler bug, while their own combined technique is — suggesting the authors did due diligence on this claim rather than taking losslessness for granted.
- **Batch-size robustness is the headline empirical result and is well-supported**: speedups persist (up to ~2x at largest batch, ~2.5x at batch-1) across the full concurrency sweep (Fig. 2, Table 18), unlike DiffusionGemma/Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion/Mercury 2/DFlash, which the paper shows (via its own reproductions and cited results) lose their speed edge or fall behind their own base AR models at large batch. This directly targets a stated blind spot in prior evaluation practice (most work reports only batch-size-1 latency).
- **Cheap to add to existing models**: diffusion weights are LoRA adapters trained on ~7-15B tokens vs. tens of trillions for AR pretraining, and can be grafted onto an off-the-shelf open-weight model (Qwen3-8B) even when trained on a *different* data distribution (OpenThoughts) than the base model's original training data — evidence that the coupling mechanism (gated LoRA + shared base weights) is robust to distribution mismatch.
- **Claimed vs. supported**: the claim that Uno's 8B model "outperforms" the 26B DiffusionGemma and Mercury 2 is really a quality comparison against genuinely lossy competitors — this isn't surprising given Uno pays zero quality cost by construction (it literally returns the base AR model's samples); the real technical achievement being tested is throughput, and there the evidence (Table 1, Table 2, Fig. 2) is reasonably thorough across many benchmarks and batch sizes.
- **Ablations (Suppl. C.6) are reasonably granular** — loss term weighting, LoRA rank/scale, block-size curriculum, which projections get adapters — and show sensible, monotonic-ish trends supporting design choices, lending some credibility beyond cherry-picked headline numbers.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Explicit RL detail is deferred** (stated): "We will provide detailed results in the next revision" for the RL rollout speedup section — the 40% RL speedup claim is currently under-documented, and the reader can't verify it yet.
- **Inference-time scaling via extra denoising steps is left unexplored** (stated): the paper explicitly says "We leave a systematic exploration of this quality-compute tradeoff to future work" — an entire proposed axis of the method (T > B denoising steps potentially exceeding AR quality) is not evaluated at all.
- **Quadratic/single-pass sampler improvements are left to future work** (stated): the two-forward-pass-per-step design (draft + verify) is acknowledged as a place where efficient kernels could help, but this isn't implemented or benchmarked here.
- **Combining with multi-token-prediction architectures is left unexplored** (stated): "complementary to Uno... We leave this combination to future work."
- **Single hardware target** (inferred): all throughput numbers are reported on a single H200 GPU; no results on other accelerators (A100, TPU, consumer GPUs, multi-GPU/tensor-parallel setups) are given, so generalization of the throughput claims to other deployment environments is untested.
- **Comparison against Mercury 2 is somewhat apples-to-oranges** (stated by authors themselves): Mercury 2's parameter count and quantization are undisclosed, and it runs on different (Blackwell) hardware, and its throughput/accuracy numbers come from a third-party live tracker rather than the authors' own controlled harness — the authors flag this but still report a "4.6x higher throughput" headline number built on this uneven comparison.
- **Benchmark selection/self-reported baselines** (inferred): baseline numbers for Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion and DiffusionGemma are "computed from their open-source checkpoints" by the authors themselves rather than taken from independent third-party evaluation, which creates some risk of implementation/tuning asymmetry favoring Uno (though the authors do describe using each baseline's documented default configurations).
- **DFlash "thinking mode" choice affects fairness framing** (stated, but worth flagging): the authors note DFlash's authors recommend disabling thinking mode for larger speedups, but the authors override this because it hurts DFlash's accuracy — a defensible choice for a "fair" comparison, but it does mean they are not using the baseline's own recommended/optimal operating point.
- **No latency/quality evaluation under adversarial or out-of-distribution prompts** (inferred): all evaluations use standard benchmarks (math, code, agentic tasks) with fixed synthetic-length throughput tests (1K input/8K output); real-world prompt-length and output-length distributions, or robustness to prompts that don't match this profile, are not addressed in the paper.
- **Overhead of maintaining two weight sets** (inferred): while adapters are described as "lightweight" (LoRA), the paper doesn't report the additional serving memory/complexity cost of the gated-LoRA machinery relative to a plain AR-only deployment beyond the raw parameter count (0.35B added for Qwen3-8B) — operational/engineering costs of integrating this into production serving stacks (beyond their own Nano-vLLM/SGLang implementations) aren't discussed.
- **"Faster RL training" numbers come from an internal proprietary setup** (inferred): the from-scratch model and its 23T-token pretraining corpus are "internal quality data" — not reproducible or independently verifiable, unlike the Qwen3-8B augmentation experiments which use public data.
- **Middle-of-paper truncation**: some supplementary sections (e.g., detailed Ψ-sampler derivations, full related-work nuances) were not available in the provided text and are not addressed in this digest.
