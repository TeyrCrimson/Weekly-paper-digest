# CRISP: Cliff-awaRe Input-adaptive Sparse Prefilling with Structural-Mass-Motivated Routing

**Authors:** Huu Huy Nguyen, Chien Van Nguyen, Franck Dernoncourt, Ryan A. Rossi, Linh Ngo Van, Jieyang Chen, Thien Huu Nguyen

**arXiv:** [2609.01925](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01925)

**Categories:** cs.LG, cs.CL

**Submitted:** 2026-09-01

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
CRISP speeds up long-context LLM attention prefilling by fixing two flaws in the prior state-of-the-art (FlexPrefill): it replaces an expensive divergence-based routing signal with a cheap structural proxy that reads mass directly off already-computed attention, and it replaces coverage-based token selection with a "noise floor" threshold that avoids accumulating O(n) background noise. Across InfiniteBench, RULER, and LongBench on Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-7B, CRISP matches or beats dense attention on retrieval tasks (+28.0pp over baselines in one case) while delivering up to 5.30× attention speedup at 512k tokens.

## First-principles breakdown

**The underlying problem.** Transformer self-attention computes, for every query token, a weighted combination over every key token — an O(n²) operation in sequence length n. At long contexts (100k+ tokens), this "prefilling" pass (processing the whole prompt before generation starts) dominates inference cost. The fix explored here is *sparse attention*: only compute attention for a small, well-chosen subset of key positions per query, since most of a softmax attention distribution's probability mass concentrates on a few tokens anyway.

**Why fixed-pattern sparsity fails.** Early approaches (Sparse Transformer, BigBird, Longformer, StreamingLLM's "attention sink" idea) hard-code which positions to attend to (e.g., first tokens + a local window), or profile patterns offline per model (MInference). These can't adapt when the *actual* important positions shift with the specific input — the paper shows MInference trailing badly (5-8pp behind FlexPrefill) exactly because of this rigidity.

**Dynamic routing (FlexPrefill, the baseline).** FlexPrefill inspects each attention head at runtime and classifies it as either "Vertical-Slash" (VS — mass concentrated at predictable columns/diagonals, worth targeting precisely) or "Pooled-Estimation" (PE — mass diffuse, no sharp pattern to exploit). To decide which bucket a head falls into, it computes two different approximate attention distributions — one by pooling queries/keys *then* softmaxing, one by softmaxing *then* averaging — and measures their Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD). Large divergence ⇒ VS; small ⇒ PE. Once routed, VS heads select tokens (vertical columns + diagonal "slashes") by sorting them and greedily accumulating until cumulative mass reaches a threshold γ (e.g., 0.95).

**Problem 1 the paper identifies: JSD is indirect and costly.** Computing the pooled estimate requires an extra matmul plus a second softmax, and JSD itself requires two KL-divergence computations — overhead that exists purely to make a binary routing decision. The paper's insight: concentrated (VS) heads reliably dump their mass onto structurally fixed spots — the first ~128 tokens ("attention sinks") and the most recent ~128 tokens (local recency window). So instead of computing divergence, just measure how much mass a head's proxy attention places at those fixed anchor positions — this quantity, called **C_struct**, is a simple sum over an already-computed matrix (no extra matmul, no KL divergence), and it reproduces JSD's routing decision 94.0%/88.1% of the time on the two tested model families.

**Problem 2: the "mass cliff" breaks cumulative thresholding.** Within a VS head, once sorted by mass, tokens fall into three tiers: (1) sink tokens that can absorb >90% of mass, (2) a moderate band of genuinely task-relevant tokens, (3) a long tail of near-zero-mass background "noise" tokens whose *individual* mass shrinks as O(1/n) but whose *count* grows with n. Cumulative thresholding (keep adding tokens until Σmass ≥ γ) treats all mass as equally meaningful, which produces two failure modes depending on where the threshold lands relative to the sink: (a) **sink-only collapse** — if sink mass alone already exceeds γ, selection stops before reaching any real signal; (b) **noise accumulation** — if sink+signal mass is below γ, the algorithm must scoop up O(n) near-worthless background tokens to hit the coverage target, wasting compute and hurting accuracy. Critically, raising γ doesn't fix this — it just pushes deeper into noise or does nothing (empirically confirmed: γ=0.97 barely changes accuracy but costs much more latency).

**CRISP's fix: sink-aware, noise-floor thresholding.** Instead of chasing a cumulative coverage percentage, CRISP computes an *expected background-noise level* µ (mass that would be spread uniformly across all non-sink, non-recency blocks if there were no signal), then keeps only blocks whose mass exceeds α·µ (α=1.0 by default — "above-average" mass). This directly separates signal from noise regardless of how much mass the sink absorbs, because µ automatically shrinks as sink mass grows. PE heads (diffuse, no sharp cliff) keep the old γ-cumsum approach since it isn't harmful there.

## Key insights
- **Genuinely new:** C_struct as a routing proxy — a re-framing showing that an expensive, indirect divergence computation can be replaced by directly reading structural mass off data you already computed, with no extra matmul/softmax/KL cost, and near-identical routing decisions to JSD (94.0%/88.1% agreement).
- **Genuinely new (the more novel theoretical contribution):** formalizing the "mass cliff" and proving/arguing that any strictly cumulative coverage criterion (like FlexPrefill's γ) inherently accumulates O(n) noise tokens at long context — this is a structural, not tunable, limitation. The sink-aware noise-floor threshold is the proposed remedy.
- **What the evidence actually supports:** CRISP is the best sparse method tested on the given benchmarks/models, achieves parity or better than dense attention specifically on retrieval-heavy tasks (large, credible gains on passkey/KV-retrieval/passage-retrieval), and shows a widening latency advantage over FlexPrefill as context grows (up to 5.30–5.40×), consistent with the O(n) noise-elimination claim. The ablation (Table 5) supports that both components (C_struct routing and sink-aware thresholding) are needed together — using either alone can regress on some benchmarks/models.
- **Weaker/mixed result acknowledged in the paper itself:** on RULER for Llama, CRISP slightly underperforms FlexPrefill (–0.40pp aggregate), attributed to the sink-aware threshold being overly conservative on tasks needing broad mid-range coverage (aggregation tasks) — a genuine precision/coverage tradeoff, not a clean win everywhere.
- Speedups are attention-only and most valuable only past a length "crossover" (~tens of thousands of tokens); at short contexts (8K) sparse routing overhead itself can make CRISP/FlexPrefill *slower* than dense attention.

## Caveats — stated and inferred
- **Architectural scope — stated**: C_struct and the sink-aware threshold rely on current "sink-having" architectures placing concentrated mass at fixed anchor positions; this is an empirical regularity of the two tested model families, not a theoretical guarantee, and the authors explicitly say architectures that suppress attention sinks (e.g., via gated attention) would break the method's assumptions, an untested regime.
- **Model scale/diversity — stated**: evaluated only on 7-8B models (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B), plus one extra check on Qwen3-4B in the appendix; authors state generalization to >8B models is unverified and "left to future work."
- **Binary VS/PE routing is inherited, not new — stated**: real heads can have mixed structure; forcing a hybrid head onto the VS path can cause the sink-aware threshold to discard tokens that don't fit the assumed pattern. No probabilistic/soft mixing is implemented.
- **Prefill-only — stated**: the entire method addresses the prefilling phase; extension to autoregressive decoding under causal masking is explicitly left as future work.
- **PE path is not improved — stated**: the paper's gains are concentrated on the VS path; the PE path still uses the old γ-cumsum approach and the authors admit "full improvements to the PE path... are left to future treatment."
- **RULER regression on Llama — stated**: CRISP underperforms FlexPrefill by 0.40pp aggregate on Llama RULER, attributed to over-aggressive noise filtering on aggregation-heavy tasks — a real, acknowledged accuracy tradeoff rather than a uniform win.
- **Single-input/limited qualitative evidence — inferred**: the mass-cliff visualizations (Figures 1, 5, 6) are drawn from what appears to be a small number of heads/layers on one input per model; the appendix itself notes "these panels show cross-head heterogeneity at a fixed input; we do not claim from them that a single head's cliff moves across inputs" — i.e., the illustrative evidence for the core theoretical claim is not a systematic large-sample study.
- **Baseline currency — inferred**: baselines compared are FlashAttention (dense), MInference, and FlexPrefill (the explicitly stated prior SOTA) — these appear reasonably current/relevant, but no comparison is made against other recent dynamic methods mentioned in related work (Quest, MagicPIG, Twilight, mixture-of-attention-spans), so "strongest sparse method overall" is only established relative to a subset of the dynamic-sparsity literature.
- **Latency benchmark isolation — inferred**: latency is measured as "attention-only" time on a single H100; end-to-end inference speedup (including other transformer components, batching, KV-cache effects) is not reported, so the headline 5.30× figure may overstate whole-pipeline gains.
- **Hyperparameter robustness checks are appendix-only and limited — inferred**: sensitivity analysis for α and τ_proxy is presented, and results look reasonably stable, but was only done on the same two model families already used for main results, not on independent held-out settings.
- **No discussion of memory/KV-cache overhead or accuracy-vs-compute tradeoffs at the extreme long end (beyond 512k tokens) — inferred**: all evaluations cap out at 512k tokens (latency) or 131k (accuracy benchmarks); behavior at larger scales predicted by the O(n) theory is not empirically verified beyond these limits.
