# Select, Compress, Reinvest: A Controlled Study of Visual-Token Allocation in Long-Video MLLMs

**Authors:** Prakhar Khatri

**arXiv:** [2609.03820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03820)

**Categories:** cs.CV, cs.CL

**Submitted:** 2026-09-03

**Relevance:** 9/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
This paper runs a controlled study isolating three separate decisions in long-video MLLM inference — which frames to keep, how much spatial resolution to spend per frame, and what to do with the tokens freed by compression — while holding the scorer, prompt, and answering model fixed. It finds that frame *selection* is by far the biggest lever (an old 1993 sparse-approximation algorithm, Orthogonal Matching Pursuit, matches or beats purpose-built modern selectors), spatial compression is nearly free, and reinvesting the freed token budget into more (compressed) frames recovers real accuracy gains. It also surfaces a self-caught implementation bug and a large cross-harness discrepancy, arguing that published selector comparisons are largely uninterpretable because too many pipeline components change at once.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** A long-video multimodal LLM (MLLM) can't process every decoded frame — an hour at 1 fps is 3,600 images — so systems keep only a small fixed budget (commonly 8–64 frames). Which frames survive that cut, and how much spatial resolution each frame gets, determines everything the model can ever "see" of the video. Historically this frame-picking step (the "selector") is treated as a minor preprocessing detail, and papers proposing new selectors report large end-to-end accuracy gains — but those comparisons conflate many things at once: a different frame-scoring model (e.g., CLIP variant), a different prompt given to the scorer, a different resolution policy, and even a different downstream answering model. So when Selector A beats Selector B in the literature, you can't tell whether the *selection rule itself* is better, or whether it just came bundled with a better encoder or more frames.

**The controlled setup.** The authors fix everything except one variable at a time:
- One frame scorer (LongCLIP) embeds every candidate frame plus the question "stem" (the question without answer options), computed once and cached, so every selection rule reads identical scores — no rule gets a better encoder than another.
- Three separate interventions, each a *paired* comparison (same question, two policies, everything else equal), so any accuracy difference is attributable to that one change:
  1. **Select** — vary which timestamps are chosen, holding frame count and resolution fixed.
  2. **Compress** — hold timestamps fixed, shrink each frame's pixel/token budget.
  3. **Reinvest** — spend the tokens saved by compression on additional timestamps instead of pocketing the savings.
- A fourth check swaps the scorer itself (LongCLIP → SigLIP) to see if the ranking of selectors is an artifact of one particular encoder's geometry.

**The selection rules compared.** Six training-free rules: uniform sampling (baseline), cosine top-k (pick frames most similar to the query), AKS (recursive coverage-preserving partitioning), FOCUS (bandit-style clip selection, replayed here on a substituted scorer), OMP, and LDDR-select (a dynamic-programming DPP-based method that is the only rule natively using LongCLIP).

**Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (OMP), explained.** OMP is a 1993 greedy algorithm from signal processing for sparse approximation: given a "dictionary" of candidate vectors, iteratively pick the one most correlated with what's left unexplained in a target signal (the "residual"), then subtract that vector's contribution from the residual (project it out) so the next pick targets a *different* direction, and repeat. Applied here: the "target" is the query embedding, the "dictionary" is the set of candidate frame embeddings, and each iteration picks the frame most aligned with the current residual, then removes the already-selected frames' subspace from consideration. The intent is a rule that is simultaneously relevant (correlated with the query) and non-redundant (each new pick explains something new). Crucially, the authors apply it completely unmodified and untuned — no hyperparameters fit to these benchmarks — precisely so any observed strength reflects the idea, not tuning effort.

**Why OMP behaves unexpectedly (the geometry finding).** In its original setting, OMP is meant to reconstruct a signal — the residual should shrink toward zero as more atoms are picked. Here it doesn't: the residual norm barely moves (0.972 of original after one pick, 0.967 after eight, no further drop by sixteen), because the text query sits nearly orthogonal to the dominant directions of the frame-embedding cone (a known "modality gap" in contrastive image-text encoders). So OMP is not doing sparse reconstruction in any meaningful sense — what it's actually doing is exhausting query-relevant signal in the first ~5 picks, then using its projection step to keep spreading later picks into non-redundant (novel) parts of the video, effectively trading relevance for coverage as it runs out of query signal. This explains both its strength (diverse coverage helps on long videos) and its main failure mode (later picks can land on visually distinctive but question-irrelevant content, like unrelated video chapters or title cards).

**Compression and reinvestment mechanics.** Frames are resized before reaching the model (Qwen3-VL's own internal resize disabled so only the paper's resize applies). Halving spatial resolution at fixed chosen timestamps barely changes accuracy — because the semantic content needed to answer the question was already captured by which frames were picked, not by their pixel count. The reinvestment step then takes that freed token budget and buys twice as many frames (16 instead of 8) at reduced resolution each — verified via an actual token-count audit (not just an assumed ratio) to confirm the 16-frame arm doesn't secretly cost more than the 8-frame arm.

## Key insights

- **Selection dominates, and evidence directly supports it (strongest, most tested claim):** Across three benchmarks (LongVideoBench, Video-MME, LVBench) and two answering models (Qwen3-VL-8B, InternVL3), OMP beats uniform sampling by 5.7–11.8 points, and 8 query-selected frames can beat 16 uniformly-sampled frames — half the input, better accuracy. This is validated with paired McNemar tests, not just aggregate deltas.
- **An off-the-shelf, untuned 1993 algorithm rivals purpose-built modern selectors** (AKS, FOCUS, LDDR-select) — supported by data across all three benchmarks, staying within ~1 point of the best method (LDDR-select). The authors' interpretation — that recent selectors' end-to-end gains largely come from their scorer/pipeline rather than their subset-selection logic — is a reasonable but not fully proven inference (they explicitly avoid overclaiming "subset rules barely matter," noting on Video-MME the between-rule spread is actually large).
- **Compression is nearly free but not independently useful; reinvestment is where the value is unlocked.** Halving resolution at fixed frames costs ≤0.44 points; but spending those same tokens on 2x more (compressed) frames returns +2 to +3 points at equal-or-lower measured cost. This is a genuinely useful practical recipe: prioritize selection, treat resolution as slack, reinvest savings into temporal coverage rather than sharper pixels.
- **Methodological contribution — cross-harness reliability:** The paper documents catching its own AKS implementation bug (a padding branch silently defaulted to top-k, 98.5% frame overlap despite intending a very different rule) via a pairwise selection-overlap sanity check, and shows two "matched" harnesses running the same published rule at the same budget disagree by 0.07–3.74 points. This is presented as a genuinely useful methodological warning: single-number cross-paper comparisons of selectors are unreliable, and result differences of the magnitude typically reported between selectors could just be harness noise.
- **Scorer-ordering robustness is claimed but only partially supported:** swapping LongCLIP for SigLIP changes 67–84% of selected frames yet the selector ranking (uniform < top-k < OMP) holds. However, the authors themselves note the confidence interval on this null result is wide (~[-3.6,+5.6] points), so it only rules out *large* scorer-dependence, and both encoders share the same contrastive objective — an ITM or attention-based scorer is untested.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Equivalence margins chosen post hoc (stated):** The ±2/±3-point TOST margins for the "compression is free" claim were selected after inspecting the data rather than pre-registered, so the equivalence result is descriptive, not confirmatory. Authors are explicit about this.
- **Two selector rows are not faithful reproductions (stated):** FOCUS⋆ replays only the selection schedule against a substituted scorer, not the original budgeted ITM pipeline; LDDR-select is stage-1 only, and its stage-2 resolution comparison is the authors' own reconstruction, not the original authors' code.
- **Mechanism evidence is encoder-specific (stated):** The residual-geometry / modality-gap explanation for why OMP works is based on frozen LongCLIP embeddings mostly on one duration bin; it's not established to generalize to ITM-based or attention-based scorers.
- **Failure audit is exploratory, not a rate estimate (stated):** The 93-case visual failure audit is failure-conditioned, category-balanced by construction, model-assisted, and unblinded with no inter-rater agreement measure — the authors explicitly say it cannot estimate population-level failure rates or prove a mechanism.
- **Secondary results measured on a different compute stack (stated):** Some contrasts (compression-shape, selector-by-budget interaction, variant sweep) ran on different GPUs/PyTorch versions than the main tables, with measurable baseline drift (0.36–0.71 points) between stacks; authors flag these numbers as not directly comparable to the main tables, though paired differences within each section should be unaffected.
- **Subtitles disabled throughout (stated):** This depresses all arms and makes subtitle-anchored questions partly inaccessible to any purely visual selector, potentially limiting generalizability to subtitle-reliant QA.
- **Uncorrected multiplicity (stated):** Many paired tests are run without correction for multiple comparisons across benchmarks/bins, raising the chance some "significant" findings are noise.
- **Not all published selectors are compared (stated):** MDP3 and Q-Frame appear in a comparison table the authors reference but were not implemented/run in this harness, since no validated cell existed at their exact budget/scorer configuration — this is the same condition that let the AKS bug go undetected, so the excluded rows carry the same undetectable-error risk the authors just demonstrated for AKS.
- **Selection gains are not universal across model/benchmark pairs (stated):** InternVL3-8B shows essentially no OMP benefit on medium/long Video-MME despite large gains on LVBench — the paper explicitly warns against generalizing from any single benchmark/answerer/duration cell.
- **All findings are single-run point estimates on one frozen scorer's cache per intervention (inferred):** While paired statistical tests are used, there's no indication of multiple random seeds or repeated runs to characterize variance in the underlying LLM's answering behavior itself (as opposed to the selection procedure) — not addressed in the paper.
- **Real-world selection cost is not fully accounted (stated):** The accuracy-per-token-savings framing excludes the cost of decoding video at 1 fps and encoding the entire candidate pool with LongCLIP, which the authors note would not amortize in a single-query-per-video deployment — a caveat that undercuts the practical "free lunch" framing of the selection step somewhat.
- **Small absolute differences drive some headline claims (inferred):** Several key results (e.g., 0.07-point AKS bug-fix delta, differences under 0.5 points in the compression tables) are close to the noise floor implied by the harness-disagreement analysis (0.07–3.74 points) the paper itself reports — suggesting some individual numbers should be read with caution even where paired tests reach significance.
