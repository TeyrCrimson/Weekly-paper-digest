# Random Attention: Rethinking KV Cache Eviction for Efficient Reasoning

**Authors:** Heng Wang, Jielin Qiu, Wenting Zhao, Cheng Qian, Liangwei Yang, Jiawei Han, Heng Ji, Silvio Savarese, Shelby Heinecke, Huan Wang

**arXiv:** [2609.03430](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03430)

**Categories:** cs.CL

**Submitted:** 2026-09-03

**Relevance:** 10/10 — matched: LLM inference efficiency and long-context serving

---

## TL;DR
KV cache eviction methods for reasoning LLMs all score tokens by predicted future importance and keep the top-scoring ones, but this paper shows the scoring is nearly worthless: a method that simply protects the prompt and evicts everything else uniformly at random per attention head matches the best scoring-based evictor on 4 models and 6 reasoning tasks. Because it skips the scoring pass entirely, it also delivers 32–43% higher vLLM serving throughput than the strongest baseline. The explanation: the prompt is the only fragile, irreplaceable content, while the reasoning trace is redundant (restated in text, and duplicated across attention heads), so random retention of the trace loses little.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Reasoning LLMs (DeepSeek-R1-style) generate very long chains of thought (10k+ tokens) to solve hard problems. Every generated token adds a key-value (KV) pair per layer/head to the KV cache, which is needed to attend back over context during decoding. This cache grows linearly with generation length and becomes the dominant memory cost, limiting how many requests a GPU can serve concurrently. "Eviction" caps the cache at a fixed budget K by permanently discarding some entries as generation proceeds (as opposed to "sparse attention," which keeps everything in memory but only attends to a subset — that doesn't save memory).

**How prior eviction methods work.** All of them follow the same recipe: at each eviction event, assign every cached token a real-valued score `s_i` estimating "how much will this matter later," then keep only the top-K scoring tokens (Eq. 2). They differ only in the scoring function:
- StreamingLLM: no real score — keep first few tokens ("sink") + recent window.
- H2O: score = cumulative attention received since the token entered the cache.
- SnapKV: score = attention from only the last w queries (recent window).
- R-KV: SnapKV-like score blended with a redundancy penalty (down-weight tokens whose key vector is similar to others), meant to avoid keeping restated content twice.
- VaSE: keeps tokens whose value vectors have large range, fills remaining budget by sampling proportional to SnapKV scores.
- TriAttention: scores by distance from the current query using a per-head-calibrated trigonometric function, plus key norm.

Each new paper in this lineage proposes a "better" heuristic score, implicitly assuming the score is what determines post-eviction accuracy.

**This paper's method — "Random Attention."** Two rules, no learned signal at all:
1. Never evict the prompt (positions 1..ℓ_p — system prompt, template, question) — force-keep it always.
2. For every other cached position, draw an i.i.d. uniform random number per attention head and keep the top-K by that random draw (Algorithm 1: one `rand()` + one `topk()` per eviction event, independently per KV head).

This costs almost nothing computationally (no attention-statistics bookkeeping, no extra pass over cache), versus TriAttention's ~1.5ms/round scoring cost.

**Why it works — the paper's two controlled experiments:**
1. *"The prompt is the fragile part of the cache."* The prompt is stated exactly once; if evicted, its content is unrecoverable. Prior methods differ mainly in whether their score happens to protect the prompt (some, like TriAttention, hard-code keeping it; others like SnapKV/VaSE/R-KV leave it to the score and often lose most of it). When the authors force every baseline to also protect the prompt (Table 2), most inter-method accuracy gaps collapse — SnapKV gains up to +22.5 points just from this rule, while R-KV (which already retained the prompt well) gains almost nothing. So most of what looked like "signal quality" was actually just "did you happen to keep the question."
2. *"The reasoning trace protects itself via redundancy."* Once the prompt is safe, the rest (the model's own generated chain-of-thought) is redundant at two levels: (a) textual — the model naturally restates facts/intermediate results it's still using as it reasons; (b) architectural — every attention head keeps its own independent copy of every cached token, and eviction decisions are made independently per head, so a fact survives as long as *any* head retains it. A "planted-fact" probe (inserting a synthetic fact into real traces and controlling which heads keep it) shows retrieval is highly superadditive across heads: one head alone retrieves a planted fact ~1-3% of the time, but 2 heads together get 60%, 3 heads 83%, all 8 heads 99%. Since random per-head sampling naturally spreads retained copies across different heads, it exploits exactly this redundancy without needing to know which token matters.

The only case random selection can't handle: a fact stated once, never restated, needed much later (tested via a "passcode" experiment) — there, content-aware scorers like R-KV (which accumulates attention over full history) do meaningfully better. But the authors argue reasoning traces rarely produce this pattern because models keep restating what they still need.

## Key insights

- **Genuinely new finding (well-supported):** the "selection signal" in KV eviction for reasoning contributes almost nothing beyond (a) protecting the prompt and (b) exploiting inherent trace redundancy — demonstrated via controlled ablations (Table 2, Table 6) that isolate prompt-protection from scoring, and the planted-fact/passcode probes that isolate cross-head redundancy from content-awareness. This is a genuine "null hypothesis" contribution that reframes what the whole eviction literature has been optimizing.
- **Practical contribution:** Random Attention itself is presented as a deployable default — needs no calibration/tuning, matches strongest baseline (TriAttention) accuracy in most settings, and is meaningfully faster to serve (32–43% higher vLLM throughput, since it skips any scoring pass entirely — important under paged serving where scoring requires extra passes over paged cache state).
- **Claimed vs. evidence-supported:** The claim "matches the strongest prior evictor" is well supported in aggregate (31/60 cells significantly better, 1/60 significantly worse) but is not uniformly true — TriAttention meaningfully wins on code reasoning (LiveCodeBench) at larger models (32B), attributed to long prompts consuming a large fraction of the budget before random selection even starts. The paper is transparent about this exception and traces its cause rather than hiding it.
- **Secondary insight**: what remains genuinely useful for a selection signal is narrow — recovering rare, once-stated facts (needle-in-haystack style), and budgeting/compressing long prompts (e.g., code scaffolding) rather than pinning them whole — the paper explicitly redirects future eviction research toward these narrower problems rather than better general-purpose scoring.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Code reasoning is a real weak spot** — TriAttention beats Random Attention significantly on LiveCodeBench for Qwen3-32B (and also on Qwen3-14B, per Appendix A), attributed to long prompts consuming much of the fixed budget. `stated`
- **Prompt protection is "all or nothing" and may be wasteful for long/scaffolded prompts** — the paper acknowledges pinning the entire prompt (including boilerplate I/O harness instructions in code tasks) is not optimal and a smarter rule "might compress rather than pin whole," left to future work. `stated`
- **The passcode/needle case is a known failure mode** — a fact stated once and never restated is essentially always lost by Random Attention (0% retrieval in Table 3), while content-aware scorers like R-KV recover it 84% of the time. The authors argue this is rare in real reasoning traces but do not quantify how often it occurs in the wild beyond the six benchmarked tasks. `stated`
- **TriAttention comparison in the efficiency section uses an "unfused re-implementation"** of the baseline's scorer, explicitly acknowledged by the authors as much slower than the authors' actual released kernels for that specific table (Table 10/Figure 5); they say the fair method-level comparison is the vLLM one instead. This means at least one efficiency table overstates Random Attention's relative advantage over TriAttention's true speed. `stated`
- **No comparison across some other published eviction methods** (e.g., H2O, PyramidKV, Ada-KV, LazyEviction, SpeContext, Quest-style sparse attention) — only SnapKV, R-KV, VaSE, TriAttention, StreamingLLM-style recency+prompt are benchmarked. Whether newer/other scoring signals also collapse to "prompt protection + redundancy" is not tested. `inferred`
- **All accuracy evaluation is on fixed benchmark task families** (MATH500, GPQA-Diamond, AIME, HMMT, LiveCodeBench) with specific models (Qwen3 family + Phi-4-reasoning); generalization to other model families/architectures (e.g., non-Qwen, MoE models, other reasoning training methods) is not addressed in the paper.
- **Sample sizes for competition-math tasks are small** (30 problems for AIME per year, 60 for HMMT) with reported run-to-run standard deviations of 2–5 points — some baseline "wins" over Random Attention (e.g., VaSE +1.5–1.7 pts on AIME) are explicitly noted by the authors as within noise, but this also means the overall "31/60 significant wins" headline rests partly on a mix of high- and low-power comparisons. `stated`
- **Appendix D redundancy analysis is presented only on Qwen3-4B MATH500** at specific budgets (K=1024/512) — whether the "shape doesn't matter" / cross-head-redundancy-is-non-load-bearing-at-these-budgets conclusion generalizes to other models/tasks/budgets is not fully re-verified across the whole grid. `inferred`
- **Efficiency claims depend on a specific serving setup** (vLLM v0.19.0, PagedAttention, H200 GPUs, specific prompt/generation lengths, request concurrency caps due to preemption handling limitations in the released integration) — the paper itself notes preemption isn't supported in their compression integration and required a concurrency cap to avoid crashes, which is a workaround rather than a robustly production-ready implementation. `stated`
- **AI-assisted drafting/analysis code disclosed** but authors state experiment design, hypotheses, and reported statistics were not AI-generated/selected. `stated`
