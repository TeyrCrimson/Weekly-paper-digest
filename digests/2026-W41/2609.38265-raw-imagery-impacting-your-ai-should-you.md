# Raw Imagery Impacting Your AI: Should You Care?

**Authors:** Adrien Dorise, Marjorie Bellizzi, Stéphane May

**arXiv:** [2609.38265](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38265)

**Categories:** eess.IV, cs.AI, cs.CV

**Submitted:** 2026-09-29

**Relevance:** 8/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

---

## TL;DR
This paper tests how three cheap image-quality knobs — Ground Sampling Distance (GSD), Modulation Transfer Function (MTF, a blur measure), and Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) — degrade ship-detection accuracy when a lightweight AI model runs directly on raw-ish satellite imagery instead of fully processed "ground products." Using simulated degradations on Maxar imagery and three small detectors (YOLOv5s, YOLOX-S, NanoDet), they find GSD degrades performance most consistently, while MTF and SNR effects are model- and resolution-dependent and often non-monotonic, with the worst-case being severe blur+noise combined. The takeaway is a practical one for satellite system design: moderate image degradation doesn't necessarily hurt detection much, so onboard restoration requirements could potentially be relaxed in some regimes.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** Modern satellites increasingly want to run AI (object detection, anomaly detection, etc.) *onboard*, before data ever reaches the ground. This cuts latency and downlink bandwidth. But traditional ground-processing pipelines "restore" raw sensor data into clean, calibrated images (correcting for optics, resampling, denoising) — and that restoration costs compute, memory, and power that a satellite doesn't necessarily have during a quick onboard pass. So there's a tension: skip some restoration steps to save resources, but doing so degrades the image the AI model actually sees. The open question: does the AI task (ship detection here) actually need "clean" images, or can it tolerate degraded ones? Nobody had systematically measured this at the task level — most "image quality" standards are defined for human visual interpretation, not for what a neural detector needs.

**The three degradation knobs, explained:**
- **GSD (Ground Sampling Distance):** how much ground area one pixel covers (e.g., 50cm vs 200cm/pixel). Bigger GSD = coarser resolution = less detail per object.
- **MTF (Modulation Transfer Function) at Nyquist frequency:** a standard optics/sensor metric for how well the imaging system preserves fine spatial detail (contrast) at the highest frequency the sensor can resolve. Low MTF = blurrier image, even at the same pixel resolution. Mechanically: they define a parametric 2D MTF curve (combining a sensor-sinc term and an optical exponential-decay term), derive the corresponding blur kernel (point spread function) via inverse Fourier transform, and convolve it with the image — i.e., literally simulate the physical blurring the optics+sensor would produce.
- **SNR (Signal-to-Noise Ratio):** how much random sensor/electronic noise corrupts each pixel's brightness reading. They use a realistic "signal-dependent" noise model (noise variance grows linearly with brightness — this mimics real photon/shot noise), calibrated from two reference brightness/SNR points, then inject matching Gaussian noise per pixel.

**The pipeline:** Starting from clean, high-res (50cm) Maxar reference images with ship annotations, they apply MTF blur → resample to target GSD → inject calibrated noise, producing a simulated "what the raw sensor would actually output" image. Critically, no restoration is applied afterward — this mimics an onboard model seeing unprocessed data directly.

**The experiment:** They generate many combinations ("operating points") of MTF, SNR, and GSD (100cm and 200cm), including sweeps that vary only MTF, only SNR, and both together, then train/evaluate three small, deployable-on-satellite object detectors (YOLOv5s, YOLOX-S, NanoDet — all chosen for low compute/memory footprint, since a satellite can't run a huge model) on each degraded dataset variant, measuring standard detection metrics (AP, AP50, AP75 — precision-recall-based scores at different overlap thresholds for "did it find the ship correctly").

**Why this differs from prior approaches:** Prior work mostly benchmarks AI models on already-restored, "clean" imagery, or studies image-quality metrics in isolation from any downstream task (i.e., quality judged by how good it looks to a human, not by what it does for a detector). This paper instead holds the task (detection) as the ground truth and asks how each physical degradation parameter — independently and in combination — actually moves task performance, which is a more direct way to set sensor/processing requirements for a specific mission.

## Key insights

- **Claimed and supported:** GSD is the most consistent degradation factor — going from 100cm to 200cm GSD reduces AP50 by ~4–8 points fairly uniformly across almost all configurations and all three detectors. This is well-supported by the reported matched-configuration comparisons.
- **Claimed and supported:** The relationship between degradation severity and performance loss is *not proportional/monotonic* for MTF and SNR — performance is fairly flat (a "plateau") across moderate degradation levels, then drops once degradation becomes severe, and which of MTF/SNR dominates depends on the specific detector and GSD. This is evidenced by the response-surface figures and tables, though the authors themselves flag the non-monotonicity as possibly partly due to training noise (see caveats).
- **Claimed and supported:** Combined severe MTF+SNR degradation produces the largest performance losses — worse than either degradation alone at matching severity, across all three detectors and both GSDs tested. This "interaction effect" is a genuinely useful finding: it means you cannot just budget for MTF and SNR requirements independently — joint worst-case tolerances matter more.
- **Useful practical framing rather than a strong result:** The paper's real contribution is methodological/system-engineering framing — providing a way to characterize detector performance across the sensor operating domain to directly inform sensor/AI co-design trade-offs (e.g., "is onboard restoration even necessary for this mission's accuracy requirements?") rather than a new detection method or restoration algorithm itself. No new model or algorithm is proposed here; this is an empirical sensitivity study.
- **Not actually established:** A universal threshold or rule for "how much quality is enough" — the paper explicitly disclaims this is not their goal, and the data shows the answer is architecture- and resolution-specific, not universal.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Stated:** No statistical/variability analysis — each configuration was trained and evaluated with a single run/seed, so the authors explicitly cannot distinguish genuine degradation effects from training-run noise. This directly undercuts confidence in the "non-monotonic" findings, since some reversals could simply be training variance rather than a real signal.
- **Stated:** The combined MTF+SNR sweep has only 8 paired operating points, so the authors themselves say the interpolated response surfaces should be treated as "visual aids," not rigorous measurements of the full response surface.
- **Stated:** Deliberately chose severe degradation parameters "to visualize impact clearly" — meaning the parameter grid may be skewed toward extremes rather than representative of realistic sensor operating ranges, which could exaggerate apparent effect sizes at the tails.
- **Inferred:** Single task, single dataset, single sensor type — all results concern ship/vessel detection on Maxar panchromatic+multispectral imagery pansharpened to 50cm. Generalization to wildfire detection, cloud detection, or other sensors/missions (mentioned in the abstract as motivating use cases) is not tested or addressed in the paper.
- **Inferred:** No comparison against a real onboard-restoration baseline — the paper doesn't show what performance would look like if lightweight restoration (e.g., their own prior ConvBEERS work) were applied before detection, so the core practical claim ("restoration might be skippable") is only indirectly supported; the actual cost/benefit of skipping restoration vs. doing it isn't quantified here.
- **Inferred:** Simulation-only degradation model — MTF/SNR/GSD degradations are synthetically applied to already-corrected Level-1-ish Maxar imagery rather than measured from actual raw sensor output; whether this simulation faithfully reproduces real raw-sensor artifacts (sensor nonlinearities, compression artifacts, actual onboard optics) is not addressed in the paper.
- **Inferred:** Baselines/models are standard off-the-shelf lightweight detectors (YOLOv5s, YOLOX-S, NanoDet) trained from scratch per operating point for 100 epochs — no comparison to degradation-aware or restoration-aware architectures (though the paper references their own separate work, TriCCOT, on this), so it's unclear whether the observed sensitivity is intrinsic to the task or an artifact of using generic (non-degradation-robust) architectures.
- **Inferred:** Reported AP values are generally low in absolute terms (e.g., AP50 mostly in the 0.2–0.4 range even at best quality), suggesting the detectors are not strongly tuned/high-performing to begin with, which could affect how degradation sensitivity generalizes to better-tuned production models.
