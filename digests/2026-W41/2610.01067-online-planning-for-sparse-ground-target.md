# Online Planning for Sparse Ground Target Search from a High-Altitude UAV under Partial Observability

**Authors:** Ashik E Rasul, Hyung-Jin Yoon

**arXiv:** [2610.01067](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01067)

**Categories:** cs.RO

**Submitted:** 2026-10-01

**Relevance:** 8/10 — matched: Onboard UAV object detection

---

## TL;DR
This paper treats "find a small, hard-to-see ground target from a high-flying UAV" as a POMDP, where instead of physically repositioning the drone (often illegal in urban airspace), the agent sequentially zooms/pans/tilts its camera to gather better evidence. It solves this online with POMCP, using an observation model whose reliability explicitly depends on the target's apparent pixel scale, plus a selectively-triggered multi-model ensemble detector once belief is concentrated. In CARLA simulations, this approach needs far fewer detector calls/steps and degrades much less with altitude/resolution than slicing or coarse-to-fine baselines, though at low altitude simple baselines are still competitive or better.

## First-principles breakdown

**The core problem.** A UAV cruising at high altitude is looking for a small, static object (here: a helipad) on the ground. Even though the object is technically inside the camera's field of view, it may occupy only a handful of pixels, so a standard object detector (e.g., YOLO) gets a weak, unreliable signal — low and uncalibrated confidence scores, missed detections, false alarms. This gets worse the higher the UAV flies.

**Why standard fixes fall short.**
- *Tiling/slicing (e.g., SAHI-style "Exhaustive-Crop")*: cut the image into overlapping tiles and run the detector on each tile to effectively "zoom in" digitally. This requires the original image to already be high-resolution; if it isn't, slicing just chops up a blurry signal into many blurry pieces, and you pay for many detector calls per image (the paper measures ~35 YOLO calls/episode for this baseline).
- *Coarse-to-fine*: run a cheap, low-threshold detector pass to find candidate clusters, then crop/zoom into those clusters for a second, finer pass. This helps, but still depends on the coarse pass surfacing a usable candidate region — if the target is too small even for the coarse pass's 0.05-confidence threshold, the whole pipeline fails to ever "see" the right region.
- *Ensembles (multiple independently trained models voting)*: boosts detection accuracy per image but is computationally expensive and still needs the object to be at a workable scale to begin with — ensembling doesn't fix a fundamentally under-resolved image.
- *Physically moving the UAV closer* (as in indoor active-search robotics literature) is the natural fix for insufficient information, but urban flight rules often forbid this kind of maneuvering.

The remaining lever is the camera's own **pan-tilt-zoom (PTZ)** hardware: without moving the airframe, the UAV can redirect and magnify its view onto a region of interest, trading wide-but-shallow coverage against narrow-but-reliable observations.

**Framing as a POMDP.** A POMDP (partially observable Markov decision process) is the standard formalism for decision-making when the agent can't directly see the true world state, only noisy observations of it. Formally it's a tuple ⟨S, A, O, T, Z, R, γ⟩: states (true target location), actions (here: which window of the image to scan via PTZ), observations (detector outputs, possibly a "null" detection), a transition model T (trivial here — the target doesn't move), an observation model Z (how noisy/reliable a detection is, given the true state and the chosen scan action), and rewards R. Instead of knowing the true state, the agent keeps a **belief** — a probability distribution over possible target locations — and updates it via Bayes' rule each time it acts and observes something.

Concretely, the search area is discretized into an m×m grid, and both the agent's possible scan actions and the hypothesized target states are rectangular "windows" (position + size) on that grid. A key derived quantity is the **scale ratio ρ = (scan window size)/(target's true window size)** — i.e., how much the PTZ action magnifies the target relative to its true footprint. Detector reliability is explicitly made a function of ρ.

**Modeling observation noise with Beta-Bernoulli priors.** Rather than assuming one fixed, globally accurate detector, the paper buckets ρ into discrete bins and, for each bin, maintains a Beta distribution over the probability of a true detection (θ⁺, from hits/misses) and a separate Beta distribution over the probability of a false alarm (θ⁻, from false-positives/correct-rejections). Beta-Bernoulli is the standard conjugate-prior pairing for modeling an unknown success probability from binary (hit/miss) trial data — it lets you encode prior domain knowledge (e.g., "at low zoom the detector is unreliable") and, in principle, update that belief as more real trial data comes in. The actual detection/false-alarm probability used in the POMDP's observation likelihood Z depends on whether the target truly lies inside the scanned window and on ρ.

**Solving the POMDP online with POMCP.** Exact POMDP solutions are generally intractable, and offline approximate solvers don't scale to large state/action spaces. POMCP (Partially Observable Monte Carlo Planning) instead: (1) represents the belief as a set of K sampled "particles" (candidate true states) rather than an explicit distribution, (2) uses Monte Carlo Tree Search (specifically PO-UCT, an upper-confidence-bound tree search) to simulate many rollouts of future action/observation sequences from a generative black-box model, picking actions that balance known-good value against under-explored alternatives, and (3) after acting and observing a real result, updates the belief via rejection sampling: repeatedly sample a particle, simulate what observation it *would* produce, and keep it only if that simulated observation matches what was actually observed — collecting K accepted particles as the new belief.

**This paper's specific pipeline (Algorithm 1):**
1. Start with a coarse, full-area scan to get an initial "saliency prior" belief (rather than starting from a uniform/blind belief), which seeds subsequent planning.
2. At each step, if the belief's MAP (maximum-a-posteriori) confidence already exceeds a declare threshold τ, stop and declare the target there.
3. Otherwise run POMCP to choose the next PTZ action (what region/zoom to scan next).
4. **Selective ensemble trick**: if confidence is already decently high (above a lower threshold τₑ) and the planner's chosen action is to scan right at the MAP location at full scale (ρ=1), swap the single-detector scan for a 5-model ensemble vote — i.e., only pay the expensive ensemble cost when it's likely to matter (disambiguating a probable true positive from a false positive), not on every step.
5. Update belief with the new observation; repeat until declaration or a step budget is hit.

The reward function penalizes each step taken (encouraging efficiency), rewards true-positive-generating actions, penalizes false positives, and gives a large terminal reward for correctly declaring the true target location (vs. a large penalty for wrong declaration) — all discounted to favor early, confident, correct declarations.

## Key insights

- **Reframing active sensing for UAVs as camera control, not motion planning**: the genuinely novel idea is substituting "move the robot to get a better view" (standard in indoor active object search) with "move/zoom the camera" to respect airspace restrictions — a reasonable and underexplored niche.
- **Scale-conditioned observation model via Beta-Bernoulli priors**: explicitly tying detector trustworthiness to the scan's scale ratio ρ, rather than assuming a fixed-noise or confidence-threshold detector, is the paper's main methodological contribution and lets the belief update reflect "small-object detections are less trustworthy."
- **Selective (not blanket) ensembling**: triggering the expensive 5-model ensemble only when belief is already concentrated at full scale is a practical compute-saving mechanism that the evidence does support — the method isn't dramatically more expensive in detector calls than exhaustive crop despite doing active sensing.
- **What the evidence actually shows**: the method's real advantage shows up specifically at high altitude / low effective resolution (300–350 m), where baselines collapse (negative reward due to repeated misses/false positives) while POMCP-PTZ keeps working with far fewer steps. At low altitude (100 m, within the detector's fine-tuned range), baselines are as good or better — so the claimed benefit is conditional on exactly the out-of-distribution-scale regime the paper targets, not a universal win.
- The "minimal dependence on sensor resolution" claim is evidenced by one experiment showing Exhaustive-Crop's performance recovering as resolution increases while POMCP-PTZ stays roughly flat (even slightly degrading due to train/test resolution mismatch) — a real but narrow demonstration, not a general robustness proof.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Discretized, not continuous, state/action/observation spaces** (stated): the authors note real-world states/actions/observations are continuous, and suggest POMCPOW as a future extension requiring more simulations — the current grid-based formulation is a simplification.
- **Fixed/conservative observation model, not online-adapted** (stated): despite the Beta-Bernoulli formulation nominally supporting online updates from feedback, the paper states the model used is "conservative domain knowledge" and that it "should adapt online as it receives more feedback" — implying no actual online adaptation was demonstrated, just the static prior.
- **Single-target, single-object-class scenario** (stated): the method is designed and tested for one helipad at a time; multi-target/multi-object search is left as future work (e.g., via object-oriented POMDPs or multiple agents).
- **Simulation-only validation** (stated): explicitly tested only in the CARLA/Unreal-based simulator; no real-world UAV or hardware PTZ testing was conducted, which the authors flag as needed future validation.
- **PTZ actuation cost not modeled** (stated): the paper admits physical camera actuation (pan/tilt/zoom) "can be time-consuming" and isn't incorporated into the planning cost, and suggests crop and PTZ should both be in the action space for efficiency — meaning the reported "fewer steps" advantage may not translate directly into "faster wall-clock time" if actuation latency is significant.
- **Not significantly better, or worse, at low altitude** (stated/inferred): at 100 m the paper reports baselines performing "either significantly better or as well as" POMCP-PTZ — a direct admission that the proposed method's advantage is confined to the harder, higher-altitude regime, which somewhat limits the generality of the headline claim.
- **Detector fine-tuning range chosen to favor the failure mode being tested** (inferred): the main YOLO detector is explicitly tuned for 0–100 m, then the paper evaluates "harder" 200–350 m cases precisely where that detector is expected to be out-of-distribution — this is a reasonable way to stress-test scale robustness, but it also means baseline underperformance at high altitude is partly a predictable consequence of the experimental setup rather than a surprising emergent finding.
- **Small-scale statistical evaluation** (inferred): 10 trials × 3 seeds × 3 towns per altitude/method is a fairly small sample for claims like "significantly outperforms," especially given reported variability (e.g., comparable-not-better performance to C2F-PTZ-Ensemble at 300 m in some scenarios); no confidence intervals or significance tests are reported.
- **Compute/inference-cost tradeoff is understated** (inferred): POMCP-PTZ uses substantially more YOLO calls than Fixed-Scan or C2F methods (comparable to, not much better than, the 35-call Exhaustive-Crop baseline), and each step also requires 50k POMCP simulations with ~11.5k particles — a nontrivial planning overhead not benchmarked against the baselines' near-zero planning cost.
- **No ablations reported** (inferred): no isolated study of the contributions of individual design choices (coarse-scan belief seeding, the scale-conditioned Beta-Bernoulli model vs. a naive fixed-noise model, the ensemble-trigger threshold τₑ, reward hyperparameters) — it's unclear how much each component contributes versus the overall pipeline.
- **Narrow target/environment generality** (inferred): all experiments use helipad detection in three CARLA towns; generalization to other object classes, more cluttered real scenes, or adversarial/dynamic conditions is untested.
