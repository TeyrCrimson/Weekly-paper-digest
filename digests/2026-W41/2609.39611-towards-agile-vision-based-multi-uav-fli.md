**Authors:** Michal Pliska, Matouš Vrba, Ondřej Víta, Martin Jiroušek, Viktor Walter, Martin Saska

**arXiv:** [2609.39611](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39611)

**Categories:** cs.RO

**Submitted:** 2026-09-30

**Relevance:** 6/10 — matched: Onboard object detection from a moving UAV

## TL;DR
Vision-only multi-UAV relative state estimation traditionally infers velocity/acceleration from position measurements alone, which the authors show introduces a fixed ~300ms delay regardless of target agility. They fix this by also measuring the target's tilt (thrust direction) via a 6D-pose detector and fusing it with a novel linear Kalman filter (Z-KF, derived from a general "Degenerate Kalman Filter" framework), cutting velocity/acceleration errors by 40%/57% and enabling closed-loop leader-follower tracking at >2g that fails entirely with position-only estimation.

## First-principles breakdown

**The problem.** A UAV trying to avoid or follow another UAV using only an onboard camera needs to know not just where the other UAV is, but how fast it's moving and accelerating, so it can predict where it will be. The camera (plus a detector/PnP solver) directly gives you *position* (and, if the detector is good, also *orientation*/pose). Velocity and acceleration are not directly observed — they have to be inferred.

**Why position-only inference is fundamentally slow.** Standard approaches run a Kalman filter (KF) with a kinematic model — Constant Velocity (CV) or Constant Acceleration (CA) — that treats unmodeled accelerations/jerks as random noise and updates its velocity/acceleration estimates purely from the pattern of positions over several frames. The core issue: acceleration literally only reveals itself as a *second derivative of displacement*, which takes several frames of accumulated position change to show up reliably above sensor noise. The paper shows this produces a roughly constant ~300ms lag before an acceleration change is detected — and crucially, this lag doesn't shrink even when the target maneuvers harder, because it's a structural property of needing N displacement samples, not a noise-tuning problem.

**Their fix: use the body tilt as a direct physics shortcut.** For a typical multirotor, nearly all its thrust is produced along the body's vertical axis (the propeller-plane normal). Thrust direction is what *causes* the horizontal acceleration you're trying to estimate (a = thrust/mass·b̂₃ + gravity + disturbance). If you can see the drone's orientation (e.g., it's tilted 20° one way), you immediately know, to within an unknown thrust magnitude, which direction it's accelerating — *before* that acceleration has had time to produce visible displacement. The key enabler is a pose detector (YOLOv5-6D + PnP) that recovers not just position but full 6D pose from a single RGB image, using known physical dimensions of the target drone and keypoint-based deep learning.

**Fusing a tilt measurement is awkward for a standard KF.** A position measurement says "the state is near this point" — easy to plug into a KF's linear measurement model. A tilt measurement says something weirder: "the acceleration vector lies somewhere along this specific line (direction = thrust direction), we just don't know how far along it (unknown thrust magnitude)." That's a constraint on an *affine subspace* of the state, not a point estimate. The paper's technical contribution is the **Degenerate Kalman Filter (DKF)**: a general recipe for converting "the state satisfies Mx = Wλ + o" (a subspace constraint with unknown scale λ) into the standard KF measurement form z = Hx + noise, by projecting out the unknown-scale direction using its null space. Applying this recipe to the tilt measurement yields their proposed **Z-KF**, a plain linear (not extended) Kalman filter that fuses position + tilt-direction constraints on acceleration.

**Evaluation setup.** They compare 4 position-only estimators (CV-KF, CA-KF, each with two different process-noise parameterizations) against 5 pose-aware estimators (two prior methods — Pose-KF, MEKF — plus their Z-KF, each with variants), across three datasets: two real-world (motion-capture ground truth, limited agility) and one new photorealistic simulated dataset (FlightForge/Unreal Engine) spanning accelerations from 3–21 m/s². All filters are tuned automatically and identically via CMA-ES optimization to keep comparisons fair, and evaluated with a Mean Error Norm metric on held-out sequences.

**Closed-loop test.** Beyond open-loop accuracy, they run a full pipeline (detect → estimate → predict → NMPC control) in simulation where a follower UAV must track a leader doing aggressive lateral maneuvers, to see if estimation quality actually translates into usable control.

## Key insights

- **Novel and demonstrated**: the diagnosis that position-only estimation has a *structural*, agility-independent ~300ms delay (not a tunable noise/lag tradeoff) — this is a genuinely clarifying reframing, backed by the step-response experiment (Table V) showing constant delay across 3–21 m/s² while tilt-aware estimators approach the camera frame-rate limit.
- **Novel and demonstrated**: the Z-KF/DKF formulation — a way to fuse "affine subspace" measurements (direction-known, magnitude-unknown) into a linear KF generally, not just for this specific tilt problem. The authors note it's algebraically equivalent to prior work [11]'s update law but claim the generalized derivation as a contribution — that generality claim itself isn't independently stress-tested on other applications in this paper.
- **Well-supported by evidence**: pose-aware estimation consistently beats position-only (40%/57% average velocity/acceleration error reduction) across all three datasets and all tested agility levels, with the gap widening as agility increases.
- **Well-supported, important for the application domain**: the closed-loop demonstration — position-only estimation fails to even sustain stable hovering of the follower, while Z-KF enables tracking >2g maneuvers. This is the strongest evidence that the accuracy gains actually matter operationally, not just as an offline metric.
- **Secondary finding**: Z-KF isn't uniformly best — on the two real-world datasets, the prior Pose-KF method actually achieves slightly lower velocity error (5–8%) than Z-KF+BDC, because its constant-thrust-magnitude assumption works well for mild, near-planar real-world motion; Z-KF's advantage shows up specifically under more three-dimensional, aggressive maneuvers (their simulated dataset).
- **Contribution of a new benchmark dataset** (photorealistic, 7 agility levels, exact simulator ground truth for velocity/acceleration, no need for noisy numerical differentiation) — useful infrastructure claim, though its value is inherently tied to trusting simulation fidelity.

## Caveats — stated and inferred

- **Closed-loop validation is simulation-only** (stated): "The closed-loop experiments were conducted only in simulation, with real-world validation remaining an open problem, which we plan to address in future work." This is the paper's headline result (position-only fails to hover, Z-KF tracks 2g), and it has not been tested on real hardware.
- **PnP pose extraction assumes known target dimensions** (stated): the method requires knowing the physical size of the UAV being tracked in advance; authors acknowledge this is limiting and suggest stereo cameras as a possible alternative, but don't implement/test that.
- **Real-world datasets have limited agility and are reused/relabeled from prior work** (stated/inferred): the two real datasets (Phantom 4, Mavic 2 from [10]) are static-camera, motion-capture lab recordings with much lower agility (max ~4.7 m/s² p98 acceleration) than the simulated dataset (up to ~19 m/s²) — stated in Table I. This means the headline high-agility results (21 m/s², 2g closed-loop tracking) rest entirely on the simulated dataset and simulated closed-loop test, not on real-world high-agility data (inferred: the real-world evaluation doesn't actually test the regime the paper is motivated by).
- **Higher-order ground truth for real datasets is derived, not measured** (stated): "this dataset does not provide higher-order states, which must therefore be derived retrospectively, introducing potential inaccuracies" — velocity/acceleration ground truth on the real-world benchmarks is itself approximate.
- **Baselines may be somewhat dated/narrow** (inferred): comparisons are against CV/CA-KF variants and two specific prior pose-aware papers (Pose-KF, MEKF); no comparison to, e.g., learning-based trajectory predictors or nonlinear/particle-filter approaches, and MEKF is described as "modified" from its original formulation by the authors themselves, which could affect fairness of that baseline.
- **Tuning variance for BDC variants is large** (stated): "the difference in the loss was up to 175%" between best/worst of 10 tuning runs for BDC filters, though they report using median-run results — this suggests those particular estimator variants are sensitive to optimization and results for them could shift with different tuning budgets/seeds.
- **No real-world closed-loop or even open-loop test at the very highest agility levels (15–21 m/s²)** for the real datasets — those levels only exist in simulation (inferred from Table I/III structure).
- **Pose-KF sometimes outperforms the proposed Z-KF on real data** (stated in results) — the paper is transparent about this, but it tempers the "Z-KF outperforms other estimators" claim in the abstract, which is true on average/in aggregate and on the harder simulated dataset but not uniformly on every dataset/metric.
- **Single target, single follower scenario** (inferred): all experiments involve one observed UAV and one observer; scalability to multiple simultaneously tracked neighbors (relevant to "multi-UAV" framing in the title) is not addressed in the paper.
