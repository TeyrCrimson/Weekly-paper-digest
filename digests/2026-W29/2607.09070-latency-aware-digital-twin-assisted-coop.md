# Latency-Aware Digital Twin-Assisted Cooperative Perception for Autonomous Vehicles

**Authors:** Boniface Uwizeyimana, Manobendu Sarker, Abraham O. Fapojuwo

**arXiv:** [2607.09070](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09070)

**Categories:** eess.SY

**Submitted:** 2026-07-10

**Relevance:** 7/10 — matched: Delay-compensated perception for mobile robots

## TL;DR
The paper proposes a digital-twin (DT)-assisted framework for cooperative perception in autonomous vehicles that jointly tunes each vehicle's data-compression threshold and transmit power to maximize detection accuracy (mAP) under strict end-to-end latency and communication limits. Because the accuracy function comes from a black-box trained perception model (no gradients available), they solve the resulting non-convex problem with a two-stage "coarse-to-fine search" (CTFS) heuristic instead of exhaustive search. Simulations (2 vehicles, 1 RSU, LiDAR data from the UrbanIng-V2X dataset) show CTFS reaches 96.6% of exhaustive-search accuracy at ~86% lower compute, and periodic (rather than continuous) DT state synchronization cuts communication overhead by 50%.

## First-principles breakdown

**The setting.** Autonomous vehicles have limited onboard sensing (occlusions, range limits). "Cooperative perception" fixes this by having nearby vehicles send their sensor-derived features to a shared aggregator — here, an edge server at a roadside unit (RSU) — which fuses everything into one better detection output and sends results back. This is called "intermediate fusion": each vehicle runs a neural network (PointPillars, converting raw LiDAR point clouds into a bird's-eye-view "feature map" image) and transmits that compressed feature map rather than raw sensor data (too much bandwidth) or just final detections (too little information, "late fusion").

**Why this is hard.** How much of the feature map you send is controlled by a "communication confidence threshold" θ (from a prior method called Where2comm): higher θ means you transmit a smaller, more compressed slice of the feature map, saving time/bandwidth but hurting accuracy. Picking θ (and transmit power) well requires balancing accuracy against latency, but latency itself depends on time-varying wireless channel quality, vehicle mobility, and RSU compute load. Prior work (Where2comm, and its evaluation on UrbanIng-V2X) picks θ reactively, based on the current instant's conditions — it can't anticipate that channel conditions are about to worsen and blow the latency budget.

**The digital twin idea.** A DT is a continuously-updated virtual copy of the physical vehicles/RSUs (positions, velocity, compute capacity, channel state, etc.), hosted at the RSU edge server. Instead of vehicles reporting fresh state every single time slot, the DT is refreshed only every π slots and fills the gaps by extrapolating (using stored trajectory history / motion patterns) the likely current state — position, channel gain, achievable data rate — of each vehicle. This lets the RSU make *proactive* rather than *purely reactive* scheduling decisions, and it also reduces how much raw state/metadata needs to be transmitted (only every π slots instead of every slot).

**The optimization problem (P1).** For each vehicle m, decide the compression threshold θ_m and transmit power p_m to maximize (predicted mAP − a penalty on power used), subject to: (a) each vehicle's total pipeline latency (feature extraction + uplink transmission + waiting for the slowest vehicle + fusion at RSU + fixed downlink delay) stays under its deadline, (b) θ_m∈(0,1], (c) p_m within hardware limits. Because mAP is only known by actually running the trained perception model (no closed-form/derivative), this can't be solved with gradient descent — it's a non-convex black-box optimization over a discrete/continuous mixed space.

**CTFS algorithm.** Two stages:
1. *Coarse search*: discretize each vehicle's θ into q evenly spaced values, giving q^M combinations. For each combination, compute the *minimum* transmit power needed to just satisfy the latency budget via a closed-form inversion of the delay equation (derived in the Appendix). If that required power exceeds the vehicle's max power, discard the candidate (infeasible) — this avoids wasting an expensive mAP model evaluation on candidates that can't work anyway. For surviving candidates, evaluate the real objective (which requires running the perception model) and keep the top-K.
2. *Fine-tuning*: re-discretize θ, but only within the narrower region spanned by those top-K candidates, using a finer resolution, and repeat the same feasibility-check-then-evaluate procedure to pick the final θ*, p*.

This mimics exhaustive grid search's quality near the optimum while sharply cutting the number of expensive perception-model evaluations, since most of the search effort goes into cheap feasibility filtering.

## Key insights
- **Claimed and reasonably supported**: CTFS gets close to exhaustive-search accuracy (96.6% under a 0.3s latency budget) while cutting computation by ~86% (94.95×10¹² vs 13.50×10¹² operations at their tested q values) — this is a plausible, evaluated efficiency/accuracy trade-off, since it's a direct simulation comparison (CTFS vs. exhaustive vs. random search).
- **Claimed and reasonably supported**: DT-based periodic (rather than per-slot) state synchronization cuts non-DT communication load by 1 − 1/π; the paper reports 50% for π=2, but this is essentially an algebraic consequence of the sync scheme, not an empirically discovered result — it follows directly from the formula.
- **Genuinely novel framing claim**: jointly optimizing communication threshold *and* transmit power under an explicit E2E latency constraint, using DT-predicted (rather than purely instantaneous) channel/state information, distinguishes this from prior work (Where2comm, UrbanIng-V2X evaluation) that only handles communication-aware feature selection without latency-aware proactive control. This is the paper's main conceptual contribution; the evidence for its practical benefit over the reactive baseline is thinner than the CTFS-vs-exhaustive-search comparison (see caveats).
- The feasibility-first filtering trick (compute minimum required power analytically, discard infeasible candidates before running the costly perception model) is the real algorithmic lever behind CTFS's compute savings — a reusable idea for any black-box-objective optimization under a hard constraint with a closed-form feasibility check.

## Caveats — stated and inferred
- **stated**: DT maintenance overhead (sync traffic, RSU compute cost of running the estimator/CTFS, storage for persistent states) is not quantified and is explicitly left for future work — so the "50% communication savings" ignores the DT's own overhead.
- **stated**: Downlink delay is fixed at a conservative constant (0.01s) rather than modeled dynamically.
- **stated**: The synchronization interval π is not optimized — the 50% figure uses an example value (π=2) without a mobility/channel-coherence-aware tuning method, which is left for future work.
- **stated**: Future physical-network state is obtained via simple estimation/extrapolation, not a learning-based predictor; the authors note learning-based prediction is left for future work.
- **inferred**: Simulation uses a "best-case" free-space path-loss exponent (γ=2.0), explicitly noted by the authors as a best-case link benchmark — real urban NLOS conditions would likely degrade both latency and DT state-prediction accuracy.
- **inferred**: Evaluation scale is very small — only M=2 vehicles and N=1 RSU — despite CTFS's complexity scaling as O(q^M), so the claimed 85.78% compute reduction and near-exhaustive accuracy are not demonstrated at larger, more realistic fleet sizes where the coarse-to-fine gap could behave differently.
- **inferred**: Baselines are limited to exhaustive search and random search; there's no comparison to other adaptive/learning-based scheduling or threshold-selection methods from the cooperative-perception literature, so CTFS's advantage relative to more competitive prior methods (beyond a brute-force upper bound and a random lower bound) is unestablished.
- **inferred**: All perception results are reported at a single IoU threshold (0.3); no sensitivity analysis at stricter thresholds (e.g., 0.5, 0.7) commonly used in detection benchmarks, so robustness of the accuracy numbers is unclear.
- **inferred**: The headline 96.6% figure only holds under the looser 0.3s latency budget; under the tighter 0.1s budget the paper itself states mAP "drops significantly" and all methods converge to similarly low performance — the flagship number is a best-case condition rather than representative across the tested regime.
- **inferred**: The framework reuses the perception model, dataset, and feature-extraction/fusion architecture entirely from prior work (Where2comm, UrbanIng-V2X) — the contribution is purely the scheduling/optimization layer around an existing model, evaluated only in simulation with no real hardware, real V2X radio, or real-world deployment test.
- **inferred**: No ablation isolates how much of the reported accuracy/latency improvement comes from the DT's predictive state estimation specifically (versus simply running CTFS on instantaneous, non-predicted state) — the accuracy experiments (Fig. 2/3) compare CTFS vs. exhaustive/random search on the optimization method itself, not DT-predictive vs. non-predictive information.
